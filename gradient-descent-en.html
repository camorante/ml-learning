Before we start

Something you already know how to do

Imagine you're at the top of a mountain and a fog rolls in so thick you can't see anything. You need to get down to the valley. What do you do?

You feel the ground with your feet, notice which way it slopes down the most, take a step that way, and feel again. Step by step, without seeing the map, you end up at the bottom.

In machine learning, the "mountain" is the model's error, and your position is its knobs (for example, the slope and the starting point of a line). Walking down the mountain means adjusting the knobs so the error is as small as possible. That is what training a model means.

Idea 1 of 5

The slope tells you where to go

At each point, the machine works out which way the ground goes up (that's the gradient) and takes a step in the opposite direction. If the ground slopes down to the right, it goes right. If it's almost flat, it takes a tiny step: it's close to the bottom.

Idea 2 of 5

The step size changes everything

How far you move on each step is called the learning rate. It's the most important knob of the method, and you have to pick it carefully:

If the step is too big, you jump over the valley and land on the opposite slope, higher than before. Every jump makes it worse and training "blows up". Try the biggest stride in the chart at the top to see it.

Idea 3 of 5

Long valleys: the zigzag

With two or more knobs, the terrain can be a long, narrow valley, like a canyon. The slope points mostly toward the walls, not toward the way out of the canyon, and the ball bounces from wall to wall while barely moving forward.

This happens when the knobs are on very different scales (for example, one in kilograms and another in tonnes). The simplest fix is to put the data on the same scale before training: the valley becomes round and the way down is direct.

A very common trick is momentum: the ball remembers where it was heading. The bounces off the walls cancel each other out, and the speed along the canyon builds up. Turn it on in the chart at the top with the long valley.

Idea 4 of 5

False holes: where you start matters

Some mountains have several holes. The ball rolls into the nearest hole, and from there everything is uphill: it gets stuck even if there's a deeper valley somewhere else. These holes are called local minima.

The good news: in the linear and logistic regression from earlier topics, the terrain has a single hole, so you always reach the best one. Neural networks have a huge number of holes, but in practice most of them are almost equally good.

Idea 5 of 5

With millions of data points: steps in a hurry

To know the exact slope you have to look at all the data. With millions of points, each step would take forever. The trick is like a poll: you ask just a few (a batch of data points picked at random) and estimate the slope from them.

Each step is less precise and the path wobbles, but the steps are so cheap that you get there sooner. This is called stochastic gradient descent (SGD), and it's how almost all large models are trained.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Define the error you want to make small (for example, the squared errors of regression).
  2. Put the data on the same scale so the valley is round.
  3. Start anywhere and work out the slope (with all the data or with a batch).
  4. Take a step downhill of the chosen size.
  5. Repeat until the error stops going down. If it goes up or jumps around, the step is too big.

In the real world

Where is it used?

Neural networks and AI

From an image classifier to large language models: all of them are trained with variants of stochastic gradient descent, with billions of knobs at once.

Classic models with lots of data

The logistic regression behind a spam filter or a fraud system is trained with gradient descent when there are millions of examples.

Learning on the fly

When data keeps arriving nonstop (vehicle telemetry, clicks on a website), the model updates with a small step for each new data point, without retraining from scratch.

Recommenders

Movie or product suggestions tune millions of hidden preferences by lowering, step by step, the error between what they predict and what people actually chose.

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • Works with any model whose error can be measured and differentiated.
  • Scales to millions of data points and billions of knobs.
  • Only needs the local slope, not the whole map.
  • With batches, each step is dirt cheap.

The not so good

  • You have to pick the step size: too small is slow, too big blows up.
  • It can get stuck in a false hole or crawl across flat areas.
  • It struggles with data on very different scales.
  • You never know for sure whether you reached the best possible valley.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Loss function
The model's error: the height of the mountain at each point.
Gradient
The direction in which the error goes up fastest. You walk against it.
Learning rate
The size of each step.
Iteration
One step.
Epoch
One full pass through all the data.
Batch (mini-batch)
The small group of data points used to estimate the slope on one step.
Local minimum
A hole you can't get out of by going downhill, even if there are deeper ones.
Momentum
Remembering the direction of previous steps so you don't zigzag.
Convergence
Reaching the bottom: the error doesn't go down anymore.
For the curious: the formula behind it all

It's a single line, repeated thousands of times:

new_knobs = knobs − step_size × slope with momentum: velocity = 0.9 × velocity − step_size × slope new_knobs = knobs + velocity
