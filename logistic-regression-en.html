Before we start

Something you already know how to do

The weather app doesn't tell you "it will rain tomorrow" or "it won't rain tomorrow". It tells you "70% chance of rain". And you decide from there: if you're going to a wedding, you bring an umbrella even at 30%; if you're just going out for bread, maybe not.

Many important questions are like that, yes or no: will they pass the exam? Is this email spam? Will this truck break down this month? Logistic regression answers with a probability, and you decide at what probability you act.

Idea 1 of 5

Why a straight line doesn't work

We could use the linear regression from the previous topic: passed = 1, failed = 0, and draw the best line. But the line doesn't know it lives between 0 and 1. With few hours it predicts negative probabilities, and with many, more than 100%. That makes no sense.

The fix is to bend the line into an S shape (it's called the sigmoid). Far to the left it flattens out at 0%, far to the right at 100%, and in the middle it rises smoothly. All of logistic regression is about finding the best S.

Idea 2 of 5

The S-curve also has two knobs

  • Midpoint: how many hours you need to study to have a 50% chance of passing.
  • Steepness: how sudden the change is. Steep: "either you study 5 hours or you fail". Gentle: each extra hour helps a little.

Idea 3 of 5

"The best" means: the least surprised one

In linear regression we measured squared errors. Here we measure surprise: for each case, we look at what probability the model gave to what actually happened.

If the model said 90% and it happened, it's barely surprised. If it said 1% and it happened, it's hugely surprised. Surprise grows without limit when the model was very confident and wrong. That's exactly what we want to punish: a model that says "definitely not" to something that happens is dangerous.

The machine looks for the S-curve with the lowest total surprise. Unlike the straight line, there's no direct formula: it finds it by taking steps, each one better than the last, until it can't go any lower. With little data it takes just a few steps.

From probability to decision: the threshold

The model gives probabilities. But in the end you have to decide: do I send this truck to the maintenance shop or not? For that you pick a threshold: if the probability of a breakdown is above that value, you act.

Idea 5 of 5

With two or more inputs, the boundary is a line

In real life you use several clues at once: kilometers since the last service and engine temperature, for example. With two clues, the 50% boundary is a straight line that splits the plane in two (what you see in the chart at the very top).

And what if the "yes" cases form a circle? A straight line can't surround it. The trick is the same as in polynomial regression: give the model extra clues (the squares of the clues) and the boundary can bend.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Ask a yes-or-no question and gather past cases with their real answer.
  2. Choose the clues (kilometers, temperature, vehicle age…) and keep part of the data aside for testing.
  3. Let the machine find the S-curve with the lowest surprise.
  4. Check whether its probabilities are honest: of the cases it gave 70%, did it happen in about 70% of them?
  5. Choose the threshold based on what each mistake costs, not 50% out of habit.

In the real world

What is it used for?

Spam filters

Based on the words and the sender, how likely is it to be spam? It was one of the first large-scale uses.

Fraud and credit risk

Banks and card companies use it because it's fast and explainable: they can say which clues raised the risk of a transaction.

Predictive maintenance

Will this vehicle break down in the next 30 days? With the kilometers and the engine alerts you decide which ones to inspect first. The technical version works through this case by hand.

Medicine

Probability of a complication based on age, blood pressure and other tests. There it matters a lot that the probabilities are honest.

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • It gives probabilities, not just a yes or a no.
  • Very explainable: "every extra 1,000 km multiplies the risk by 1.2".
  • Fast to train and to use, even with millions of cases.
  • It's the starting point of neural networks: a neuron is almost a logistic regression.

The not so good

  • Without help, it only draws straight boundaries. Complicated shapes need extra clues or more powerful models.
  • If the "yes" cases are very rare (1 in 1,000), you have to handle the imbalance carefully.
  • Clues that say almost the same thing muddle the interpretation.
  • Like any regression, it finds relationships, not causes.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Classification
Predicting which group something belongs to (yes/no, spam/not spam) instead of a number.
Sigmoid
The S-shaped curve that turns any number into a probability between 0 and 1.
Odds
Another way of stating a probability: 75% is the same as "3 to 1".
Decision boundary
The line where the model is torn at 50%.
Threshold
The probability above which you decide to act.
Log loss
The technical name for the "surprise" the machine tries to minimize.
False alarm
The model said "yes" and it was "no".
Missed case
The model said "no" and it was "yes".
For the curious: the formulas behind it

You don't need them to understand the idea:

score: z = a·x + b (a straight line, as before) probability: p = 1 / (1 + e^(−z)) (the S-curve) surprise of one case: −log(probability it gave to what happened) the machine picks the a and b that minimize the total surprise
