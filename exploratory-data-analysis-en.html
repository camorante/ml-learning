Exploratory data analysis

Before you ask a model for anything, you have to look at the data yourself: what values each column takes, which ones are impossible, which ones move together and which ones say the same thing. It's like checking a truck before a long trip: ten minutes that save you a breakdown halfway down the road.

The setup

We're handed a table with 2,400 trips from a fleet with three kinds of truck: light, medium and heavy. Each row is a trip and each column is a value measured by the truck's onboard device. Later on we want to use it to predict fuel consumption. First, let's look at each column on its own.

How to read the chart. It's a histogram: the range of values is split into bins, and each bar counts how many trips fall into that bin. A tall bar means "lots of trips with values like this". Bars in red are impossible values. The two vertical lines mark the mean (the average of all of them) and the median (the value of the trip that sits right in the middle if you sort them from smallest to largest).

Should we remove the impossible values?

"Impossible" means it can't happen in a real truck: an average of 255 km/h, −40 °C with the engine running, or a negative load. They're usually error codes from the device, not measurements. Remove them and watch how the chart changes.

What you just saw

Look before you use

It's called exploratory data analysis (EDA for short) because you aren't looking for a specific answer yet: you explore to find out what you're working with. In five minutes with the table above, three problems turned up that would have ruined any model: speeds of 255 km/h, engines at −40 °C and negative loads. None of them shows up if you only look at the mean.

One rule from the previous topic still stands: this analysis is done only on the training part, the part the model will learn from. The data set aside for the exam (the final test) isn't looked at until the end.

Idea 1 of 5

The mean gets dragged along; the median doesn't

Most trips are short (30–60 km), but a few are really long. The mean adds up all the trips and divides by how many there are, so a single huge trip pushes it up. The median sorts the trips from shortest to longest and takes the one in the middle: it doesn't matter how long the last one is.

When a column has a long tail on one side, it's called skewed. With columns like that, the mean does a poor job of describing "the typical trip".

How to read the chart. Histogram of the distance of the 2,400 trips (each bar counts the trips in a range of kilometers). The orange bars on the right are the very long trips you add with the slider. Watch how the mean line moves and the median line stays put.

Idea 2 of 5

Values that repeat too often: error codes

A real measurement with two decimals, like engine temperature, almost never repeats exactly: 87.46 °C shows up a few times, 87.47 a few others, and so on. When one exact value appears dozens of times, it's almost always a sentinel value: the number the device writes when it couldn't take a reading. The most common ones in telemetry are −40, 0, 255, 9999 and −1.

How to read the chart. Each bar is one exact value of the column, and its length is how many times it appears. The 10 most repeated values are shown. If they all repeat only a little, the column is healthy. If one shoots up, it's suspicious.

Idea 3 of 5

Which columns move together? Correlation

Correlation is a number between −1 and 1 that tells you how much two columns move together, as if a straight line tied them:

  • Close to 1: when one goes up, so does the other (more kilometers, more liters).
  • Close to −1: when one goes up, the other goes down.
  • Close to 0: there's no straight-line relationship. There may be some other kind of relationship, or none.

Careful: two columns moving together doesn't mean one causes the other. And a handful of impossible values is enough to change the number a lot.

How to read the chart. Each dot is a trip: its horizontal position is the value of one column and its vertical position, the value of the other. If the dots form a band that goes up, the correlation is positive; if it goes down, negative; if it's a round cloud, close to 0. Idea 4 of 5

Columns that say the same thing

Some columns are near-copies of others: distance from the odometer and from the GPS, or trip duration, which comes from distance and speed. They're called redundant. They add no new information and they cause trouble: a regression can't tell which of the two to credit with the effect and gives absurd numbers, and Naive Bayes counts the same clue twice.

How to read the chart. Each square crosses a column from the row with one from the column, and its color tells you how much they move together (whether up or down doesn't matter): dark blue, nearly the same thing; light, nothing to do with each other. The diagonal is always 1 because it's each column against itself. Tap a square to see the pair.

Idea 5 of 5

Looking at everything together can tell the opposite story

Do trucks burn more fuel when they go faster? If you look at all the trips together, it seems not: fast trips burn less. But the fast ones are almost all light trucks, which burn little because they weigh little, and the slow ones are heavy. When you split by truck type, the truth comes out: within each type, going faster burns more (because of air resistance). This is called Simpson's paradox.

How to read the chart. Each dot is a trip (without the impossible values): further right, more speed; higher up, more consumption. The lines show the trend: if a line rises to the right, more speed goes with more consumption.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Set the exam aside first and explore only the training data.
  2. Look at each column with a histogram, not just the mean: shape, minimum, maximum and odd values.
  3. Write down the physical limits (speed, temperature, load) and count how many trips break them.
  4. Look for values that repeat too often: −40, 0, 255, 9999 are usually error codes.
  5. Use the median to describe skewed columns.
  6. Check which columns move together and drop or merge the ones that say the same thing.
  7. Repeat the analysis by group (truck type, customer, route): what you see in the total can be the opposite of what happens within each group.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

New customer, new devices

Each device brand has its own "no reading" codes. Exploring a new customer's data before mixing it in avoids surprises.

Reports that don't add up

An average speed through the roof or a strange average consumption is almost always a few bad records, not a real change.

Comparing fleets or drivers

Before concluding that one group burns more fuel, check whether it runs different trucks, different routes or different loads.

Choosing columns for a model

Removing the redundant ones leaves models that are more stable and easier to explain.

Connections

With the models of the ML series

What you find here decides which model fits. Very skewed columns or columns with extreme values are bad news for linear regression and for distance-based methods like k-nearest neighbors or K-means, and much less so for decision trees. Many redundant columns can be summarized into a few with PCA.

To be fair

What it costs and what you gain

What you gain

  • Recording errors are found before they reach the model.
  • You understand which columns matter and which ones are extra.
  • Misleading conclusions, like Simpson's paradox, get caught.

What it costs

  • Time, and not always with a clear ending.
  • If you look too much (especially at the exam), you end up "discovering" coincidences.
  • Some things only show up when you combine columns, not one at a time.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Histogram
A bar chart that counts how many cases fall into each range of values.
Mean
The average: the sum of all the values divided by how many there are.
Median
The middle value when you sort them all from smallest to largest.
Skew
When the values have a long tail on one side (lots of short trips and a few very long ones).
Sentinel value
A fixed number a device writes when it has no reading (−40, 255, 9999…). It looks like data, but it isn't.
Correlation
A number between −1 and 1 that tells you how much two columns move together along a straight line.
Redundant
A column that repeats the information in another one.
Simpson's paradox
When a trend in the total reverses once you look within each group.
