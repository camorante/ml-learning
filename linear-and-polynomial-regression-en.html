Linear and polynomial regression

How to find the line (or curve) that best sums up a bunch of points, and why the most complicated curve is almost never the best one.

Before we start

Something you already know how to do

Look at these taxi rides. Each point is one ride: how many kilometers it covered and how much it cost.

Without doing any math, you already see a trend: the farther you go, the more it costs. If someone asked you how much a 7 km ride would cost, you'd look at the points and say "about 11 or 12 dollars".

Regression does the same thing as your eye, but with a precise rule for deciding which line is the best. That way two different people always get the same answer.

Idea 1 of 5

A straight line has only two knobs

Any straight line is described by two numbers:

  • Slope: how much the price goes up for each extra kilometer.
  • Starting point: how much the ride costs before the taxi moves (the "flag fare").

Move the two knobs and try to get the line as close as possible to all the points. The violet lines are the errors: the distance between what the line says and what actually happened.

Idea 2 of 5

"The best" means: squared errors as small as possible

To compare lines, you have to turn all the errors into a single number. The most common rule is called least squares:

  1. Measure the error at each point (the violet line).
  2. Square each error. Literally, draw a square with that side (turn on "Show errors as squares" above).
  3. Add up the areas of all the squares. The best line is the one that leaves the smallest total area.

Why squared? For two simple reasons. An error upward and another one downward don't cancel out (both squares add up). And one big error weighs much more than several small ones: an error of 4 is a square of 16, while two errors of 2 are only 4 + 4 = 8. The line would rather be a little off everywhere than way off at one point.

The nice part is that the machine doesn't try lines at random. There is an exact calculation that gives the best line in one go, even if you have millions of points. That's why linear regression is so fast and so widely used.

Idea 3 of 5

When the data curves: the parabola

Not everything follows a straight line. The distance a car needs to stop grows much faster than its speed: at 120 km/h you need almost twice as much as at 80, not 50% more.

If you fit a straight line to that data, the line is wrong in an orderly way: the points sit above it at the ends and below it in the middle. When the errors form a pattern (here, a "smile"), that's the sign the shape was chosen badly.

The parabola is a straight line with one extra knob: how much it bends. With three knobs it can follow a U-shaped or arch-shaped curve. The way to find it is exactly the same: least squares.

Idea 4 of 5

More knobs, more flexible: polynomial regression

You can keep adding knobs. Each one lets the curve make one more "turn". This is called the degree: degree 1 is a straight line, degree 2 a parabola, degree 3 an S-shaped curve, and so on.

With enough knobs, the curve can pass exactly through every point. Looks perfect, right? Look at the next idea.

Idea 5 of 5

The trap: memorizing is not learning

Imagine a student who memorizes the answers to the practice exam. On that exam they get a perfect score. On the real exam, with new questions, they fail.

A curve that is too flexible does the same thing: it bends to touch every point, including the noise (the small measurement errors). With new data (the hollow circles), it fails. This is called overfitting.

Data to learn from New data (the real exam)

The golden rule: never judge a model on the same data it used to learn. Set a part aside and see how it does on it. Pick the degree where the error on new data is lowest, not where the curve looks most "perfect".

And be careful outside the data

High-degree curves go wild outside the zone where there is data. Here the three curves look alike between 2 and 6, but outside that stretch each one says something very different. Using the curve to guess outside what was measured is called extrapolating, and it's dangerous.

Putting it all together

The complete recipe

  1. Plot the data. Before anything else, check whether the trend is straight or curved.
  2. Set aside a group of test data. Don't touch it until the end.
  3. Start simple: a straight line. Fit it with least squares.
  4. Look at the errors. If they form a pattern, raise the degree (2, then 3).
  5. Compare on the test data and keep the simplest degree that works well.
  6. Use it only within the range of your data.

In the real world

What is it used for?

Price of a home

How much the price goes up for each extra square meter. It's the classic example of linear regression and the basis of many automated appraisers.

Fuel consumption vs. speed

A truck burns a lot of fuel at very low speed and also at very high speed. The curve is U-shaped, and a parabola finds the most efficient speed. The technical version works through this case step by step.

Calibrating sensors

A cheap sensor measures a little off, in a predictable way. You compare it with a reference sensor, and a regression (sometimes of degree 2 or 3) corrects its readings.

Trends and planning

How many sales, trips or kilometers to expect next month if the trend stays the same. Useful as a first approximation, always remembering the danger of extrapolating.

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • Very fast: the best curve comes out of an exact calculation, no trial and error.
  • Very easy to explain: "for each extra km, the price goes up $1.20".
  • Works well with little data if the chosen shape is the right one.
  • It's the foundation of many more advanced models.

The not so good

  • You choose the shape (line, parabola…). If you choose badly, it's wrong in a systematic way.
  • High degrees overfit and go wild outside the data.
  • A single very odd point (a badly measured value) can tilt the whole line, because big errors count squared.
  • It finds relationships, not causes: two things rising together doesn't mean one causes the other.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Regression
Finding a line or curve that sums up how one number changes when another one changes.
Slope
The tilt of the line: how much the output goes up for each unit the input goes up.
Intercept
The starting point: the value of the line when the input is zero.
Residual
The error at one point: what was measured minus what the curve says.
Least squares
The rule that picks the curve with the smallest sum of squared errors.
Degree
How many turns the curve can make. 1 = straight line, 2 = parabola.
Overfitting
When the curve memorizes the data (noise included) and fails on new data.
R²
A number between 0 and 1 that says how much of the variation in the data the curve explains. 1 is perfect.
