Distributions

If you write down the same thing many times (the fuel used on each trip, the deliveries that arrive late each day, the alerts from a truck), the data ends up drawing a shape. A few shapes show up again and again, and knowing which one applies tells you what's normal, what's rare and how much to expect.

The setup

A trucking company records four things about its fleet: the fuel used on each trip (liters of diesel per 100 km), how many of the day's 20 deliveries arrive late, how many harsh-braking alerts a truck gives in a day, and how long stops last at a customer's loading dock.

A single number on its own says little. But if you record many and count how often each value comes up, a bar chart appears (a histogram). With little data the chart jumps around; with a lot of data it settles into a shape. That shape is the distribution.

How to read the chart. Each orange bar says what share of the data (in %) falls on that value or in that range. All the bars together add up to 100%. The purple vertical line is the mean of the data. The blue line or dots are the underlying shape: what you would get with infinitely many data points.

What you just saw

Each kind of data has its own shape

A distribution is the list of values something can take and how often each one comes up. The histogram of a few data points is a blurry photo of it; with lots of data the photo sharpens. Three things worth keeping in mind:

  • Discrete or continuous. What you count (deliveries, alerts) only takes whole values: its distribution is separate bars. What you measure (liters, minutes) can take any value: its distribution is a curve.
  • Everything adds up to 100%. Across all the bars, or under the whole curve, is 100% of the cases. That's why a distribution can answer "what share of days…?".
  • The mean and the standard deviation summarize the center and the width (they are explained in topic 7), but not the shape: the stops have a tail that those two numbers don't tell you about.

The ideas below explain how to read a probability off the chart and the three shapes that show up most: the normal (the fuel bell), the binomial (late deliveries) and the Poisson (alerts). At the end, long tails and how to recognize which one applies.

Idea 1 of 6

Probability is a bar or a piece of area

With the underlying shape you can ask questions: what share of days have at most 2 late deliveries? What share of trips use between 30 and 35 liters? In a distribution of bars, the answer is to add up the bars for those values. In a curve, it's the area under it between those two values.

How to read the chart. It's the underlying shape from the toy. The part in orange is the range you pick with the two sliders: its probability is the sum of those bars, or the orange area under the curve. The blue part is the rest. Orange and blue together are always 100%.

Idea 2 of 6

The normal: a bell with a center and a width

The fuel used on a trip depends on many small things at once: the wind, the load, the traffic, the driver, the hills. When something is the sum of many small effects that don't depend on each other, almost always the same shape comes out: a symmetric bell, the normal distribution (or Gaussian). It only needs two numbers: the center, μ ("mu", the mean), and the width, σ ("sigma", the standard deviation).

How to read the chart. The blue bell is the fuel use of the trips. The background bands mark μ ± 1σ, μ ± 2σ and μ ± 3σ, with the % of trips that falls inside each one. The orange line is one particular trip, and the orange area is the trips that use even more than it.

The 68-95-99.7 rule holds for any normal, with any μ and σ: 68% of cases fall within 1σ of the center, 95% within 2σ and 99.7% within 3σ. That's why distance from the center is measured in sigmas, with a number called z: z = (value − μ) / σ. A trip with z = 2 is rare (only 2.3% use more) for any truck, whatever its average fuel use. It's the idea behind many rules for spotting outliers.

Idea 3 of 6

The binomial: how many of n come out "yes"

Every day there are 20 deliveries. Each one may arrive late or not, and at this company one in ten arrives late (p = 10%). If each delivery goes its own way, with no delay affecting the others, the number of late deliveries in a day follows the binomial distribution. It only needs two numbers: how many tries there are (n) and the probability of "yes" on each one (p).

How to read the chart. Each blue bar is the % of days with that number of late deliveries (0, 1, 2…). The orange bars are the "bad days": those with at least the number you pick below. The purple line is the mean, n × p.

The binomial shows up whenever you count the "yeses" out of a fixed number of similar tries: of 12 trucks going through their annual inspection, how many fail; of 300 brakings, how many are harsh; of 1,000 customers notified, how many reply. It's also the basis of logistic regression, which is really estimating the p of each case.

Idea 4 of 6

The Poisson: counting rare things over a stretch of time

A truck's harsh-braking alerts don't have a clear "n tries": they can happen at any moment of the day, and each moment has a tiny probability. When you count separate events in an interval (alerts per day, breakdowns per month, calls per hour), you get the Poisson distribution. It only needs one number, λ ("lambda"): how many events there are on average in the interval.

How to read the chart. The blue bars are the % of days with 0, 1, 2… alerts; the orange ones, the days with many (at least the number you pick). If you choose to compare, the purple rings are a binomial with n tries and p = λ / n: same mean, but with a fixed number of tries.

A useful quirk of the Poisson: the mean and the variance are equal, both λ. If a truck gives 2 alerts a day on average, its variance (the square of the standard deviation, topic 7) is also 2. If in the data the variance comes out much larger than the mean, something isn't Poisson: for example, because calm drivers and harsh drivers are mixed together.

Idea 5 of 6

Long tails: when the bell misleads you

Stops at a customer's loading dock aren't a sum of small effects: they are a multiplication. If there's a line, it takes twice as long; if the person in charge is also missing, twice as long again. Besides, they can't last less than 0 minutes. What comes out is a skewed shape: most stops are short, but a few last a very long time. It's said to have a long tail to the right (a typical shape is the lognormal).

How to read the chart. The blue curve is the real duration of the stops. The dashed purple curve is a normal bell with the same mean and the same standard deviation: what you would assume without looking at the shape. The hatched zone left of 0 is negative minutes that the bell treats as possible. The vertical lines are the median (half of the stops last less) and the mean. The orange part is the stops longer than the limit you pick.

almost symmetricvery long

With long tails, the mean drifts toward the tail and stops being "the typical value": the median describes it better. And the "mean plus two standard deviations" rule from the outliers topic flags too many normal stops as rare and, at the same time, falls short of telling how long the worst ones can last. Another typical tail is the exponential: if alerts arrive like a Poisson, the waiting time between one and the next follows that shape, with many short waits and the occasional very long one.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. A distribution says which values come up and how often. A histogram with lots of data gets close to it; with little data, it jumps around.
  2. What you count goes in bars; what you measure, in a curve. Probability is the sum of bars or the area under the curve, and the total is always 100%.
  3. The normal comes from adding many small effects. μ and σ are enough, and the 68-95-99.7 rule and z tell you what's rare.
  4. The binomial counts the "yeses" out of n tries with the same p. Its mean is n × p.
  5. The Poisson counts separate events in an interval. Its mean and its variance are both λ, and it's what a binomial becomes with many tries and a small p.
  6. If something multiplies or can't go below 0, suspect a long tail: look at the median and the percentiles, not just mean ± 2σ.
  7. Always check against the data: draw the histogram on top of the chosen shape and compare the mean with the variance for counts.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Unusual fuel use

With the bell for each truck model and route, a trip with z greater than 3 deserves a look: an underinflated tire, a dirty filter or aggressive driving. Comparing in sigmas works just as well for a delivery truck as for a long-haul one.

Alert thresholds that make sense

If a driver gives 2 alerts a day on average, 7 in one day only happens by chance 1 time in 221. With the Poisson you set a threshold that flags what's rare without filling the dashboard with false alarms.

Late-delivery penalties

With 20 deliveries a day, p = 10% and $15 per late delivery, the binomial says the average cost is $30 a day and that about 3 days a year will have 6 or more delays: useful for budgeting and for knowing when a bad day is no longer bad luck.

Stop times and service agreements

The duration of stops, unloadings or repairs has a long tail. Promising "under 45 minutes 95% of the time" is computed with the percentile of the real shape; with the bell you would promise something you can't keep.

Connections

With the models of the ML series and the other topics

The mean and the standard deviation that summarize each shape are explained in topic 7. In the ML series, Bayes' theorem uses these distributions to say what you believe about a p or a λ before and after seeing the data; Gaussian processes and the Kalman filter are built on the normal (GPS noise is modeled as a bell); logistic regression is a binomial in which p depends on the data of each case. In "Preparing the data", exploratory analysis starts by drawing histograms like the ones in the toy, outliers are hunted for in the tails, and imbalanced classes appear when what you're looking for is a rare event, like a breakdown.

To be fair

Where it helps and where it misleads

Where it helps

  • With one or two numbers (μ and σ, n and p, λ) you describe a whole shape and can answer any "what share of days" question.
  • They tell you what's truly rare and what's chance: a day with 4 late deliveries isn't a problem; one with 8 is.
  • They let you simulate: generate fake but realistic days and trips to test a dashboard or a budget.

Where it misleads

  • Real data almost never follows a textbook shape to the letter: you have to check it, especially in the tails, which is where the surprises are.
  • Mixing different groups (delivery and long-haul trucks, calm and harsh drivers) gives shapes that are none of the three.
  • The binomial and the Poisson assume each event goes its own way. If one traffic jam delays ten deliveries at once, bad days are much more frequent than they say.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Distribution
Which values something can take and how often each one comes up. Its drawing is the "shape" of the data.
Histogram
A bar chart that counts how many data points fall on each value or range.
Discrete / continuous
Discrete: only separate values, usually counted (0, 1, 2…). Continuous: any value, usually measured (32.47 liters).
Normal (or Gaussian)
The symmetric bell. It appears when you add many small, independent effects.
μ and σ
The center (the mean) and the width (the standard deviation) of a distribution.
z
How many sigmas from the center a value is: (value − μ) / σ.
Binomial
How many "yeses" come out of n identical tries, each with probability p.
Poisson
How many separate events happen in an interval, if λ happen on average.
Long tail
When a few values are much, much larger than the typical ones. The mean ends up above the median.
Lognormal and exponential
Two shapes with a long tail: the first, for things that multiply (durations, costs); the second, for waits between events of a Poisson.
