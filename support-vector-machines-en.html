Support vector machines (SVM)

To separate two groups, just any line won't do: the SVM looks for the widest street it can fit between them. The more room to spare, the fewer mistakes it makes on new cases.

Before we start

A street between two rows of cones

Imagine you have to lay out a street between orange cones and purple cones without knocking any over. You can make it crooked and tight, brushing past the cones, or you can turn it until it's as wide as possible. With a wide street, a truck that drifts a little doesn't hit anything.

The SVM does exactly that with data. Each case is a cone: for example, each braking event the GPS records, placed according to how hard it was and how fast the truck was going. The SVM draws the center line of the widest possible street. New cases are classified by which side of the line they fall on.

Idea 2 of 5

Only the edge points matter

The street rests on just a few points: the ones touching its edges (with a ring). They are the support vectors, and they give the method its name. The rest could disappear and the street would stay the same.

Drag the points. Move a far-away one and nothing happens, but if you move one with a ring, the street moves with it. If you bring a far-away point up to the edge, it becomes a support vector.

Idea 3 of 5

When they can't be separated: let some through

In real life, there's always some normal braking mixed in with the harsh ones, or the other way around. No clean street is possible. The SVM handles this by letting some points into the street or onto the wrong side, but it charges a fine for each one. The slider decides how expensive that fine is.

With a cheap fine, the street comes out wide and calm, even if it leaves several points inside. With an expensive fine, it narrows and twists so it doesn't leave any out of place, and then it risks memorizing the noise.

Idea 4 of 5

The trick: lift the data

What if no line can separate the groups? Look at delivery arrival times: arriving a few minutes early or late is normal (purple), but arriving very early or very late is an anomaly (orange). The anomalies are on both sides, so a single cut isn't enough.

The trick is to add a dimension. Raise each point to a height that depends on how far it is from zero, as if you were setting it on a bowl. Once lifted, the anomalies end up on top and the normal arrivals at the bottom, and one straight line is enough to separate them. Seen from below, that line amounts to two cuts.

Idea 5 of 5

The kernel: how far each point reaches

The good news is that the SVM doesn't need to compute those new dimensions: all it needs is a way to measure how similar two points are, the kernel. The most popular one (RBF) says "very similar if they're close, not at all if they're far apart". The slider decides how far that influence reaches.

Here, GPS positions of trucks inside the depot yard (purple) and out on the street (orange). If each point reaches very far, the boundary can't bend and comes out almost straight. If it reaches only very close, the SVM wraps each point in a little island and memorizes the noise. In between, you get the circle of the yard.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Put the features on the same scale (otherwise the one with the biggest numbers dominates the distances).
  2. Pick the kernel: linear if you expect a straight boundary or have a huge number of features; RBF for everything else.
  3. Find the widest street that puts almost every point on the right side, paying a fine for the ones it doesn't.
  4. Keep only the support vectors: they're all you need to make decisions.
  5. Classify new cases by which side of the line they fall on. The farther from the line, the more certain.
  6. Tune the fine and the reach by testing on data the model hasn't seen.

In the real world

What is it used for?

Classifying driving events

Dangerous braking, turns or acceleration, from just a few accelerometer and GPS measurements.

Text and images

Before deep learning, it was king at classifying text (spam, topics) and recognizing images. With a linear kernel it's still a very strong contender for text.

Spotting the unusual

A variant (one-class SVM) learns the boundary of "normal" and raises an alert for whatever falls outside it: failing sensors, strange routes.

Predicting numbers

Another variant (SVR) uses the same street idea to predict values, such as fuel consumption, ignoring small errors.

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • Works very well with little data and many features.
  • Always finds the same solution, the best possible one (no random starts).
  • With the right kernel it draws curved boundaries without computing new dimensions.
  • The final model only stores the support vectors.

The not so good

  • It gets slow with hundreds of thousands of cases.
  • You have to scale the features and tune the fine and the reach.
  • It doesn't naturally give probabilities, only "which side and how far".
  • With an RBF kernel it's hard to explain why it decided something.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Decision boundary
The line (or surface) that separates what gets classified as one group from what gets classified as the other.
Margin
The width of the street: the free space on each side of the line.
Support vectors
The points that touch the edge of the street or get inside it. They're the only ones that define the solution.
Soft margin
Allowing some points to sit inside the street or on the wrong side, in exchange for a fine.
C
The price of that fine. Large: a narrow, demanding street. Small: a wide, tolerant street.
Kernel
A way to measure how similar two points are. It's equivalent to lifting the data into more dimensions without actually doing it.
Gamma (γ)
In the RBF kernel, how short the reach of each point's influence is: large gamma, short reach.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need these to understand the idea:

score of a case = sum, over the support vectors, of (weight × class × similarity to the case) + offset class = the sign of the score (+ harsh, − normal) width of the street = 2 / size of the line's weights → making the street wide = making the weights small
