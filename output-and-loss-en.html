Output and loss

The last layer of a network has to answer in the language of the question: liters, a percentage, or a split across several options. And to learn, the network needs a single number that says how wrong it was: the loss. Choosing both well decides what the network learns.

The setup

A truck has just finished a 180 km trip. A neural network looks at its data (speed, stops, load, engine hours…) and answers three questions at once: how much fuel did it use, in liters per 100 km? Will it break down in the next 30 days? What kind of trip was it: urban, regional or highway?

The whole network is shared except the end: it has three heads, one per question. Each head computes one or more sums, which we will call z (the raw numbers that come out of the network), and turns them into an answer. Later, once we know what really happened, the loss grades each answer: zero if it is spot on, more the worse it does.

What you just saw

One output for each question and one grade for each answer

Inside the network, everything is sums that can give any number: −3, 0.4, 812. The last layer translates those numbers into the language of the question. For liters, nothing: the number as it is. For a yes or no, the sigmoid from topic 2, which turns it into a percentage. To choose among several options, the softmax, which splits 100% across all of them.

The loss is a grade turned upside down: a single number that says how wrong the network was. Training a network is exactly this: moving its weights so that the loss, added up over thousands of trips, goes down.

  • For numbers, the squared error. Being off by twice as much costs four times as much.
  • For probabilities, the cross-entropy. The penalty is −log of the probability the network gave to what happened: if it gave 1%, the penalty is 4.6; if it gave 90%, 0.1.
  • Being confident and wrong is very expensive. The penalty has no ceiling: the closer to 0% it gave to what happened, the higher it gets.
  • The loss decides what the network learns. Changing the loss changes what the network considers a good result.

The ideas that follow: which activation to put at the end (1), how the softmax works (2), losses for numbers and odd data (3), why a single confident and wrong answer weighs so much (4), why the squared error is not used with probabilities (5), the loss as a landscape you have to walk down (6) and how to put the dollars into the loss (7).

Idea 1 of 7

The last layer speaks the language of the question

The network ends with a sum z, which can be any number. The output activation decides what to do with it:

  • None (linear output): the number comes out as it is. For quantities: liters, minutes, dollars.
  • Sigmoid: an S that takes any number to something between 0 and 1. For the probability of a yes.
  • Softmax: takes one z per option and turns them into percentages that add up to 100%. To choose among several options.

Idea 2 of 7

Softmax: exponentiate and split

The trip-type head ends with three numbers, one per type: the higher, the more the network believes in that type. They can be negative and they do not add up to anything in particular. The softmax turns them into percentages in two steps:

  1. Exponentiate: compute ez (the number e = 2.718 raised to each z). Everything comes out positive, and each extra point of z multiplies by 2.7.
  2. Split: divide each result by the sum of the three. Now they add up to exactly 100%.

The temperature T divides the z values before exponentiating. With a high T, the percentages even out; with a low T, the highest one takes almost everything. The order never changes.

Idea 3 of 7

For numbers: the squared error and odd data

For fuel use, the most common loss is the mean squared error (MSE): the difference between what the network says and what happened, squared, and averaged over all trips. Being off by 2 liters costs 4; being off by 10 costs 100. So the network is very afraid of big errors.

The problem is that a single odd data point also produces a huge error. Here, 10 trips on the same route; the tenth was misread by the tank sensor. If the network could only give one number for the route, which one would each loss choose? With the MSE, the mean; with the absolute error (MAE, the distance without squaring), the median; with the Huber loss, which is quadratic for small errors (up to 2 liters) and a straight line for big ones, something in between.

Idea 4 of 7

Confident and wrong: a single truck can take over the grade

For probabilities, the loss is the cross-entropy: each truck's penalty is −log(the probability the network gave to what happened). Topic 15 of the math collection explains where it comes from: it is the surprise, measured in the same units as information. Here we look at what it means when training on a batch of trucks.

Idea 5 of 7

Why not the squared error with probabilities

What if we also gave the breakdown head the squared error, (probability − what happened)²? It seems reasonable, but it fails exactly when it is needed most. To learn, the network looks at how much the loss changes if it moves its z a little: that is the push it gets (the derivative, from topic 16 of the math collection).

With the cross-entropy, the push is simply probability − what happened: if it gave 1% to a breakdown that happened, the push is almost 1, the maximum. With the squared error, the push is also multiplied by the slope of the sigmoid, which is almost zero at its ends: the more confident and wrong the network is, the less it gets corrected.

Idea 6 of 7

The average loss is a landscape you have to walk down

A network does not focus on a single truck: it adds up the penalties of all the examples and takes the average. That average depends on the weights of the network. If we draw the average loss for each value of one weight, we get a landscape with valleys and slopes, and training means walking down to the bottom of the valley.

Here, the smallest possible network: one neuron with a sigmoid (the logistic regression) that estimates the probability of a breakdown in 30 days from the kilometers since the last inspection, with 40 trucks. To be able to draw it, we keep its bias fixed and move only its weight w: positive means “more kilometers, more breakdowns”.

Idea 7 of 7

Putting the dollars into the loss

The cross-entropy treats both mistakes the same: warning about a breakdown that does not come and not warning about one that does. In the fleet they do not cost the same. A roadside breakdown (tow truck, urgent repair, the lost delivery) costs about $4,000; sending a truck that did not need it to an inspection, about $300.

The fix is a class weight: in the loss, each truck that broke down counts w times. The network then learns to warn earlier. Training with weight w and warning from 50% up is the same as warning from a probability of 1/(1 + w) up with the unweighted network. Imbalanced classes have their own topic in Preparing the data.

At the bottom, what each weight would cost in dollars on 2,000 new trucks: breakdowns that slip through × $4,000 plus extra inspections × $300. The green dot is the cheapest weight.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Look at what kind of answer the question asks for: a quantity, a yes or no, or one among several options.
  2. Choose the output: no activation for quantities, sigmoid for a yes or no, softmax for several options.
  3. Choose the loss that goes with it: squared error for quantities (MAE or Huber if there is odd data), cross-entropy for probabilities.
  4. If there are several heads, add up their losses, taking care that none shouts louder than the others just because of its units.
  5. If the mistakes do not cost the same, give them weights according to what each one costs in dollars.
  6. Average over all the examples: that gives a landscape over the weights, and training means walking it down step by step.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Fuel use

Networks that estimate the liters for a route end in a linear output with squared error. If the data has odd tank readings (badly logged refuelings, loose sensors), you switch to Huber so they do not drag the whole network.

Predictive maintenance

“Breakdown in the next 30 days?” ends in a sigmoid with cross-entropy. Since breakdowns are rare and expensive, you train with a weight for them or lower the alert threshold, depending on what each mistake costs.

Trip type and driving behavior

Classifying a trip (urban, regional, highway) or an accelerometer event (hard braking, pothole, curve, impact) ends in a softmax: one percentage per class that adds up to 100%, ready to make a decision or to feed another system.

Arrival time

An estimated time of arrival model can use MAE instead of MSE: a two-hour delay because of an accident should not drag the forecasts for every other trip. Many teams also use several heads: arrival, fuel use and risk in a single network.

Connections

With the other topics

The cross-entropy comes from topic 15 of the math collection, where it is explained as surprise and as the price of using the wrong code. That each loss is “the least surprising” is the maximum likelihood of topic 14: the squared error corresponds to bell-shaped noise, and the cross-entropy, to a loaded coin or die. The push “probability − what happened” comes from the chain rule of topic 16.

A neuron with a sigmoid and cross-entropy is exactly the logistic regression, and a linear output with squared error, the linear regression. Walking down the loss landscape is gradient descent, and class weights come back in imbalanced classes. In this collection, the neuron is topic 1, the sigmoid appears in activation functions, the shared body of the three heads is the multilayer network, and how the pushes reach the first layers is topic 5, backpropagation.

To be fair

What it gives and what to watch

What it gives

  • With the output and the loss properly paired, the push is “what it said − what happened”: simple, and strong when the network is badly wrong.
  • The cross-entropy gives honest probabilities: if the network says 10%, it happens roughly one time in ten.
  • A single network can answer several questions by adding up the losses of its heads.

What to watch

  • The squared error lets itself be dragged by a single odd data point.
  • One confident and wrong answer can dominate the loss of a whole batch; sometimes it is a labeling error, not the network’s.
  • The losses of several heads have different units: without weights, the one measured in big numbers takes over.
  • Lowering the loss is not the same as making money: the dollars have to be put in on purpose.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Head
The final part of a network that answers one question. A network can have several on the same body.
Logit (z)
The raw number that comes out of the last sum, before the output activation.
Linear output
No activation: the number comes out as it is. For quantities.
Sigmoid
Takes any number to a probability between 0 and 1.
Softmax
Turns several scores into percentages that add up to 100%: exponentiate and split.
Temperature
A number that divides the scores before the softmax: high, everything more even; low, more decisive.
Loss
The number that measures how wrong the network was and that training tries to bring down.
MSE, MAE, Huber
Losses for quantities: squared error, error without sign and a mix of the two.
Cross-entropy
Loss for probabilities: −log of the probability given to what happened.
Gradient (push)
How much and in which direction the loss changes when each number in the network moves a little.
Class weight
A multiplier that makes the mistakes of one class count more, for example breakdowns.
