Imbalanced classes

When what you're looking for is rare (a breakdown, a fraud, a crash), a model that never raises an alert looks almost perfect. To judge it properly you have to ask different questions and, above all, decide at what level of risk an alert is worth it.

The setup

Every Monday, a model looks at each truck's data from the previous week (engine vibration, temperature, hours since the last service, fault codes, age) and computes a breakdown risk for the week ahead, between 0% and 100%. If the risk goes above a threshold, the truck goes in for an inspection before it heads out.

Out of every 1,000 trucks, only about 23 break down that week (2.3%). Breakdowns are the rare class and healthy trucks are the common class: that's why people call these classes "imbalanced". The squares below are 1,000 real trucks from the test (data the model didn't see while learning).

How to read the chart. Each square is a truck. Solid orange: it broke down and the model raised an alert (a useful hit). Hollow orange: it broke down and there was no alert (the breakdown that slips through). Purple: an alert, but the truck was healthy (a false alarm: one inspection too many). Gray: healthy and no alert.

It starts at 100%: the model never raises an alert. Lower it little by little and watch the three numbers.

Three ways to measure how well it does

  • Accuracy: out of the 1,000 trucks, how many it got right (alert and breakdown, or no alert and no breakdown).
  • Precision: of the trucks it sent to inspection, how many were really going to break down.
  • Recall: of the trucks that broke down, how many it warned about in time.

Try this, in order:

  1. At 100% (never alert): accuracy is 97.7%, without preventing a single breakdown.
  2. Lower it to 50%: very few alerts, with good precision, but almost every breakdown slips through.
  3. Lower it to 4% or 5%: many more breakdowns caught in exchange for quite a few extra inspections. Accuracy drops, and yet the model is far more useful.

What you just saw

With rare classes, accuracy is useless

If only 2.3% of trucks break down, always saying "it won't break down" is right 97.7% of the time. No useful model beats that by much on accuracy, because to find breakdowns it has to take risks and get some healthy trucks wrong. That's why, with rare classes, you look at two other questions separately:

  • Precision: when it raises an alert, is it usually right? (This measures the cost of false alarms.)
  • Recall: of what there was to find, how much does it find? (This measures the cost of what slips through.)

Moving the alert threshold trades one for the other. There is no "correct" threshold in the abstract: it depends on how much each kind of mistake costs (idea 3). It's the same idea as the breakdown alert in the Bayes topic: when what you're looking for is rare, even a good detector gives lots of false alarms.

Idea 1 of 5

The rarer it is, the more accuracy fools you

The model that never raises an alert is right exactly as often as the cases that aren't rare. If breakdowns are 30%, it gets 70% right (and you can see it's bad). If they're 0.5%, it gets 99.5% right and looks like an excellent model.

Idea 2 of 5

The curve that shows off and the curve that tells the truth

To see a model at every alert threshold at once, you draw curves. The most famous one, the ROC curve, compares the breakdowns caught with the false alarms as a share of the healthy trucks. Since there are so many healthy trucks, a thousand false alarms look like nothing and the curve comes out beautiful. The precision-recall curve compares the breakdowns caught with all the alerts, and it shows the real problem: lots of inspections to find each breakdown.

How to read the chart. Each point on the line is a different alert threshold; the orange dot is the one you choose below. On the ROC curve, a perfect model goes through the top-left corner and one that flips a coin follows the diagonal. On the precision-recall curve, the perfect one goes through the top right and the coin-flipper follows a horizontal line at 2.3% (alerting at random, only 2.3 out of every 100 alerts would be breakdowns). The big number is the area under each curve: the closer to 1, the better.

Idea 3 of 5

Costs decide the threshold

A breakdown on the road costs a lot: tow truck, emergency repair, lost delivery, angry customer. Say $4,000. A preventive inspection that turns out to be unnecessary costs little: an hour at the maintenance shop and the truck off the road, about $150. With those numbers, alerting pays off even if only one in 27 alerts is a real breakdown. The best threshold is much lower than the 50% used by default.

Idea 4 of 5

Rebalancing the data: less magic than it seems

A very common recipe is to "balance" the data before training, so the model sees as many breakdowns as healthy trucks. There are several ways:

  • Weights: while learning, each breakdown counts as 42 healthy trucks.
  • Oversampling: copy breakdowns until they match the number of healthy trucks.
  • Undersampling: keep only as many healthy trucks as there are breakdowns (97% of the data is thrown away).
  • SMOTE: invent new breakdowns halfway between similar breakdowns.

We compare them all with the same model and the same 10,000 test trucks.

How to read the bars. The first two measure how well the model ranks the trucks from most to least risky, without fixing a threshold (the areas from idea 2). The next two are precision and recall with the default 50% threshold. The last one is the average risk the model gives compared with the real breakdown rate (2.3%, the black tick).

Idea 5 of 5

If you balance, correct the percentages

The model trained with weights has learned in a world where half the trucks break down. Its risks are inflated: it says "40%" for trucks that actually break down 2% of the time. For ranking trucks, that doesn't matter; for deciding with costs (idea 3) or for a report ("this truck has a 40% risk"), it's a serious mistake. The fix is a simple calculation that undoes the rebalancing, the same one Bayes uses to go from a world with a different rate back to the real one.

How to read the chart. Trucks are grouped by the risk the model gives them (horizontal) and, for each group, we check what percentage really broke down (vertical). If the model tells the truth, the dots fall on the gray diagonal: "of the trucks I give 20%, 20% break down".

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Check the share of the rare class and always compare against the model that never raises an alert.
  2. Forget accuracy: measure precision and recall, and the precision-recall curve.
  3. Split carefully: training and the exam should have the same share of breakdowns, and the exam needs enough breakdowns (with 20, each one is worth 5% of recall).
  4. Put a price on each mistake and choose the alert threshold with those costs.
  5. Rebalancing is optional: it almost never improves how the model ranks; it moves the threshold and inflates the risks.
  6. If you rebalance, correct the risks before using them as percentages.
  7. Revisit the threshold if the costs or the breakdown rate change.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Predictive maintenance

Engine, brake and tire breakdowns: rare, expensive, and with cheap inspections. The alert threshold is usually low.

Fuel fraud

Suspicious fill-ups among thousands of normal ones: a person reviews each alert, and their time sets the threshold.

Crashes and dangerous driving

Very rare; near-misses (hard braking, sharp turns) are easier to predict because they're less rare.

Serious delays

Deliveries more than two hours late: few, but they're the ones the customer cares about.

Connections

With the models of the ML series

Logistic regression gives risks as percentages and has a threshold you can move. Bayes' theorem explains why alerts about something rare are usually false and how to correct a risk when the rate changes. XGBoost has its own parameter to give more weight to the rare class, with the same caveats as idea 4.

To be fair

What it costs and what you gain

What you gain

  • An evaluation that reflects what matters: breakdowns found and inspections spent.
  • An alert threshold justified with money, not habit.
  • Risks that mean what they say.

What it costs

  • Putting a price on each mistake means talking to the business side and the maintenance shop.
  • With few rare cases, every number is very noisy.
  • The threshold has to be revisited when the fleet changes.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Rare class (minority class)
What you're looking for that almost never happens: the breakdown.
Precision
Of the alerts, how many were real.
Recall
Of the real cases, how many got an alert.
False alarm (false positive)
An alert for a case that wasn't one.
Case that slips through (false negative)
A real case that got no alert.
ROC curve / PR curve
Charts that show the model at every alert threshold at once.
Rebalance
Change the proportions of the data (or their weights) before training.
Calibration
A 20% risk meaning that 20 out of 100 break down.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need it to understand the idea:

precision = correct alerts / all alerts recall = correct alerts / all breakdowns cheapest alert threshold (with well-calibrated risks): inspection cost / (inspection cost + breakdown cost) = 150 / 4,150 ≈ 3.6% correcting a risk r from a model trained with weight ×w on breakdowns: odds = r / (1 − r) / w real risk = odds / (1 + odds)
