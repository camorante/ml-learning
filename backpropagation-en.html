Backpropagation

the chain rule, backwards

For a network to learn, you need to know, for each of its weights, whether moving it a little brings the error down, and by how much. Trying them one by one is impossible when there are millions. Backpropagation computes them all at once: it walks the network backwards, from the error to the input, and shares out the blame among the weights according to what each one contributed.

The setup

These are the same 30 trips from topics 1 and 3: for each one we know the load (in tonnes) and the fuel use (in liters per 100 km). Normal trips form a sloping band, and there are 12 trips that don’t add up: 6 burn too much and 6 burn too little.

The network is the one from topic 3: neuron A watches whether a trip burns too much, neuron B whether it burns too little, and the output neuron combines their votes. But this time someone has set it up wrong: line B is 3.5 liters too low, and the 6 trips that burn too little get away. Let’s see how the network works out, on its own, which weights are to blame.

How to read the chart. On the left, the trip’s two numbers; in the middle, neurons A and B; on the right, the output neuron and the loss, the number that measures how badly the network did (the one from topic 4: 0 if it is right and completely sure, more and more if it is wrong). The arrows carry the weights. Numbers in black go forward (what the network computes); numbers in orange go backward: they are the blame, how much the loss would change if that number went up a tiny bit. A negative blame means “raise me and the loss goes down”.

Walk through the network, step by step

There are six steps: the forward pass, the output error, the blame of the output weights, the blame of the hidden neurons, the blame of the input weights and, at the end, one learning step: each weight moves a little against its blame.

Try this, in order:

  1. With trip 3, press “Next” until the end. Notice that the blame gets multiplied by a number at every arrow and every neuron.
  2. In step 4, look at neuron A: its blame is almost zero. A didn’t vote on this trip (its vote is 0.0002%), and a neuron that switched off can’t change anything. The blame falls entirely on B.
  3. In step 6, the loss of trip 3 drops from 4.81 to 2.17 in a single step.
  4. Switch to trip 6, which is normal and which the network already gets right: all the blames come out tiny. If you’re not wrong, you hardly correct anything.

What you just saw

Forward to answer, backward to share out the blame

Training a network means moving its weights so that the loss goes down. To know which way to move each one, you need its gradient: how much the loss changes if that weight goes up a tiny bit (it is the derivative from math topic 16). Here we have called it blame. Backpropagation is the way to compute all the blames at once:

  • First, forward. The network computes its answer and keeps the numbers in between: the scores and the votes of each neuron.
  • At the output, the error is very simple. With the sigmoid and the cross-entropy from topic 4, the blame of the output score is p − y: what the network said minus what really happened.
  • Then, backward. Each neuron receives its blame from above and passes it down multiplied by its local slope: how much what comes out changes if what goes in changes. That is the chain rule.
  • The blame of a weight is the signal that goes in along its arrow times the error that comes back along it.
  • One learning step moves each weight a little against its blame.

The ideas that follow take it one piece at a time: why you don’t try weight by weight (1), the chain of multiplications (2), what happens where paths split (3), the “signal times error” shortcut (4), what happens to the blame in networks with many layers (5) and one learning step with all 30 trips at once (6).

Idea 1 of 6

What if you nudge each weight by hand?

There is a way to get the blame of a weight without knowing anything about chains: move it a tiny bit, run the 30 trips through the network again and see how much the loss changed. It works, and it is what you would do with a spreadsheet. The problem is the price: one full pass over the data for every weight.

Idea 2 of 6

The chain: blame multiplies link by link

Think of a delivery that arrives late. You can trace the final delay backwards through each leg of the route: how much the unloading delayed the arrival, how much the traffic jam delayed the unloading, how much the late departure delayed the jam. If each leg turns one minute of delay into two, three legs turn one into eight: the effects multiply.

The same happens in the network. The weight that links fuel use to neuron B affects the loss through a chain: the weight changes B’s score, the score changes B’s vote, the vote changes the output score and that changes the loss. The weight’s blame is the product of the four links.

How to read the chart. From right to left (on a phone, from top to bottom), the blame travels from the loss to the weight. Each box is a link and its orange number is its local slope: how much what comes out of the link changes if what goes in changes. Underneath, the blame accumulated on reaching that point: the product of all the links it has crossed. On wide screens, some bars give an idea of the size of each blame (on a scale in factors of 10).

Idea 3 of 6

Where paths split, the blames add up

The trip’s fuel use goes into both neurons, A and B. If the measured fuel use were a bit higher, the loss would change along two paths at once: through A and through B. The total blame of the fuel use is the sum of what arrives along each path. It is like a delay at the warehouse that affects two routes: the total cost is the delay to one plus the delay to the other.

It is not just a curiosity: with many neurons, each signal spreads over hundreds of paths, and backpropagation adds up what comes back along all of them. The same happens with the trips: the blame of a weight is the sum of what each trip contributes (you will see it in idea 4).

How to read the chart. Trip 3 carries a 4.8 t load. Across, the fuel use it could have logged; up, how much the loss would rise for each extra liter of fuel use (its blame). The blue line is what arrives along A’s path; the green one, along B’s; the thick black one is the sum. Above zero, more fuel use makes the loss worse; below, it makes it better. The vertical line is the slider’s fuel use.

Idea 4 of 6

The shortcut: signal in times error back

There is a rule that saves nearly all the arithmetic: the blame of a weight is the signal that goes in along its arrow (what the neuron before it sends) times the error that comes back to the neuron it reaches. A weight that carries no signal can’t be to blame, and neither can one that reaches a neuron with no error.

With 30 trips, the blame of each weight is the average of what each trip contributes. The chart shows those 30 contributions for the weight you pick.

How to read the chart. One bar per trip, numbered from 1 to 30. Downward, negative contributions (“raise this weight”); upward, positive ones (“lower it”). Orange: a trip that doesn’t add up; purple: a normal one. The bars are divided by 30, so the weight’s blame is the sum of all of them; the dashed line is that sum and the big number, its value.

Idea 5 of 6

With many layers, the blame fades along the way

In topic 2 you saw that the slope of the sigmoid never goes above 0.25. Now you can see why that matters so much: the blame that reaches a layer has crossed every layer above it, and in each one it was multiplied by that slope. Ten layers are ten multiplications.

The chart is a real network with 10 hidden layers of 8 neurons, put to work on the 30 trips with random weights (drawn the recommended way for each activation) and repeated with 50 draws. For each layer we measure the average blame of its weights.

How to read the chart. One bar per layer: on the left, the first hidden layer (the one that looks at the data); on the right, the output. The height is the average blame of that layer’s weights compared with the output’s, which is 1. The scale jumps in factors of 10: each horizontal line is ten times less than the one above.

Idea 6 of 6

One learning step with all 30 trips

In the toy, the network learned from a single trip. The usual thing is to compute the average blame over many trips and move all the weights at once: new weight = weight − rate × blame. The learning rate says how long the step is. With 0.03, each weight moves three hundredths of its blame.

Here you can take the steps one by one and watch the lines move. How to choose the rate, how many trips to use in each step and when to stop is topic 6, training well; this is the gradient descent from the ML series, with the blames supplied by backpropagation.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Forward: run the data through the network and keep the scores and votes of each neuron.
  2. The loss: compare the answer with what really happened. At the output, the blame is p − y.
  3. Backward, layer by layer: each neuron receives its blame from above and passes it down multiplied by its slope and by the weight of each arrow.
  4. Where paths meet, the incoming blames add up.
  5. The blame of each weight: signal in times error back, added up (or averaged) over all the examples.
  6. One step: each weight moves a little against its blame. All of this costs about two or three forward passes, whether the network has 9 weights or millions.
  7. Watch the blame in deep networks: if the slopes are small, almost nothing reaches the first layers.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Odd fuel-use detectors

Any network that learns to tell normal trips from odd ones (by fuel use, load, grade, speed) adjusts its weights with backpropagation, trip by trip or in batches, every time the maintenance shop confirms a new case.

Estimated time of arrival

Arrival-time models have millions of weights. Nudging them one by one would take days for every step; backpropagation gives all the blames for the cost of two or three passes, which is why they can be retrained every night with the day’s trips.

Sensor series

Networks that read a trip’s series (temperature, engine speed, second-by-second GPS) are very deep “along time”. The fading blame from idea 5 is their big problem; you will see it in topic 9.

Why the network raised an alert

The blame of the fuel use or of the load (idea 3) is not only for training: it says which input, if it changed a little, would move the answer the most. It is a quick way to explain an alert to the fleet manager.

To be fair

What it gives you and what to watch

What it gives you

  • All the blames for the price of about two or three passes, whether the network has 9 weights or billions.
  • It is exact: it doesn’t approximate like the nudge by hand, apart from the computer’s rounding.
  • It is mechanical: each piece only needs to know its own slope. That is why libraries do it on their own.

What to watch

  • You have to keep the numbers from the forward pass: with large networks and large batches, that is a lot of memory.
  • It only says which way is down from here, not where the best point is: with steps that are too long, you overshoot.
  • If the slopes are small (saturated neurons, many layers), the blame doesn’t reach the first layers and those layers don’t learn.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Backpropagation
Computing the blame of every weight by walking the network backwards, from the loss to the input.
Gradient
The blames of all the weights, taken together: how much the loss changes if each weight goes up a tiny bit.
Forward pass
Computing the network’s answer, keeping the numbers of each neuron.
Backward pass
Sharing out the blame from the output to the input, multiplying by the slope of each piece.
Local slope
How much what comes out of a piece changes if what goes in changes a little.
Chain rule
In a chain of pieces, the slopes multiply.
Output error (p − y)
The blame of the output score: what the network said minus what happened.
Learning rate
How long each step is: what fraction of its blame is subtracted from each weight.
Finite differences
Computing the blame by nudging the weight by hand and seeing how much the loss changes. Slow, but useful for checking.
Vanishing gradient
Blame that becomes tiny after crossing many layers with small slopes.

forward: zA = −1.1·load + 2·fuel − 53 hA = σ(zA) zB = 1.1·load − 2·fuel + 36 hB = σ(zB) z = 10·hA + 10·hB − 5 p = σ(z) loss: −ln p if the trip is odd, −ln(1 − p) if it is normal backward: blame of z = p − y blame of vB = hB · (p − y) (signal × error) blame of zB = (p − y) · 10 · hB·(1 − hB) (= δB) blame of wB = δB · (load, fuel) (signal × error) blame of fuel = δA·2 + δB·(−2) (two paths: they add up) trip 3 (4.8 t, 22.6 L): zB = −3.92 hB = 1.9% p = 0.8% loss 4.81 p − y = −0.99 δB = −0.99 · 10 · 0.019 = −0.19 blame of wB·fuel = −0.19 · 22.6 = −4.28 one step: new weight = weight − 0.03 · blame wB·fuel: −2 − 0.03·(−4.28) = −1.87 → loss of trip 3: 2.17
