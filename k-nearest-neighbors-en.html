k-nearest neighbors

The easiest algorithm to explain: to decide what something new is, find the known cases that look most like it and go with what the majority says.

Before we start

"Birds of a feather…"

You want to know what your house is worth. What do you do? You look at what the most similar houses sold for: same neighborhood, similar size, similar age. If the three most similar ones sold for $180k, $190k and $200k, yours is probably worth about $190k.

You haven't learned any formula for the housing market. You've just looked for neighbors and copied what happened to them. That is exactly k-nearest neighbors (KNN for short).

Idea 1 of 5

How many neighbors? Small k is jumpy, large k is blurry

With k = 1, each new point copies its single nearest neighbor. If that neighbor was an odd case (noise), we copy the mistake: the map fills up with little islands. With a very large k, even faraway points get a vote and the map turns into a blur that no longer follows the real shape. The best k is somewhere in between, and you pick it by looking at accuracy on new data.

Idea 2 of 5

What does "close" mean? Watch the units

Eight trucks: kilometers since their last service, average engine temperature, and whether they broke down. A new truck (♦) arrives with 14,000 km and 97 °C. Does it look more like the ones that broke down?

If we measure distance with the raw numbers, a difference of 1,000 km weighs the same as a difference of 1,000 °C. Temperature, which goes from 87 to 101, practically disappears from the calculation. The fix is to put all the features on the same scale (for example, from 0 to 1) before measuring distances.

Idea 3 of 5

It learns nothing: it remembers everything

Most models study the data once, extract a rule (a line, a tree) and then throw the data away. KNN does the opposite: it doesn't "study" anything, it just stores every case, and all the work comes at prediction time, when the new case has to be compared with every stored one.

100100 million

That's why, with lots of data, people use "indexes" (like the index of a book) that find the neighbors without looking at all of them, or approximate searches that accept not always finding the exact neighbor in exchange for being thousands of times faster. That's how search engines for similar images or texts work.

Idea 4 of 5

It predicts numbers too: the average of the neighbors

To predict a number (minutes of delay, price, fuel use), instead of voting you average what happened to the k neighbors. Move k and watch the curve: with k = 1 it passes through every point, noise included; with a large k it flattens out.

Idea 5 of 5

With too many features, everyone is far away

With 2 features, some points are close and others far. But with 50 or 100 features something strange happens: all the distances look alike. The "nearest" neighbor is almost as far as the farthest one, and "being similar" stops meaning anything. This is called the curse of dimensionality.

1500

That's why, before using KNN, it pays to keep only the features that matter, or to compress them (we'll see this in PCA). Modern search systems use hundreds of features, but chosen very carefully by a neural network so that distance really does mean something.

Putting it all together

The complete recipe

  1. Store every known case along with its answer.
  2. Put the features on the same scale so none of them dominates just because of its units.
  3. Measure the distance from the new case to every stored one.
  4. Keep the k nearest.
  5. Vote (if it's a category) or average (if it's a number). The closest ones can count more.
  6. Choose k by trying several and checking accuracy on data it didn't use.

In the real world

What is it used for?

Finding similar things

"Trips like this one", "customers like this one", "products you might like": almost every similarity search is nearest neighbors.

Spotting the unusual

A trip whose nearest neighbor is very far away doesn't look like anything known: maybe a GPS fault, fraud or a strange event.

Filling in missing values

If a record is missing its temperature, it gets filled in with the temperature of its most similar records (KNNImputer).

The memory of AI assistants

When an assistant "searches your documents", it turns the text into numbers and looks for the passages closest to your question.

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • You can understand it in a minute, and every prediction explains itself: "these are the similar cases".
  • It assumes no particular shape: it adapts to curved, odd boundaries.
  • Adding new data is instant: you just store it.
  • It works just as well for categories, numbers and similarity search.

The not so good

  • Predicting is slow and uses a lot of memory with lots of data.
  • Very sensitive to units: you have to scale.
  • It struggles with many features or with features that don't matter.
  • It doesn't tell you which features matter or give you a general rule.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Neighbor
A stored case that looks like the new one.
k
How many neighbors vote or get averaged.
Distance
A number that measures how alike two cases are: the smaller it is, the more alike they are.
Scaling
Putting all the features in comparable ranges, for example from 0 to 1.
Lazy learning
Not building any model until the question arrives; KNN is the classic example.
Curse of dimensionality
With many features, all points end up almost equally far from each other.
Approximate search
Finding "almost" nearest neighbors much faster.
