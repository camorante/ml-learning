Eigenvectors and eigenvalues

When a matrix moves the plane, almost every arrow turns. A few special directions only stretch or shrink, without turning. Those directions (and how much they stretch) explain where a fleet ends up in the long run, what shape a cloud of data has, and whether a filter settles down or spins out of control.

The setup

As in topic 3, a matrix (four numbers in two columns) moves the whole plane: the first column says where the arrow → ends up, and the second, where the arrow ↑ ends up.

This time we don't move a truck but a fan of 16 arrows that leave the center in every direction, all of length 1. After applying the matrix, almost all of them have turned: they point somewhere else. We look for the ones that don't turn: they stay on their own line and only get longer or shorter.

What you just saw

The directions a matrix doesn't turn

A matrix turns almost every arrow, but there are often a few special directions where it only stretches or shrinks. Mathematicians call the arrows on those directions eigenvectors (“own vectors”, from German eigen) and the number they stretch by, the eigenvalue.

  • An eigenvalue greater than 1 stretches; between 0 and 1, it shrinks; negative, it also flips the arrow around; 0, it squashes it down to the center.
  • A 2 × 2 matrix has at most two different special directions (except for cases like “do nothing”, where every direction is one).
  • Along those directions the matrix is as simple as it gets: multiply by a number. Everything else is a mix of the two.

That last sentence is the reason for the whole topic. If you know how a matrix behaves along its special directions, you know what will happen when you apply it many times in a row, which is exactly what a Markov chain, a filter or a training algorithm does. The five ideas that follow tell that story.

Idea 1 of 5

What “not turning” means, and why sometimes there is no such direction

Pick a direction and look at how much the matrix turns it. If you repeat that for every direction, from 0° to 180°, you get a curve. The special directions are the points where the curve touches 0 (no turn at all) or ±180° (it flips around, but stays on its own line).

  • A rotation turns every direction by the same amount: the curve is flat and never touches 0. It has no special directions (at least not with ordinary numbers; the technical version tells what happens with complex numbers).
  • A symmetric matrix (one that stays the same if you swap rows and columns: the top-right number equals the bottom-left one) always has two special directions, and they form a right angle.

Idea 2 of 5

Applying the same matrix over and over

What happens if you apply the matrix to an arrow, then again to the result, and again, and again? Any arrow can be seen as a mix of the two special directions. At each step, each part of the mix gets multiplied by its eigenvalue. The part with the largest eigenvalue grows faster (or fades more slowly), so after a few steps almost all of the arrow points along its direction, no matter where it started.

Computers use exactly this trick to find the most important special direction of huge matrices: it is called the power method.

They are all drawn the same size so you only see where they point; how long they really are is given by the “length” figure.

Idea 3 of 5

Where the fleet ends up in the long run

Every day, each truck in the fleet is in one of three states: on the road, in the shop or idle (no load assigned). From one day to the next it can change, and the table below says with what probability. For example, out of every 100 trucks in the maintenance shop today, tomorrow 30 go back on the road, 60 stay in the shop and 10 end up idle. This is a Markov chain.

Applying the table for one day is applying a matrix to the fleet split. Applying it day after day is idea 2: the split gets closer and closer to a special direction. This one has eigenvalue exactly 1 (it neither grows nor fades, because trucks neither appear nor disappear), so the split stays put on it. That final split is called the stationary distribution.

Idea 4 of 5

The shape of a cloud of data

Each dot is a truck: horizontally, how far its engine temperature is from the fleet average; vertically, the same for the oil. The two tend to rise and fall together, so the cloud comes out stretched and tilted.

That shape is summed up in a table of three numbers: how spread out the dots are horizontally, how spread out vertically, and how much the two temperatures tend to move together (positive if, when one goes up, the other does too). It's called the covariance matrix, and it is symmetric (idea 1), so it has two special directions at a right angle: they are the axes of the cloud. The eigenvalue of each one says how much variation there is along that axis. The one with the larger eigenvalue is the direction in which trucks differ the most: that is what principal component analysis (PCA) looks for.

How to read the chart. Each gray dot is a truck. The ellipse covers the vast majority. The orange arrow is the special direction of the larger eigenvalue (the long axis) and the purple one, that of the smaller one (the short axis). Each arrow is as long as two standard deviations along its axis: twice the square root of its eigenvalue.

Which way?

With many features. In the real fleet there are dozens of signals per truck (speeds, RPM, fuel use, temperatures, pressures…). The covariance matrix is then 30 × 30 instead of 2 × 2, but the idea is the same: its eigenvectors are the axes of a cloud in 30 dimensions, and the two or three with the largest eigenvalues usually explain almost all of the variation. First it pays to look at the data in the exploratory analysis and put the features on comparable scales: otherwise the one with the biggest numbers (RPM) takes the main axis just because of its units.

Idea 5 of 5

Settles down or spins out of control: the size of the eigenvalues

A filter tracks a truck's position and speed from the GPS. Every second it predicts where the truck will be and then corrects with the new reading: a bit of the position and a bit of the speed (it's a very simplified version of the Kalman filter). At startup it is off by 20 meters. Does that error go away?

What happens to the error from one second to the next is, once again, a matrix applied over and over. By idea 2, in the long run the eigenvalue that is largest in size (ignoring the sign) rules. If it measures less than 1, the error is multiplied each second by something smaller than 1 and fades out. If it measures more than 1, it grows without end: the filter spins out of control, even if each correction seems reasonable.

How to read the chart. On the left, the filter's position error, second by second, for 30 seconds (above the line, the filter thinks the truck is further ahead than it is; below, further behind). On the right, the filter's two eigenvalues as dots: their distance from the center is their size. If both fall inside the circle (size less than 1), the error fades out. Sometimes the eigenvalues aren't ordinary numbers and come out as a pair above and below the axis: then the error also oscillates.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. A matrix turns almost every direction. The ones it doesn't turn, and only stretches or shrinks, are its special directions (eigenvectors).
  2. The eigenvalue is how much each one stretches: greater than 1, less than 1, negative (it flips) or 0 (it squashes).
  3. They don't always exist: a rotation has none; a symmetric matrix always has them, and at a right angle.
  4. Applying the matrix many times pulls any arrow toward the direction of the eigenvalue that is largest in size.
  5. In a Markov chain, that destination has eigenvalue 1: it is the stationary split of the fleet.
  6. In a cloud of data, the eigenvectors of the covariance are its axes: PCA.
  7. In a system that repeats itself, eigenvalues smaller than 1 in size calm it down; larger than 1, they make it spin out of control.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Planning the shop

With the table of changes between states from the last few months, the stationary distribution says how many trucks will be in the shop on any given day (with 200 trucks, about 13) and how many days the fleet takes to recover after a spike in breakdowns.

Summing up the telemetry

Five or thirty signals per trip are summed up in two or three numbers, the main axes of the cloud. In the example of the technical version, two axes explain 89% of the variation of five signals: one is “how much load it carries” and the other, “city or highway”.

Filters that don't spin out of control

Before turning on a position filter or a cruise control, you check that the eigenvalues of its error matrix measure less than 1. Otherwise any small error gets amplified second by second.

Which warehouse is the most central

If a truck goes from warehouse to warehouse following the usual routes, the stationary distribution of that chain says which warehouses it visits most. It's the same idea Google used to rank web pages (PageRank): the eigenvector with eigenvalue 1 of a huge network.

Connections

With the models of the ML series and with the other topics

Several models of the ML series rest on this piece. Principal component analysis is the eigenvectors of the covariance (idea 4). Markov chains end up at their eigenvector with eigenvalue 1 (idea 3). The Kalman filter works because the eigenvalues of its error measure less than 1 (idea 5). And gradient descent moves quickly or zigzags depending on how different the eigenvalues of the error's curvature are: if the valley is very steep in one direction and very flat in another, it has to take short steps and takes forever.

It all starts from seeing matrices as movements of the plane (topic 3), and topic 4 already used special directions without naming them: a projection leaves alone the arrows that are already in its plane (eigenvalue 1) and squashes the perpendicular ones (eigenvalue 0). The next step is topic 6, the SVD: similar directions that exist for any matrix, even if it is not square or symmetric.

To be fair

What helps and where it misleads

What helps

  • It turns “apply a matrix many times” into “multiply a few numbers many times”, which you can understand at a glance.
  • Symmetric matrices (covariances, curvatures) always behave well: directions at a right angle and ordinary eigenvalues.
  • A single number, the size of the largest eigenvalue, tells you whether a system calms down or spins out of control.

Where it misleads

  • Not every matrix has ordinary special directions (rotations) or enough of them (shears).
  • If the two largest eigenvalues are almost equal, applying the matrix many times converges extremely slowly.
  • An eigenvector has no fixed sign: (1, 1) and (−1, −1) are the same direction, and each program may return one or the other.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Eigenvector
An arrow the matrix doesn't turn: after applying it, it stays on the same line (though it may point backward).
Eigenvalue
How much that arrow stretches. Negative if it flips around; 0 if it gets squashed.
Special direction (eigendirection)
The line of an eigenvector. Any arrow on that line, long or short, is also an eigenvector.
Symmetric matrix
One that doesn't change when you swap rows and columns. It always has special directions at a right angle.
Power method
Applying the matrix many times to any arrow to find the direction of the largest eigenvalue.
Stationary distribution
The split among states that a Markov chain reaches, no matter where it starts. It is the eigenvector with eigenvalue 1.
Covariance matrix
The table that sums up how much each feature varies and how much they vary together. Its eigenvectors are the axes of the data cloud.
Stable
A system that repeats itself and whose errors fade out: all its eigenvalues measure less than 1.
