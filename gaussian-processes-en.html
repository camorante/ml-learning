Before we start

Something you already know how to do

Imagine you measured the temperature in your town three times in one day: at 6:00 it was 12°, at 13:00 it was 24° and at 19:00 it was 18°.

If someone asks you what the temperature was at 9:00, you didn't measure it, but you can guess: "somewhere between 15° and 21°". You're fairly sure, because you have measurements close by.

And if they ask about 3:00 in the morning, you can still guess, but with more doubt: "maybe between 4° and 16°". You have no measurement anywhere near.

What you just did in your head (guess the value and also say how unsure you are) is exactly what a Gaussian process does. The difference is that it does it with numbers and by clear rules.

Idea 1 of 4

Before seeing any data, many curves are possible

A Gaussian process starts out knowing nothing. Think of it as a giant bag full of curves. They are all smooth, they are all possible, and none of them is "the right one" yet.

Below you see a few pulled out of the bag at random. They move to remind you that there are infinitely many more.

Idea 2 of 4

Each data point rules out curves

When we measure something, we keep only the curves that pass through that point or very close to it. All the others get thrown out. It's like a detective: every new clue eliminates suspects.

If you average all the curves that survive, you get the blue line: the best guess. In the jargon it's called the mean.

Idea 3 of 4

The zone of doubt: knowing when you don't know

The most valuable thing about a Gaussian process is that it doesn't just give one answer. It also draws a shaded zone that says: "the real value is almost certainly in here".

  • Close to the data the zone is narrow: the model is sure.
  • Far from the data the zone widens: the model admits it's guessing.

Many machine learning models always answer with the same confidence, even when they're making things up. A Gaussian process warns you. That's gold when a mistake is expensive: medicine, finance, costly machines.

Idea 4 of 4

The kernel: the "neighbors look alike" rule

How does the model decide which curves are reasonable? With a very simple rule called the kernel: two nearby points have similar values; two far-apart points, not necessarily.

The temperature at 9:00 and at 9:05 is almost the same. The one at 9:00 and the one at 21:00 can be very different. The kernel only decides how far that likeness reaches. Move it and see what happens.

An important detail

What if the measurements have errors?

In real life sensors are a little off: a cheap thermometer, a GPS with a bad signal. If the model blindly trusts every measurement, it makes crazy curves to pass through every point.

That's why you tell it how much noise to expect. With noise, the curve passes near the points instead of through them, and it stays calmer.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Choose the likeness rule (kernel). How smooth do you think the thing you'll measure is? How much error do your measurements have?
  2. Measure a few points. You don't need thousands; a handful already works.
  3. Keep the curves that fit. The model throws out the ones that don't pass near your data.
  4. Read the result. The mean gives you the best guess anywhere, and the zone of doubt tells you how much to trust it.

In the real world

What is it used for?

Looking for gold and mapping terrain

The idea was born in the mines of South Africa in the 1950s (it's called kriging, after the engineer Danie Krige). From a few boreholes you estimate how much ore there is across the whole site, and where it's worth drilling again because the doubt is largest.

Finding the best settings with few tries

When every try is expensive (training an AI model for hours, baking a recipe, a lab experiment), the model suggests what to try next: where the mean looks promising or where the doubt is large. This is called Bayesian optimization.

Filling gaps in sensor signals

A vehicle enters a tunnel and the GPS stops sending positions. A Gaussian process estimates the route in that gap and also says how much it trusts each estimated stretch.

Spotting odd things

If a new measurement arrives that falls way outside the zone of doubt, something is probably wrong: a broken sensor, a machine overheating, a patient getting worse.

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • It works very well with little data.
  • It tells you how unsure it is, not just the answer.
  • It's flexible: you don't force the curve to be a straight line or a parabola.
  • It can be understood and explained, as you just saw.

The not so good

  • It gets slow with lots of data: if you double the data, the work goes up about 8 times. With hundreds of thousands of points you need tricks or approximations.
  • Choosing the kernel well takes some judgment.
  • It loses strength when each data point has a huge number of input variables (for example, the pixels of a photo).

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Gaussian process
A bag of possible curves, from which we keep the ones that fit the data.
Prior
What you believe before seeing data: the whole bag.
Posterior
What you believe after seeing data: the curves that survived.
Mean
The best guess: the average of the curves that survive.
Variance
How much doubt there is at each spot. It's the width of the shaded zone.
Kernel
The rule that says how alike two points are, based on their distance.
Noise
The error we expect in each measurement.
For the curious: the two formulas behind it all

You don't need them to get the idea. The blue line and the zone of doubt come from these two calculations:

mean(x) = k(x)ᵀ · (K + σ²·I)⁻¹ · y variance(x) = k(x,x) − k(x)ᵀ · (K + σ²·I)⁻¹ · k(x) RBF kernel: k(a,b) = exp( −(a−b)² / (2·ℓ²) )

K compares the data points with each other using the kernel, k(x) compares the new point with each data point, y is the measured values, σ² is the noise and ℓ is the "reach of the likeness" from the slider.
