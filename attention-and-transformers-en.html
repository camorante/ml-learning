Attention and Transformers

The piece behind LLMs, the language models like the chatbots that answer questions. A recurrent network reads a sequence one item at a time and sums it all up in a single memory. Attention does something else: each item looks directly at all the others and decides how much each one matters to it. Take that idea, stack it many times, and you have a Transformer.

The setup

A truck's black box logs what happens on each trip as a list of tokens: it leaves a depot, loads, brakes, gets stuck in traffic… Sometimes a fault code turns on, like the ones in topic 10. At the end, the truck stops at the workshop, where the mechanic writes down the last code that turned on, and then it returns to the depot it left from. This is one of the trips:

A small model, a Transformer with 3,972 numbers, has learned to predict the next token by reading 2,000 trips like this one. Almost every token is random and can't be guessed. But two can: the one after “return” (the depot from the start, far back) and the one after “workshop” (the last code, which may not be the only one). Try this, in order:

  1. At the start, “return” asks, at position 18. Head 1 puts 90.5% of its attention on “Valencia”, position 1, and head 2 puts 88.3%. The model predicts Valencia with 99.7%.
  2. Press “workshop”. Head 1 puts 97.7% on P0299, the last code, and only 2.3% on C0035, which turned on earlier. Switch to head 2: it barely looks at the codes (3.0% and 2.4%).
  3. Go back to “return” and raise the temperature to 4: the attention spreads out (23.3% on Valencia with head 1) and the model gets it wrong: it predicts Zaragoza with 92.9%. Lower it to 0.25: all the attention goes to Valencia.
  4. Leave the temperature at 1, press “workshop” and turn off the mask: now the model sees tokens it shouldn't, and it gets thrown off. It predicts P0562, with 71.0%, a code that never showed up on the trip.
  5. Tap an event, for example “braking”: what comes after it is random, and the model spreads the probability without pretending to know.

What you just saw

Each token decides whom to listen to

In attention, each token does three things with its numbers: a question (“where is the depot?”), a label that announces what it is (“I'm a depot”) and a content it can hand over (“Valencia”). For each token, its question is compared with the labels of all the others. The better they match, the higher the score. The scores are turned into percentages that add up to 100%, and the token takes a mix of the contents with those percentages.

Nobody told the model that “return” has to look for the depot: the numbers of the questions and the labels were learned on their own, with the usual training, to predict the next token better.

The ideas that follow: what changes compared with a recurrent network (1), the math of the question and the label (2), why we divide by √d (3), the several heads (4), how it knows the order (5), what else is in a Transformer block (6) and how it writes token by token, which is what a language model does (7).

Idea 1 of 7

Reading one at a time or looking at everything at once

The recurrent network of topic 9 reads the trip token by token and keeps everything it has read in a single memory, its state, which gets rewritten at every step. If the answer depends on something from many tokens ago, that piece of information has to survive all the steps in between. Attention keeps nothing: when it needs something, it looks.

Idea 2 of 7

Question, label and content

Each token comes in as a list of numbers, its embedding (the one from topic 10). The attention layer multiplies it by three learned matrices and gets three arrows: the question q, the label k and the content v. How well they match is measured with the dot product: high if two arrows point the same way, zero if they form a right angle, negative if they point in opposite directions. The scores become percentages with the softmax from topic 4.

Here, with a trip of only 3 tokens and 2 numbers per arrow, so you can see the math: “departure”, “Madrid” and “return”. “return” asks.

How to read the chart. The three thin arrows are the labels k of the tokens. The orange one is the question q of “return”: drag it by its tip. The table gives, for each token, the dot product q·k, the score divided by √2 (2 is how many numbers each arrow has; idea 3 explains why) and the weight that comes out of the softmax. The circle is the output: the mix of the contents v with those weights.

Idea 3 of 7

Why we divide by √d

In a real model, the arrows don't have 2 numbers but 64 or 128 per head: that's d. The dot product adds up d products, and the more random numbers you add up, the more the results spread apart: they grow like √d. With very spread-out scores, the softmax gives everything to a single token, and then it barely reacts to changes: the network gets very small nudges during training. Dividing by √d brings the scores back to a normal scale, whatever d is.

How to read the chart. A random question against 10 random labels, with d numbers each. Each bar is the weight a label gets; the orange one, the favorite. On the left (or top), without dividing; on the right (or bottom), dividing by √d.

Idea 4 of 7

Several heads, each doing its own thing

A single attention gives a single mix per token. A Transformer does several at once, the heads: each one with its own question, label and content matrices, and therefore free to look for something else. At the end, the outputs of all of them are joined together. The toy model has 2 heads of 8 numbers; the big ones, dozens.

Idea 5 of 7

Attention knows nothing about order: you have to tell it

Look at the math of idea 2: the position of each token never shows up. To attention, the trip is a pile of tokens with no order. If you swap two of them, “workshop” sees exactly the same thing. But to know which code was the last one, order matters.

The fix is positional encoding: each token gets a fingerprint added to it that depends only on its position, made of waves that rise and fall at different speeds. We trained two identical models, one with fingerprints and one without.

Idea 6 of 7

A Transformer block: attention and a bit more

Attention mixes information between tokens. A Transformer block adds three pieces to it: a small network that works on each token separately (with what it has gathered, it decides what to do), two shortcuts that add the input to the output so nothing gets lost, and norms that keep the numbers on a reasonable scale (the ones from topic 7). A Transformer is a stack of identical blocks.

How to read the chart. From top to bottom, the path of each token's numbers. The dashed box is the block, the one that gets repeated in big models. Tap a piece to see what it does and how many numbers it learns in the toy model.

Idea 7 of 7

Writing token by token: a language model

With the causal mask, the prediction at each position only uses what's to its left. That's why the model can write: it predicts the next token, adds it and predicts again. An LLM (large language model) is exactly that, with tokens that are pieces of words and with many more blocks and numbers, trained on a huge amount of text to predict the next token. It doesn't look things up in a database or reason separately: everything it “knows” is in its numbers, learned from that task.

Putting it all together

How a Transformer that writes works

  1. Chop the text into tokens (the events of the trip, or pieces of words) and swap each token for its embedding, a row of a table (topic 10).
  2. Add the position: the wave fingerprint of each place, so that order counts.
  3. Attention: each token makes its question, its label and its content; it compares its question with the labels of the earlier ones, divides by √d, turns the scores into percentages with softmax and takes the mix of contents. Several heads at once.
  4. Shortcut, norm and small network on each token. That's a block; many are stacked.
  5. Predict: at the end, softmax over all the possible tokens. It's trained on a huge amount of text to get the next one right, with backpropagation (topic 5) and Adam with warm-up (topic 6).
  6. Write: pick a token, add it and start again.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

The breakdown history

The codes and workshop visits of each truck, in order, are a sequence. A Transformer can predict the next breakdown by looking directly at a code from months ago that a recurrent network would have forgotten.

Trips and arrival times

Stops, traffic jams, refueling and legs of a trip, as in the toy: to estimate the arrival time or flag an odd trip, attention links what's happening now with what happened at departure.

Notes and messages

A language model summarizes the mechanics' notes, sorts the incidents drivers write up or answers questions about the manual. It writes with confidence even when it's wrong: anything important has to be checked.

Long sensor series

Engine temperature, fuel use or acceleration, one reading per second: one day is 86,400 readings. Comparing all of them with all of them doesn't fit, so the series are chopped into windows or summarized before going through attention.

To be fair

What it gives and what to watch out for

What it gives

  • It looks directly at any distance: the depot, 10 or 35 tokens back, is right 100% of the time; the LSTM from topic 9, with the same numbers, doesn't get past 51.5%.
  • All positions are computed at once, so training is highly parallel.
  • You can see where each head looks, as in the map of idea 4.
  • The same recipe works from 4,000 to 175 billion numbers.

What to watch out for

  • The cost grows with the square of the length: 40 tokens are 820 pairs; 100,000 tokens, about 5 billion in each layer.
  • Without positional encoding it doesn't know the order: the last code drops from 99.2% to 62.4%.
  • It needs a lot of data and depends on the starting point: with two other seeds, the last code is right 83.2% and 97.5% of the time.
  • An attention map isn't a complete explanation: the small network and the other pieces also decide. And a language model predicts what's likely, not what's true.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Token
Each piece a sequence is split into: an event of the trip, a word or a piece of a word.
Attention
The way each token mixes the information of the others according to how much each one matters to it.
Question, label and content (q, k, v)
The three arrows each token makes: what it looks for, what it announces and what it gives. In English papers: query, key and value.
Attention weight
The percentage one token pays to another; the ones of each token add up to 100%.
Temperature
A number the scores are divided by before the softmax: low, it focuses; high, it spreads out.
Head
One of the attentions that work at the same time, each with its own matrices.
Positional encoding
The wave fingerprint added to each token so the model knows its place.
Causal mask
What stops each token from looking at the ones that come after it.
Shortcut (residual connection)
Adding the input of a piece to its output.
Transformer block
Attention + shortcut + norm + small network + shortcut + norm. They're stacked.
Language model (LLM)
A huge Transformer trained to predict the next token of a huge amount of text.
