Maximum likelihood

The data has already happened and can't be changed. What moves is the guess: which value of the parameter made exactly what happened most probable? That question, asked again for different kinds of data, explains where almost every loss function in machine learning comes from.

The setup

A truck's telemetry system sends a harsh braking alert every time the deceleration goes past a limit. For 14 days, the alerts of each day were counted: 3, 1, 4, 0, 4, 2, 3, 1, 3, 2, 1, 6, 3, 2.

A day's alerts are counted well by the Poisson distribution (topic 8), which has a single number, λ (lambda): how many alerts the truck gives on a normal day, on average. That number isn't written anywhere: it has to be worked out from the counts. The idea of maximum likelihood is to try values of λ and ask each one: what probability did you give to what actually happened? The one that gave it the most wins.

How to read the chart. At the top, each box is a day: the orange number is how many alerts there were and the blue bar is the probability the chosen λ gave to that day (with its percentage). Bottom left (or in the middle on a phone), the orange dots stack the days by their alerts and the blue bars are how many days of each kind that λ would expect in 14 days. Bottom right (or at the end), the likelihood curve: for each possible λ, how well it explains all 14 days at once (higher is better). The orange dot is your λ.

Which truck?

Truck B drives on the highway and brakes hard less often. Its 14 days: 3, 1, 1, 0, 2, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 2, 2, 0.

Shortcut

Take λ straight to the highest point of the curve: the most likely value.

Try this, in order:

  1. With λ = 1, the 6-alert day gets 0.05% and the 4-alert days get 1.5%: this λ says those days can hardly happen, and they did.
  2. Go up to λ = 5: now the ones that don't fit are the quiet days, with 0 or 1 alerts.
  3. Find the highest point of the curve by hand. It's at λ = 2.5: 35 alerts over 14 days, the plain old average.
  4. Switch to truck B: the peak moves to 1.0, which is its average. Press “Go to the peak” to check.

What you just saw

Likelihood: the data stays still, the parameter moves

Ordinary probability goes forward: “if the truck gives 2.5 alerts a day on average, what's the probability it gives 6 tomorrow?”. Likelihood turns it around: the 14 days are already counted, and for each possible λ you ask what probability it gave to those 14 specific days. It's the same calculation, read backwards.

  • One number for each guess. λ = 1 gives the 14 days together a probability of 1 in 5 · 10¹⁵. λ = 2.5 gives them 1 in 8 · 10¹⁰: about 64,000 times more.
  • The one that gave the most probability wins. The value of λ at the peak of the curve is the maximum likelihood estimate. For the Poisson it always comes out as the average of the counts, which is what anyone would have done by eye; the nice part is that the same reasoning works when there's no answer that obvious.
  • Likelihood is not “the probability that λ is 2.5”. It compares guesses with each other, but it doesn't share out 100% among them. That's what the Bayesian approach does (idea 7).

The ideas that follow explain why probabilities are multiplied and logarithms are used (1), what happens with percentages (2), why more data gives more certainty (3) and, above all, where the loss functions used by the models of the series come from: least squares (4), absolute error (5) and log loss (6). At the end, the danger of taking the data too literally (7) and how the peak is found when there's no formula (8).

Idea 1 of 8

Multiply probabilities, add logarithms

Why is the probability of the 14 days together the product of each day's probability? Because the days are assumed to be independent: what happened on Monday doesn't change Tuesday's probabilities (topic 9). With independent events, “this and this and this” is computed by multiplying.

The practical problem: multiplying many numbers smaller than 1 gives a tiny number. With a year of data you get something like 0.000…0003 with 282 zeros in front, and with a year and a half the computer can no longer store it and rounds it to 0. The fix is to work with logarithms: the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms, and adding up hundreds of normal numbers is no problem at all. Since the logarithm always grows when what's inside grows, the peak is in the same place. That's why people almost always talk about the log-likelihood.

How to read the chart. Horizontally, how many days of truck A's data are used (on a log scale: 1, 10, 100, 1,000). Vertically, how many zeros the probability of all those days together has (with the best λ): −100 means 10⁻¹⁰⁰, a 1 preceded by 99 zeros after the decimal point. The dashed red line is the smallest value an ordinary computer number can hold. The orange dot is your choice.

Idea 2 of 8

Percentages: the most likely one is the one you saw

Another kind of data: of a customer's 40 deliveries, 3 arrived late. Each delivery is a “yes or no” with the same probability p of being late, so the number of late ones follows a binomial (topic 8). For each possible p you compute the probability of “3 late out of 40”: if p is very small, 3 late deliveries are too many; if it's very large, they're too few.

The peak is always at late / deliveries: 3 / 40 = 7.5%. Again, what anyone would compute. But now you know why it's the best estimate and, above all, you can see the shape of the curve around it (idea 3).

How to read the chart. Horizontally, the percentage of late deliveries you assume. The blue curve is the likelihood of each guess, divided by the one at the peak (so the peak is 1 and the rest reads as “how many times less”). The dashed line marks the peak and the orange dot, the percentage you choose below.

Idea 3 of 8

More data, narrower peak: more certainty

A 10% late rate can come from 1 out of 10 deliveries or from 100 out of 1,000. The peak is in the same place, but the curve doesn't have the same shape. With 10 deliveries it's a broad hill: 3% or 25% explain the data almost equally well. With 1,000 it's a sharp spike: any value a little away from 10% makes the data much, much less probable.

The width of the peak measures how much you know. A common rule is to keep the values whose likelihood is at least 14.7% of the peak's: that gives 95% intervals, like those of topic 12, and almost the same as Wilson's. And as there, cutting the width in half takes four times as much data.

How to read the chart. Four likelihood curves, all with 10% late deliveries: with 10, 40, 200 and 1,000 deliveries. The chosen one is thick blue; the others, gray. The blue band is the interval: the percentages that stay above the dashed 14.7% line.

Idea 4 of 8

If the noise is a bell, you get least squares

This is where it gets interesting for machine learning. You want a straight line that gives a truck's fuel use from the load it carries. The points don't fall exactly on the line: there's noise (wind, driver, traffic). If you assume that noise has the shape of a bell (a normal) with a fixed width, the probability of each trip depends on how far it is from the line, and the log-likelihood calculation boils down to this:

log-likelihood = a fixed number − (sum of squared errors) / (2 × width²)

Making the likelihood as large as possible is the same as making the sum of squared errors as small as possible: the least squares of linear regression and of topic 4. They aren't a whim: they're the right answer if the noise is a bell.

How to read the chart. On the left (at the top on a phone), 12 trips: load in tonnes and fuel use in liters per 100 km. The blue line has the slope you choose (and always goes through the center of the data); the purple lines are the errors. On the right, for each slope, the sum of squared errors (a valley) and the log-likelihood with noise of width 2 (a hill). The orange dot is your slope.

Idea 5 of 8

If the noise has fat tails, you get absolute error

Changing the shape of the noise changes the loss. Nine unloadings at a customer's dock took 18, 22, 15, 25, 20, 17, 23, 19 and 95 minutes (that day a delivery note was missing). What's the typical duration?

With bell-shaped noise, a data point 70 minutes from the center is almost impossible, so maximum likelihood moves the center toward it so it isn't quite so impossible: you get the mean, 28.2 minutes. With fat-tailed noise (the Laplace distribution, which allows big surprises), the calculation turns into the sum of absolute errors (not squared), and its minimum is the median: 20 minutes, as in topic 7. Choosing a loss is choosing what you believe about the noise.

How to read the chart. At the top, the 9 unloadings (orange dots) and the shape of the noise centered on the value you choose (in blue): the height of the curve above each dot is how probable it finds it. At the bottom, the loss of each central value (minus the log-likelihood: lower is better); the dashed lines mark the mean and the median.

Idea 6 of 8

If the answer is yes or no, you get log loss

A model gives each delivery a probability of being late based on the trip's kilometers, with an S-shaped curve: logistic regression. The likelihood of one delivery is the probability the model gave to what happened: if it was late, its probability of being late; if it was on time, its probability of being on time. You multiply the 40 together and take the logarithm.

With the sign flipped and divided by the number of deliveries, that calculation is the log loss, also called cross-entropy, the one minimized by logistic regression and almost every network that classifies. (Entropy will get its own topic later in the collection.) It punishes being very sure and wrong extremely hard: giving 1% to something that happens costs 4.6; giving it 50% costs only 0.69.

How to read the chart. At the top, each delivery is a dot: at the very top the ones that were late and at the very bottom the ones that were on time, by the trip's km. The blue curve is the probability of being late the model gives. At the bottom, each delivery's red bar is its penalty: minus the logarithm of the probability the model gave to what happened.

Idea 7 of 8

With little data, maximum likelihood believes everything

A new customer: 5 deliveries, none late. Maximum likelihood says 0%: “this customer will never have a late delivery”. It's the answer that makes those 5 data points most probable, and it's absurd: across the fleet, 8% of deliveries are late. With little data, maximum likelihood overfits: it takes what it has seen literally.

The fix is to add what you already knew before seeing the data, a prior (the Bayesian approach). Here it amounts to adding a few imaginary deliveries with the fleet's average punctuality. The peak of “likelihood × prior” is called MAP (maximum a posteriori). In logarithms it's “log-likelihood + a penalty for straying from normal”: the same thing regularization does in machine learning models.

How to read the chart. The dashed orange curve is the likelihood (only the customer's data); the purple one, the prior (what is known about the fleet, narrower the more imaginary deliveries there are); the blue one, both together. Each curve is scaled so its peak is 1. The diamonds mark the peaks.

Idea 8 of 8

No formula: climbing the hill step by step

For the Poisson or the binomial, the peak has a formula (the mean, k / n). For the logistic regression of idea 6, it doesn't: you have to search for it. The most used method is the hiker in the fog: look at which way the ground goes up right where you stand, take a step in that direction and repeat. It's the gradient descent of the ML series (here “ascent”, because you climb the likelihood; going down the loss is the same thing). The slope of the ground is computed with derivatives, which will get their own topic later.

How to read the chart. It's a map of the terrain: each point is a model from idea 6 (midpoint horizontally, steepness vertically), and the darker the blue, the more likely, like the contour lines of a mountain map. The star is the peak. The orange path is the steps taken from the bottom-left corner.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Choose a model of how the data is generated: Poisson for counts, binomial for yes/no, normal for measurements with bell-shaped noise…
  2. Write the probability of each data point in terms of the parameters and multiply them (if the data points are independent).
  3. Take logarithms: the product becomes a sum and the peak doesn't move.
  4. Find the peak: with a formula if there is one (the mean, k / n) or by climbing step by step if not.
  5. Look at the width of the peak: it tells you how much you know and gives intervals.
  6. Be wary with little data: add a prior or regularization so you don't believe a 0% or a 100%.
  7. And the other way around: every loss function hides an assumption about the noise. Squares = bell; absolutes = fat tails; log loss = yes/no.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Is this day unusual?

With λ = 2.5 alerts a day fitted by maximum likelihood, a day with 9 alerts has a probability of 0.09% (9 or more, 0.11%). Going from “that looks like a lot” to “this is an anomalous day” first requires a model of what's normal.

Stop times and arrival times

Stops at the dock are fitted with a lognormal (topic 8) by maximum likelihood. With it you compute the probability that a stop goes over 45 minutes, and whether or not to charge for waiting time.

Late risk per delivery

The model that gives each delivery's risk is trained by minimizing log loss, that is, by maximum likelihood. That's why its percentages can be read as probabilities and used to make decisions in dollars.

Driver rankings

A new driver with 0 alerts in 3 days is not “the smoothest in the fleet”. With a prior (the fleet's average alerts), their estimate stays close to the average until they build up days, and the ranking stops rewarding luck.

Connections

With the models of the ML series and the other topics

Almost every model in the series is trained by maximizing a likelihood, even if it doesn't say so. Linear regression with least squares is maximum likelihood with normal noise (and the projection of topic 4 is its peak). Logistic regression maximizes the likelihood of a Bernoulli, and gradient descent is the way to find the peak. The Bayesian approach multiplies the likelihood by a prior: that's where regularization comes from. Gaussian processes choose their kernel's scale by maximizing the likelihood of the data, and the Kalman filter tunes how much noise the GPS and the motion have in the same way.

In this collection it builds on the mean and median of topic 7, the distributions of topic 8, the independence of topic 9 and the sampling of topic 11; the width of the peak connects with the intervals of topic 12, and comparing the peaks of two models is one of the hypothesis tests of topic 13. To be fair

Where it helps and where it misleads

Where it helps

  • A single recipe for any kind of data: counts, percentages, times, measurements, yes/no.
  • It explains where each loss function comes from and when another one is a better fit.
  • With lots of data it's the best possible estimate, and the width of the peak tells you how much to trust it.

Where it misleads

  • Everything depends on the chosen model: if the alerts aren't Poisson (very different drivers mixed together), the peak is the exact answer to the wrong question.
  • With little data it believes everything: 0 out of 5 gives 0%.
  • It assumes independent data; with days or trucks that resemble each other, the peak comes out too sharp.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Parameter
The number you can't see that decides the shape of the data: λ for the alerts, p for late deliveries.
Likelihood
With the data fixed, the probability each value of the parameter gave to that data. It's used to compare values with each other.
Log-likelihood
Its logarithm: the sum of the logarithms of each data point's probability. Same peak, no tiny numbers.
Maximum likelihood
Keeping the parameter value that makes the data most probable (the peak of the curve).
Independent
Data where what happens in one doesn't change the probabilities of another. It lets you multiply.
Loss function
What a model tries to make small while training. Many are minus the log-likelihood of some noise model.
Log loss (cross-entropy)
The average of minus the logarithm of the probability given to what happened. The one classifiers use.
Overfitting
Believing the data too much: with 0 late out of 5, saying there will never be a late delivery.
Prior and MAP
What was known before the data, and the peak of likelihood × prior. In logarithms, likelihood plus a penalty: regularization.
Gradient
The direction in which the hill rises fastest from where you stand. It's used to find the peak step by step.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need it to understand the idea:

Poisson: P(k alerts) = e^(−λ) · λ^k / k! log-likelihood of 14 days: 35 · log λ − 14 · λ − (sum of log k!) peak: derivative 35/λ − 14 = 0 → λ = 35 / 14 = 2.5 (the mean) with λ = 2.5: log-likelihood = −25.11 → probability 1.24 · 10⁻¹¹ binomial (3 late out of 40): 3 · log p + 37 · log(1 − p) + constant peak: 3/p − 37/(1 − p) = 0 → p = 3/40 = 7.5% normal (fixed σ): log-likelihood = constant − Σ errors² / (2σ²) → maximum likelihood = least squares Laplace: constant − Σ |errors| / b → median yes/no: Σ log(probability given to what happened) → minus this, divided by n = log loss with a prior (m imaginary deliveries at 8%): p = (late + 0.08 · m) / (deliveries + m) 0 out of 5 with m = 20 → 1.6 / 25 = 6.4%
