Least squares as a projection

Fitting a line to some points looks like a matter of trial and error. It isn't: the best line has an exact formula, and behind it there is a simple picture. The data are an arrow, the model is a plane, and the best prediction is the shadow of the arrow on that plane.

The setup

A truck has made 10 trips with different loads, from 2 to 23.5 tonnes. On each trip the telemetry measured its fuel use, in liters per 100 km. The more load, the more fuel, but not always by exactly the same amount: wind, traffic and the driver matter too.

We want a line that sums up the relationship, so we know how much it will burn with any load. A line is set by two numbers: its height (what the truck would burn empty) and its slope (how many extra liters per tonne). Which one is best?

How to read the chart. Each orange dot is a trip: further right, more load; higher up, more fuel. The blue line is your line. The purple segments are the errors: how far off the line is on each trip, measured vertically. Each square has that error as its side: its area is the squared error.

  4. Press the best line: 26.20 + 0.41 × load, with a sum of 12.3. Try nudging either slider a little: the sum always goes up.

What you just saw

"Least squares": the line with the smallest sum of squares

To compare lines you need a single number that says how badly each one does. The most common one is this: take the error on each trip, square it, and add them all up. The line with the smallest possible sum is called the least squares line, and it is the one inside linear regression.

The surprising part is that you don't have to grope around for it. The button doesn't try thousands of lines: it does a short calculation and jumps straight to the best one. In the topic on gradient descent you reach the same place by going downhill step by step; here we see why a shortcut exists. The five ideas below explain it with a picture: a shadow and a right angle.

Idea 1 of 5

Why squares, and not just the errors?

Adding up the raw errors doesn't work: some are positive and some negative, and they cancel out. There are two natural fixes: add up the errors without their sign (the absolute values), or add up their squares. Squares have three advantages:

  • They punish big errors hard: an error of 4 liters counts as 16; two errors of 2 liters, as 8 in total. The line would rather be a little off on every trip than very off on one.
  • There is exactly one best line, no ties.
  • That line comes from a formula, with no searching.

The downside: because big errors are punished so hard, a single odd data point pulls the line a lot.

How to read the chart. The same 10 trips. The blue line is the best line according to the squares; the dashed purple one, the best according to the absolute values. The last trip (the one with the most load) has a sensor you can break: its reading goes up and gets marked with a ring.

Idea 2 of 5

The picture: the data are an arrow, and the best prediction is its shadow

To see it, we keep only 3 trips: with 4, 12 and 20 tonnes, the truck burned 26, 33 and 34 liters. Those three fuel readings are three numbers, so they can be drawn as an arrow in space (as in topic 1, but with three axes: one per trip).

A model also gives three numbers, one per trip. If the model is "the same number for every trip", its predictions are arrows like (30, 30, 30) or (27, 27, 27): they all fall on one line. If the model is "height + slope × load", with two numbers to choose, its predictions fill a plane. The best prediction is the point on that line (or plane) closest to the data arrow: its shadow, as in topic 2. And the distance that's left is exactly the square root of the sum of squares.

Idea 3 of 5

The error is perpendicular to everything the model can explain

In the previous drawing, the error of the best prediction makes a right angle with the line or the plane. In numbers, "right angle" means the dot product is 0 (topic 2). The plane of the line model is built from two columns of data: a column of ones (the height) and the load column (the slope). So the best line meets two conditions that are very easy to check:

  • The sum of the errors is 0 (dot product with the column of ones): overall, the line sits neither above nor below the dots.
  • The sum of error × load is 0 (dot product with the load): the errors don't grow or shrink with the load, so tilting the line wouldn't help.

That's two equations with two unknowns (height and slope), and they're solved with school math. They are called the normal equations ("normal" is another way of saying perpendicular). That's the button's shortcut.

Idea 4 of 5

More columns, bigger plane: the training error always goes down

Each data column you add to the model (road grade, temperature, time of day…) makes the space of possible predictions bigger: from a line to a plane, from a plane to something with more dimensions. The shadow on a bigger space is always as close or closer to the data. So the error on the training data never goes up when you add columns, even if they are pure noise.

Idea 5 of 5

Two columns that say almost the same thing

A trip table has the load and the total weight (load + the weight of the empty truck, the tare). If all trucks weigh almost the same when empty, the two columns go up and down together: they say almost the same thing. Then the model's plane is "poorly anchored": there are countless combinations of the two that predict almost equally well, and least squares picks one almost at random. The coefficients blow up and change from one week to the next, even though the predictions are still good.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Write the data as an arrow: one number per trip (or per row).
  2. Each column of the model is another arrow; together they make the line, plane or space of possible predictions.
  3. The best prediction is the shadow of the data on that space: the one that leaves the smallest sum of squares.
  4. The error ends up perpendicular to each column: dot product 0. That gives as many equations as columns, the normal equations, and they're solved without searching.
  5. Be wary of an odd data point: the squares amplify it.
  6. Measure on data set aside: adding columns always lowers the training error.
  7. Watch out for twin columns: drop one or use a brake (ridge) if you want coefficients that mean something.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Fuel use by load and grade

Liters per 100 km explained by load, route grade and speed: a baseline for spotting trucks or drivers that burn more than expected.

Calibrating a sensor

Fuel level sensor reading versus the actual liters from several fill-ups: a least squares line gives the correction (how much it scales and how much it shifts).

Tire and brake wear

Millimeters of tread or pad versus kilometers: the slope is the wear rate, and it tells you when it will hit the legal minimum.

Clock drift on a device

Difference between the device's clock and GPS time over several days: the slope is how many seconds it runs fast or slow per day, so you can correct the timestamps.

Connections

With the models of the ML series and the other topics

Linear and polynomial regression is exactly this shadow (a polynomial curve too: its columns are load, load², load³…). Gradient descent reaches the same solution step by step, which is useful when the data are huge. Logistic regression has no exact shadow and no closed formula: that's why it is solved in steps. Principal component analysis also projects, but it chooses the plane itself. The dot product and the shadow come from topic 2, and the idea that a table of columns is a transformation, from topic 3.

To be fair

Where it helps and where it misleads

Where it helps

  • An exact, unique solution, computed in an instant.
  • Easy-to-read coefficients: liters per tonne, millimeters per kilometer.
  • Works the same with 2 columns as with 200: the shadow is computed the same way.

Where it misleads

  • One odd data point pulls the line a lot.
  • The training error always improves with more columns, even useless ones.
  • With near-duplicate columns, the coefficients mean nothing, even if it predicts well.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Error (residual)
How far off the model is on one data point: actual value minus prediction.
Least squares
Choosing the model that makes the sum of the squared errors as small as possible.
Projection (shadow)
The point on a line or plane closest to an arrow. The leftover piece sits at a right angle.
Perpendicular (orthogonal)
At a right angle. Two arrows are perpendicular if their dot product is 0.
Normal equations
The equations that say "the error is perpendicular to each column". Their solution is the best line.
Column
One kind of data in the table (load, grade…), seen as an arrow with one number per row.
Collinearity
When two or more columns say almost the same thing and the model can't tell how to split the credit between them.
Ridge
Least squares with a small penalty on large coefficients, so they don't blow up.
