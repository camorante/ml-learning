Mean, variance and standard deviation

When you have a column full of numbers, the first thing to do is sum it up in two: where the center is and how far the values stray from it. It sounds easy, but there are traps: a single badly stored value can move the center, and there is a "divide by n − 1" that nobody explains. Here you'll see all of it with the trips of one truck.

The setup

A truck made 8 trips this week, and the telematics system stored its fuel consumption on each one, in liters per 100 kilometers (L/100 km): 32, 35, 30, 33, 37, 31, 34 and 32. Each orange dot in the drawing is a trip. We want two numbers: how much it normally burns and how much that changes from one trip to the next.

Imagine the thick line at the bottom is a stick, and each trip is a weight, all the same weight, hanging at its spot. The mean is the point where you would have to rest the stick so it doesn't tip. You can drag the dots (or use the slider) and watch everything change.

How to read the chart. Each row is a trip, and its orange dot sits at its consumption (the scale is at the bottom). The blue triangle under the stick and the vertical blue line mark the mean. The purple bars go from the mean to each trip: they are the deviations, how far each one strays, with its number at the end (+ if it burns more than the mean, − if it burns less). The light blue strip covers the mean ± one standard deviation. The green line is the median, the value with half of the trips on each side.

What you just saw

Two numbers to sum up a column

To sum up a column of data (here, the consumption of 8 trips) two numbers are used above all:

  • A center: the "typical" value. The most used one is the mean: add everything up and divide by how many there are. (32 + 35 + 30 + 33 + 37 + 31 + 34 + 32) / 8 = 264 / 8 = 33 L/100 km. Another center is the median: sort the values and keep the one in the middle (with 8 values, the mean of the two in the middle: 32 and 33 give 32.5).
  • A spread: how far the values stray from the center. The most used one is the standard deviation, which here is 2.27 L/100 km: any given trip strays from the mean by about 2 liters, sometimes more and sometimes less.

With those two numbers you can already say a lot: "this truck burns 33 ± 2.3". The five ideas that follow explain why the mean is that balance point, when it misleads, why the distances are squared, where the famous "divide by n − 1" comes from, and how to compare columns measured in different units.

Idea 1 of 5

The mean is the number that misses the least, counting squared

Imagine you have to bet one single number for the 8 trips: "this truck burns this much". On each trip you miss a little: the distance between your number and what it actually burned. If you count each error squared (multiplied by itself) and add them up, which number gives the smallest sum? Always, with any column of data, the mean. Move around and check it.

How to read the chart. At the top, each row is a trip: the dashed blue line is your number and the purple bar is how much you miss on that trip, with its squared error next to it. At the bottom, the curve tells how much the errors add up to for each number you could pick (the bottom scale is the same as the top one). The dot is your choice: try to bring it to the lowest point of the curve.

How I count the errors

Squared, the mean wins; as is, the median.

There is one calculation that explains it all: sum of squared errors = 36 + 8 × (distance to the mean)². The 36 is the minimum, what the squared errors add up to if you pick exactly the mean; and every step you move away is paid by each of the 8 trips. That's why the mean is also the balance point of the toy: there, what is extra on one side exactly makes up for what is missing on the other.

This idea, "the number that misses the least, squared", is the same one used by linear regression with a line instead of a number, the least squares of topic 4. The mean is the simplest model there is.

Idea 2 of 5

A bad record: the mean runs off, the median stays

Real telematics brings errors. A classic case: trip 5 burned 37 L/100 km, but when it was stored the decimal point of a "37.0" got lost and it became 370. Or the digits got swapped and it became 73. The mean adds up all the values, so a single absurd number drags it along. The median only looks at the order: the error goes to the end of the row, and the one in the middle is still the same.

How to read the chart. At the top, the 8 trips on a line; the ringed dot is trip 5, with the value that was stored. The scale widens so it fits. At the bottom, how the mean, the median and the trimmed mean (drop the highest and the lowest trip and take the mean of the rest) change with the stored value, from 37 (correct) to 370.

So should you always use the median? No: the mean uses all the values and has good properties you'll see in idea 4 and in the technical version. The practical thing is to compute both. If they are similar, fine; if they are far apart, something is odd (an error, or data heavily skewed to one side) and it's worth looking before going on. How to hunt for those odd values is told in outliers, inside "Preparing the data".

Idea 3 of 5

How far they stray: why squared, and the root at the end

To measure how far the trips stray from the center, the natural thing is to look at the deviations: each value minus the mean. But their mean is useless: the positive and negative ones cancel out and always add up to 0 (that's what makes the stick balance). You have to get rid of the sign. You can take the distance as is, but the usual thing is to square it: −3 × −3 = 9, no sign, and on top of that what is far away weighs more.

The mean of those squares is the variance. It has a problem: if the data are in L/100 km, the variance is in "L/100 km squared", a unit that means nothing. That's why at the end you take the square root, and out comes the standard deviation, in the same units as the data.

How to read the chart. One purple bar per trip, with its number on top. Depending on the button, the bar is the deviation with its sign (pointing up if it burns more than the mean), the distance without sign, or the squared deviation. The blue line is the mean of the bars; in "squared" mode, the dashed orange line is the sum divided by 7 instead of by 8 (idea 4 explains why).

Idea 4 of 5

Divide by n or by n − 1?

The 8 trips are a sample: what we really care about is the truck, all the trips it makes in a year (the population). With the sample we want to guess how much the truck varies. The problem: the distances are measured from the mean of the sample, and that mean is, by construction, in the center of the sample. The true mean of the truck is a bit further away, toward one side. Measured from the sample's own center, the distances come out a bit shorter than they really are.

How much shorter? Just enough that, dividing by n, the variance comes out on average (n − 1)/n of the true one: with 2 trips, half; with 8, seven eighths. Dividing by n − 1 instead of by n fixes it. It's called Bessel's correction. Here you can check it by drawing samples from a truck whose trips we all know.

How to read the chart. At the top, the truck's 400 trips of the year (gray dots) and their true mean (blue line). The orange dots are the last sample and the orange line, its mean. At the bottom, three bars: the true variance of the year, and the mean of all the variances computed from the samples, dividing by n (purple) or by n − 1 (green). The small vertical tick marks the truth on each bar.

When it matters: with lots of data, hardly at all (with 1,000 trips, dividing by 1,000 or by 999 is the same). With little data, a lot: with 3 trips, dividing by 3 underestimates the variance by a third. And watch out for tools, because they don't all do the same: some divide by n and others by n − 1 by default (the technical version has the list).

Idea 5 of 5

Comparing different columns: coefficient of variation and z

The same 8 trips have another column: the idling minutes (engine running with the truck stopped): 12, 25, 8, 15, 40, 10, 18 and 16. Mean 18 minutes, standard deviation 10.3 minutes. Does idling vary more than consumption? The standard deviations can't be compared, because one is in minutes and the other in L/100 km. Two tricks:

  • Coefficient of variation: the standard deviation divided by the mean. Consumption varies 6.9% of its mean (2.27 / 33); idling, 57% (10.3 / 18). Idling depends much more on the trip.
  • Standardize (compute the z): subtract the mean from each value and divide by the standard deviation. The result says how many standard deviations away from the mean it is, with no units. That way both columns are compared on the same scale.

How to read the chart. Two rows: consumption on top and idling below, each with its own scale. The blue tick is the mean of each row and the light blue strip, the mean ± one standard deviation. The chosen trip has a ring and its value. Press "standardized": both rows move to the same scale, the mean goes to 0 and the strip, from −1 to +1.

Standardizing is exactly what the scaling in preparing the features does: many models (the ones that measure distances, like nearest neighbors or k-means, or the ones trained with gradient descent) work poorly if one column goes from 0 to 40 and another from 30 to 37. A warning: the mean and standard deviation used to standardize are computed on the training data and saved, and then applied the same way to new data.

Putting it all together

The complete recipe

  1. The mean is adding up and dividing by how many there are: the balance point, and the number that misses the least counting the errors squared.
  2. The median is the middle value once sorted: the number that misses the least counting the errors as is. It doesn't get dragged along by an absurd value.
  3. Compute both. If they are far apart, there are errors or skewed data: look before going on.
  4. Deviations: each value minus the mean. They always add up to 0, so they get squared.
  5. The variance is the mean of those squares, dividing by n − 1 if the data are a sample. The standard deviation is its root: in the units of the data.
  6. To compare columns in different units: coefficient of variation (deviation / mean) or standardize, z = (value − mean) / standard deviation.
  7. With data that never stops arriving (telematics), the mean and the variance can be updated value by value, without storing everything, with a stable calculation (Welford's, in the technical version).

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telematics

Consumption of each truck

With thousands of trips a month, a few records arrive with the decimal point lost or the sensor stuck at zero. The median consumption per truck withstands those errors; the mean doesn't, and it can flag as a "gas guzzler" a truck that isn't one.

Idling that varies a lot

A high coefficient of variation in a driver's idling minutes says that the way they stop depends a lot on the day: more room for training than a driver who always leaves the engine running the same amount.

Fuel budget

The dollars of fuel per trip on a route have a mean and a standard deviation. With them you set a realistic budget and see which trips stand out: one at 3 standard deviations from the mean deserves a question.

Real-time summaries

The service that receives the GPS data can keep updating, for each truck, the mean and variance of the speed or the consumption with every message, without storing the history: three numbers per truck are enough.

Connections

With the models of the ML series and the other topics

The mean is the simplest model of linear regression (a flat line) and comes from the same idea as least squares. The centers in k-means are means of groups. Principal component analysis looks for the directions with the most variance, and the Kalman filter keeps track of the variance of its estimate at every step. The deviations of a column form a vector, and the standard deviation is its length divided by √(n − 1). In "Preparing the data", exploratory analysis starts with these summaries, outliers are hunted with them, and scaling is standardizing. The next topic, distributions, goes one step further: not just the center and the spread, but the whole shape of the data.

To be fair

What helps and where it misleads

What helps

  • Two numbers sum up thousands of values and are understood in any meeting: "33 ± 2.3 L/100 km".
  • Mean and variance can be computed value by value, and summaries of different chunks (days, trucks, servers) can be merged without reading the data again.
  • The standard deviation is in the units of the data and works as a ruler: standardizing puts all the columns on the same scale.

Where it misleads

  • A single absurd value drags the mean and, even more, the standard deviation. And with little data, that same value inflates the deviation so much that it doesn't look odd (idea 2).
  • Two numbers don't tell the shape: two kinds of route mixed together give a mean where there is no trip at all (button "two kinds of route").
  • The mean of ratios misleads: the mean of each trip's L/100 km is not the fleet's consumption if the trips have different lengths (the technical version computes it). And n versus n − 1 changes the result depending on the tool.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Mean
Add up all the values and divide by how many there are. The balance point.
Median
The middle value once sorted (with an even number of values, the mean of the two in the middle).
Deviation
How far a value strays from the mean: value minus mean. With its sign.
Variance
The mean of the squared deviations (dividing by n − 1 for a sample). In squared units.
Standard deviation
The square root of the variance: how far a value typically strays from the mean, in the units of the data.
Sample and population
The population is everything you care about (all the trips of the year); the sample, what has been measured (8 trips).
Bessel's correction
Dividing by n − 1 instead of by n when computing the variance of a sample, so it doesn't come out short on average.
Coefficient of variation
Standard deviation divided by the mean: spread as a percentage of the center, with no units.
Standardize (z)
(Value − mean) / standard deviation: how many standard deviations a value is from the center.
Trimmed mean
The mean after dropping a share of the highest and the lowest values. A middle ground between mean and median.
