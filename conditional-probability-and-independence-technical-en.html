Conditional probability and independence, for programmers

Joint, marginal and conditional probability on contingency tables (and from a DataFrame with pd.crosstab), the product and chain rules, total probability and trees, the step to Bayes and the fallacy of the transposed conditional, independence (and why mutually exclusive is not independent), the χ² test of independence, conditional independence and Naive Bayes, and Simpson's paradox. With a from-scratch module verified against pandas and SciPy, 200,000 simulated trips and a worked example by hand.

Summary

  • Conditioning on means restricting the sample space to and renormalizing. On a table of counts: dividing a cell by the total of its row or its column (crosstab(normalize="index") / "columns").
  • Product rule and total probability : they are the probability tree. Together they give Bayes.
  • in general; the ratio between them is . With a breakdown alert that catches 90% of breakdowns and 2% of trucks broken down, only 26.9% of alerts are breakdowns.
  • Independence: the joint table is the outer product of the marginals. Mutually exclusive with positive probability implies dependent. The χ² test compares the table with that product; with large , also look at the effect size (Cramér's V).
  • Independence doesn't imply conditional independence, nor the other way around. Naive Bayes assumes the features are conditionally independent given the class.
  • Simpson: an overall rate is an average weighted by the mix of groups; if the mix differs, the overall comparison can flip. Which table to look at is decided by the causal structure, not by the data.

§2 · With pandas

From a DataFrame to the table and its conditionals

pd.crosstab counts; its normalize argument chooses the denominator: "all" gives the joint, "index" divides each row by its total (conditional on the row variable) and "columns", each column (conditional on the column variable). A binary conditional is also a grouped mean: df.groupby("lluvia").alerta.mean().

tabla.py

import numpy as np, pandas as pd

# 1,000 trips: 200 with rain (50 with a hard-braking alert) and 800 without rain (80 with an alert)
filas = [(True, True)] * 50 + [(True, False)] * 150 + [(False, True)] * 80 + [(False, False)] * 720
df = pd.DataFrame(filas, columns=["lluvia", "alerta"]).sample(frac=1, random_state=0)

print(pd.crosstab(df.lluvia, df.alerta, margins=True, margins_name="total"))
print("-- all"); print(pd.crosstab(df.lluvia, df.alerta, normalize="all"))       # P(rain and alert)
print("-- index"); print(pd.crosstab(df.lluvia, df.alerta, normalize="index"))     # P(alert | rain): each row adds up to 1
print("-- columns"); print(pd.crosstab(df.lluvia, df.alerta, normalize="columns")) # P(rain | alert): each column adds up to 1
print("-- groupby"); print(df.groupby("lluvia").alerta.mean())  # same as the True column of 'index'
print(df[df.alerta].lluvia.mean(), df.alerta.mean(), (df.lluvia & df.alerta).mean())

arbol.py

from cond import prob_total, bayes

p_av = 0.02                     # P(breakdown this week)
sens, fa = 0.90, 0.05           # P(alert | breakdown), P(alert | no breakdown)
p_alerta = prob_total([p_av, 1 - p_av], [sens, fa])
post = bayes([p_av, 1 - p_av], [sens, fa])[0]
print("P(alert) =", round(p_alerta, 4), " P(breakdown | alert) =", round(post, 4))
# odds form: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio
o0, rv = p_av / (1 - p_av), sens / fa
o1 = o0 * rv; print("odds:", round(o0, 4), "×", rv, "=", round(o1, 4), "->", round(o1 / (1 + o1), 4))
# cost of inspecting every alert ($150) per breakdown found
print("$ per breakdown found:", round(150 / post, 1))
# second signal (vibration), independent of the first one GIVEN the truck's condition
s2, f2 = 0.80, 0.10
o2 = o0 * rv * (s2 / f2); print("with two signals:", round(o2, 4), "->", round(o2 / (1 + o2), 4))
# out of 1,000 trucks
print("of 1,000:", 1000 * p_av, 1000 * p_av * sens, 1000 * (1 - p_av) * fa)

§6 · Conditional independence

Independent given C, and Naive Bayes

and are independent given () if , or, with , : once you know , adds nothing about . Neither kind of independence implies the other:

  • Conditional without marginal: hard braking and speeding are independent within rookies and within veterans, but dependent across the fleet, because the type of driver influences both (common cause).
  • Marginal without conditional (“explaining away”, or Berkson's bias): a brake failure and a faulty sensor can be independent, but within the trucks that triggered an alert they become dependent: if the sensor was broken, the failure is less likely. Selecting data by a common effect creates dependencies.

§7 · Simpson

When the total contradicts every group

By total probability, a group's overall rate is the average of its per-stratum rates weighted by its own mix of strata:

If two depots have different weights , the order of the overall averages can reverse the order within each stratum. With the data from the simple version:

Depotcityhighwaytotalsame 50/50 mixlate × $30
North600/800 = 75%190/200 = 95%79%85%$6,300
South140/200 = 70%720/800 = 90%86%80%$4,200

The “same mix” column is a direct standardization: applying each depot's rates to a common reference mix. Which number is right? It depends on the causal structure (Pearl, 2014). Here the route type is a common cause (it determines which depot serves it and how hard it is to arrive on time): you have to stratify. If the variable were a mediator (something the depot itself causes, like choosing slower routes), stratifying would hide part of the effect. The classic case is the Berkeley admissions (Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell, 1975): apparent bias in the total, none department by department.

§8 · From scratch

Tables, conditionals, Bayes and χ², verified

The χ² p-value needs the survival function of the χ² distribution, which is the regularized upper incomplete gamma function: . It's computed with its series if and with a continued fraction (Lentz's method) otherwise, as in Numerical Recipes.

cond.py

"""Conditional probability and independence, from scratch (numpy and math only)."""
import math
import numpy as np


def tabla(a, b):
    """Contingency table: rows = values of a, columns = values of b (counts)."""
    fa, ia = np.unique(np.asarray(a), return_inverse=True)
    fb, ib = np.unique(np.asarray(b), return_inverse=True)
    T = np.zeros((len(fa), len(fb)), int)
    np.add.at(T, (ia, ib), 1)
    return T, fa, fb


def conjunta(T):
    """P(row = i and column = j): each cell divided by the total."""
    T = np.asarray(T, float); return T / T.sum()


def marginales(T):
    """P(row = i) and P(column = j): summing the joint over rows and over columns."""
    P = conjunta(T); return P.sum(1), P.sum(0)


def condicional(T, dado="filas"):
    """P(column | row) if dado='filas' (each row adds up to 1); P(row | column) if dado='columnas'."""
    T = np.asarray(T, float)
    return T / T.sum(1, keepdims=True) if dado == "filas" else T / T.sum(0, keepdims=True)


def prob_total(p_b, p_a_dado_b):
    """P(A) = sum_j P(B=j) P(A | B=j)."""
    return float(np.dot(p_b, p_a_dado_b))


def bayes(p_b, p_a_dado_b):
    """P(B=j | A) from P(B=j) and P(A | B=j): multiply and normalize."""
    num = np.asarray(p_b, float) * np.asarray(p_a_dado_b, float)
    return num / num.sum()


def esperada(T):
    """Counts there would be if rows and columns were independent: n * P(row) * P(column)."""
    T = np.asarray(T, float); return np.outer(T.sum(1), T.sum(0)) / T.sum()


def _q_gamma(a, x):
    """Regularized upper incomplete gamma Q(a, x) (series or Lentz continued fraction)."""
    if x <= 0:
        return 1.0
    lg = a * math.log(x) - x - math.lgamma(a)
    if x < a + 1:                                    # series for P(a, x)
        s = t = 1.0 / a; k = a
        while abs(t) > 1e-17 * abs(s):
            k += 1; t *= x / k; s += t
        return 1.0 - s * math.exp(lg)
    b = x + 1 - a; c = 1e300; d = 1 / b; h = d       # continued fraction for Q(a, x)
    for i in range(1, 10000):
        an = -i * (i - a); b += 2
        d = an * d + b; d = 1e-300 if abs(d) < 1e-300 else d
        c = b + an / c; c = 1e-300 if abs(c) < 1e-300 else c
        d = 1 / d; h *= d * c
        if abs(d * c - 1) < 1e-16:
            break
    return math.exp(lg) * h


def chi2(T, yates=False):
    """Independence test: statistic, degrees of freedom and p-value."""
    T = np.asarray(T, float); E = esperada(T); d = np.abs(T - E)
    if yates and T.shape == (2, 2):
        d = np.maximum(d - 0.5, 0)
    est = float((d**2 / E).sum()); gl = (T.shape[0] - 1) * (T.shape[1] - 1)
    return est, gl, _q_gamma(gl / 2, est / 2)


def cramer_v(T):
    """Effect size between 0 (independent) and 1: sqrt(chi2 / (n (min(r, c) - 1)))."""
    T = np.asarray(T, float); est = chi2(T)[0]
    return math.sqrt(est / (T.sum() * (min(T.shape) - 1)))

check.py · 200 random tables from 2×2 to 5×5 against pandas and SciPy

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
from scipy.stats.contingency import association
from cond import tabla, conjunta, marginales, condicional, esperada, chi2, cramer_v, bayes, prob_total

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
err = dict(tabla=0, conj=0, filas=0, cols=0, marg=0, esp=0, chi=0, p=0, yates=0, v=0, bayes=0)
for it in range(200):
    r, c = rng.integers(2, 6, 2); n = int(rng.integers(50, 5000))
    a = rng.integers(0, r, n); b = (a + rng.integers(0, c, n) * rng.integers(0, 2, n)) % c
    T, _, _ = tabla(a, b); X = pd.crosstab(a, b)
    err["tabla"] = max(err["tabla"], np.abs(T - X.values).max())
    err["conj"] = max(err["conj"], np.abs(conjunta(T) - pd.crosstab(a, b, normalize="all").values).max())
    err["filas"] = max(err["filas"], np.abs(condicional(T, "filas") - pd.crosstab(a, b, normalize="index").values).max())
    err["cols"] = max(err["cols"], np.abs(condicional(T, "columnas") - pd.crosstab(a, b, normalize="columns").values).max())
    pa, pb = marginales(T)
    err["marg"] = max(err["marg"], np.abs(pa - pd.Series(a).value_counts(normalize=True).sort_index().values).max())
    s = stats.chi2_contingency(T, correction=False)
    err["esp"] = max(err["esp"], np.abs(esperada(T) - s.expected_freq).max())
    e, g, p = chi2(T)
    err["chi"] = max(err["chi"], abs(e - s.statistic) / s.statistic); err["p"] = max(err["p"], abs(p - s.pvalue) / max(s.pvalue, 1e-300))
    err["v"] = max(err["v"], abs(cramer_v(T) - association(T, method="cramer")))
    if r == 2 and c == 2:
        s2 = stats.chi2_contingency(T, correction=True)
        err["yates"] = max(err["yates"], abs(chi2(T, yates=True)[0] - s2.statistic))
    # Bayes: P(row | column j) from P(row) and P(column j | row)
    j = 0; post = bayes(pa, condicional(T, "filas")[:, j])
    err["bayes"] = max(err["bayes"], np.abs(post - condicional(T, "columnas")[:, j]).max())
    err["bayes"] = max(err["bayes"], abs(prob_total(pa, condicional(T, "filas")[:, j]) - pb[j]))
for k, v in err.items():
    print(f"{k:6s} {v:.1e}")

§9 · In the fleet

200,000 simulated trips

Rain on 20% of trips, rookie drivers on 30% (independent of rain), a braking alert that depends on rain and driver (7, 17, 19 and 39%, chosen so that the 10% and 25% of the simple version come out) and speeding that depends only on the driver (30% rookies, 10% veterans).

sim.py

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from cond import chi2


def viajes(n=200_000, seed=9):
    """Simulated trips: rain (20%), rookie driver (30%, independent of rain),
    hard-braking alert depending on rain and driver, and speeding depending on the driver."""
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    lluvia = r.random(n) < 0.20
    novato = r.random(n) < 0.30
    p_alerta = np.select([~lluvia & ~novato, ~lluvia & novato, lluvia & ~novato], [0.07, 0.17, 0.19], 0.39)
    alerta = r.random(n) < p_alerta
    exceso = r.random(n) < np.where(novato, 0.30, 0.10)
    return pd.DataFrame(dict(lluvia=lluvia, novato=novato, alerta=alerta, exceso=exceso))

    T = pd.crosstab(g.alerta, g.exceso).values; e, gl, p = chi2(T)
    print(f"{nombre:9s} P(speed)={g.exceso.mean():.4f}  P(speed|alert)={g[g.alerta].exceso.mean():.4f}  chi2={e:8.1f}  p={p:.2g}")
# how much a conditional estimated from few cases moves: 500 trips, 1,000 times
r = np.random.default_rng(1); est, nB = [], []
for _ in range(1000):
    m = d.iloc[r.integers(0, len(d), 500)]; b = m[m.lluvia & m.novato]
    est.append(b.alerta.mean()); nB.append(len(b))
print("P(alert | rain, rookie) with 500 trips: mean", round(np.mean(est), 3), " sd", round(np.std(est), 3),
      " mean cases", np.mean(nB), " theoretical", round(np.sqrt(0.39 * 0.61 / 30), 3))

Three takeaways. The simulated conditionals recover the model's (0.100 and 0.250; ). Alert and speeding are clearly dependent across the fleet () and independent within each type of driver ( and ): conditional independence through a common cause. And a conditional on a small group is noisy: with 500 trips there are only about 30 with rain and a rookie, and has a standard deviation of 0.09, the binomial . Each added condition splits the data: multi-way tables empty out quickly, and that's why models (logistic regression, Naive Bayes) impose structure instead of counting cell by cell.

§10 · Checklist

Before you trust a conditional probability

  • It's clear what the denominator is: divides by the cases with (and in crosstab, the normalize axis is the right one).
  • The conditional hasn't been transposed: to go from to you use the base rates (Bayes).
  • The conditioning group has enough cases; report the interval or at least .
  • When comparing a conditional with the overall one, look at the size (difference, ratio, Cramér's V) and not just the p-value.
  • Expected counts ≥ 5 for the χ²; otherwise, Fisher.
  • You've looked for variables that explain both at once (type of driver, route type) and compared within their groups.
  • Before comparing totals between groups, you've checked that the mix is similar or you've standardized.
  • If a model assumes independence (Naive Bayes, binomial, Poisson), you've checked it at least with a table.

§11 · Worked example

Rain and braking alerts, by hand

The 1,000-trip table from the simple version, from start to finish: marginals, the three divisions of a cell, the product rule, total probability in a winter month, turning it around with Bayes, and the independence test.

The situation. The fleet operations manager sees that on trips with a hard-braking alert it rained 38.5% of the time, and wants to know three things: how much the risk of an alert goes up when it rains, how many alerts to expect in winter, and whether the relationship is real or could be chance.

Example map

1,000 fleet trips: it rained on 200, there was a hard-braking alert on 130, and both things happened on 50. From just those three numbers you can compute everything in this topic: marginals, the joint and both conditionals, the product rule, total probability in a rainier month, Bayes and the independence test.

  1. Complete the table and get the marginals.
  2. The same cell, three divisions.
  3. The product rule, both ways.
  4. Total probability: a winter month.
  5. Turning it around: Bayes.
  6. Independent? The χ² test.

  1. Complete the table and get the marginals

    In words

    With the total of each row and column and one cell, the rest of the table follows by subtraction. Then each total divided by 1,000 is a “marginal” probability (it's called that because it's written in the margin of the table).

    The data

    alertno alerttotal
    rain50150200
    no rain80720800
    total1308701,000

    Where do the numbers come from?

    They're the trips from the toy in the simple version: the telemetry flags an alert when deceleration goes over a threshold, and the rain comes from the weather report for the area the truck was driving through. The missing cells follow by subtraction: 150 = 200 − 50, 80 = 130 − 50 and 720 = 800 − 80; the totals, 800 = 1,000 − 200 trips without rain and 870 = 1,000 − 130 without an alert.

  2. The same cell, three divisions

    In words

    The 50 trips with rain and an alert can be divided by all trips, by the rainy ones or by the ones with an alert. Each division answers a different question.

    The math

    What does each symbol mean?

    is read “and” (both events at once). The bar is read “given that” or “if”: what comes after it is what you already know, and that's why it goes in the denominator. Without rain, the alert comes up on .

    What it means: if it rains, the alert goes from 10% to 25%: 2.5 times more likely. And among trips with an alert, 38.5% were in the rain, almost double the overall 20%. They're two different numbers for two different questions.

  3. The product rule, both ways

    In words

    The probability of “rain and alert” can be built in two ways: first the rain and then the alert given that it rained, or first the alert and then the rain given that there was an alert. Both have to give the same result.

    The math

    Why does it work?

    It's the definition of a conditional, rearranged: if , multiplying by gives . In counts: , the 200 cancels out.

  4. Total probability: a winter month

    In words

    In January it rains on 40% of trips, not 20%. If drivers drive the same way (the conditionals 25% and 10% don't change), how many alerts will there be? You add up the two paths of the tree that end in an alert.

    The math

    Where does each number come from?

    0.60 is , the trips without rain. The $6 is the assumed cost of reviewing each alert: a supervisor watches the camera video for about 10 minutes, at about $36 an hour. With 20% rain there were 130 alerts, .

    What it means: in winter there will be 23% more alerts (160 vs. 130) and $180 more in reviews for every 1,000 trips, without anyone driving worse. Before comparing January's alerts with July's, you have to take the mix into account.

  5. Turning it around: Bayes

    In words

    Suppose you only know what came before: rain on 20% of trips, an alert on 25% if it rains and on 13% overall. What share of the alerts come from rainy trips? It's going from P(alert | rain) to P(rain | alert). For short, = rain and = alert.

    The math

    What are odds?

    The odds are the probability that something happens divided by the probability that it doesn't: means “1 rainy trip for every 4 dry ones”. The 2.5 is the likelihood ratio: how many times more common the alert is with rain than without it (). Knowing there was an alert multiplies the odds of rain by 2.5. To go back to a probability: odds / (1 + odds).

    What it means: the same 38.5% as in step 2, now without looking at the cell: just from how often it rains and how much rain raises the alert. That's all there is to Bayes' theorem: the product rule read backwards.

  6. Independent? The χ² test

    In words

    If rain had no influence on alerts, each cell would have “its row total × its column total / 1,000” trips. You compare what's there with that, cell by cell, and add it up.

    The math

    Where do the 3.84 and the V come from?

    If they were independent, the χ² statistic of a 2×2 table would follow a χ² distribution with 1 degree of freedom (a 2×2 table with fixed totals has only one free cell: the other three follow by subtraction). That distribution goes above 3.84 only 5% of the time; 3.84 is , the square of the normal's 1.96 (topic 8). 31.83 is very far away: the probability of something like that by chance is . Cramér's V divides by to measure the strength of the relationship without the sample size getting in the way: 0 is independence and 1, a complete relationship.

    What it means: the relationship between rain and alerts isn't a coincidence, but it's moderate (V = 0.18): rain raises the alert from 10% to 25%, it doesn't decide it. With only 100 trips and the same proportions, χ² would come out at 3.18 and chance couldn't be ruled out.

ejemplo.py · reproduces the numbers above

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

T = np.array([[50, 150], [80, 720]])           # rows: rain yes / no; columns: alert yes / no
n = T.sum(); fil, col = T.sum(1), T.sum(0)
print("1. totals", fil, col, n, " P(rain) =", fil[0] / n, " P(alert) =", col[0] / n)
print("2. 50 over:", 50 / n, 50 / fil[0], round(50 / col[0], 4))
print("3. product:", 0.20 * 0.25, round(0.13 * (50 / 130), 4))
print("4. winter:", round(0.40 * 0.25 + 0.60 * 0.10, 4), " alerts per 1,000:", round(1000 * (0.40 * 0.25 + 0.60 * 0.10)),
      " × $6 =", round(1000 * (0.40 * 0.25 + 0.60 * 0.10)) * 6, "vs", 130 * 6)
print("5. Bayes:", round(0.20 * 0.25 / 0.13, 4), " odds", 0.2 / 0.8, "×", 0.25 / 0.10, "=", 0.25 * 2.5, "->", round(0.625 / 1.625, 4))
E = np.outer(fil, col) / n
c = (T - E) ** 2 / E
print("6. expected", E.tolist(), " terms", c.round(2).tolist(), " chi2 =", round(c.sum(), 2),
      " critical", round(stats.chi2.ppf(0.95, 1), 2), " p =", f"{stats.chi2.sf(c.sum(), 1):.1e}", " V =", round(np.sqrt(c.sum() / n), 3))

What would change in a real system

  • More variables at once: night, type of driver, road type. Before blaming the alerts on rain, compare within each group (§6, §7).
  • Unit of analysis: trips by the same truck or driver aren't independent of each other; the χ² assumes each row is an independent case.
  • Exposure: a rainy trip can be longer; alerts per 100 km are better than “trip with an alert, yes or no”.
  • Intervals: report with its interval (with 200 trips, a 95% Wilson interval from 0.20 to 0.31).
