Outliers

An outlier is a value that sits far away from the rest. Sometimes it is a mistake (a misplaced decimal point), and sometimes it is the most interesting part of the data (a truck climbing a mountain pass). Finding them is easy; the hard part is deciding which is which, and what to do with each one.

The setup

We have 40 trips with two numbers each: the load the truck was carrying (in tons) and its fuel use (liters per 100 km). The more load, the more fuel. We want a straight line that sums up that relationship so we can, for example, predict how much a truck carrying 20 tons will burn.

One of the trips, the one marked in red, actually used 33 L/100 km. But whoever typed it in by hand put the decimal point in the wrong place and wrote 330. With the control below you can move that value from the correct one (33) to the wrong one (330) and see what happens to the line.

How is the line computed?

  • Ordinary line: the one that passes as close as possible to all the points at once. It is what Excel or any other program gives you by default.
  • Robust line: similar, but it gives less weight to points that are very far away, so a single one cannot drag it around.
  • Remove the outlier: the ordinary line without the marked trip.

Try this, in order:

  1. With "Ordinary line", move the value from 33 to 330 and watch the line tilt and the prediction for 20 t drift away.
  2. Leave the value at 330 and switch to "Robust line": it barely moves.
  3. Try "Remove the outlier": it works here because we know it is a mistake. Ideas 1 and 5 explain why you can't always do that.

What you just saw

A single value can outweigh all the others

The ordinary line tries to make the sum of the squared distances between the points and the line as small as possible. Squaring means a point 300 units away weighs as much as 10,000 points 3 units away. That is why a single wrong value can bend the whole line. Robust methods put a limit on how much each point can weigh.

But the toy was easy because we knew the 330 was a mistake. In real data nobody tells you. Before deciding what to do, you have to find the outliers and, above all, understand why they are outliers.

Idea 2 of 5

Looking at one column: the fences

The simplest way to find outliers is to look at one column and set up two "fences": anything outside them is suspicious. The fences are placed based on the middle part of the data. You take the middle 50% of trips (without the lowest quarter or the highest quarter) and measure how wide that range is. The fences go that width times a factor below and above it. This is the method behind box plots.

How to read the chart. Each dot is a trip, placed by its fuel use (further right, more fuel); the height means nothing, it only spreads the dots out so they don't cover each other. The blue band is the middle 50%. The red lines are the fences: dots outside them turn red. Dots with a black outline are the real mistakes, so you can see which ones get caught and which ones don't.

Idea 3 of 5

When mistakes hide each other

Another common way to look for outliers is to measure how many "standard deviations" each value is from the mean and flag the ones beyond 3. The problem is that the mean and the deviation are computed from all the data, mistakes included. If there are many big mistakes, they inflate the deviation and no longer look so far away: they hide each other. This is called masking. The robust version uses the median (the middle value) and a deviation also computed with medians, which the mistakes cannot inflate.

How to read the chart. We start with the normal trips and add misplaced-decimal mistakes (fuel use of 250 to 400). Further right, more mistakes. Each line shows what percentage of those mistakes each method finds. The vertical line is the amount you pick below.

Idea 4 of 5

Unusual in the combination, not in each column

A trip carrying 20 tons is normal. A trip burning 24 L/100 km is normal. But a trip carrying 20 tons that burns 24 L is impossible: with that load it would burn much more. Looking at each column on its own, you will never find it. You have to check whether the trip fits the relationship between columns: how far it sits from the robust line, compared with how far trips usually stray.

How to read the chart. Each dot is a trip. The blue band is the zone where normal trips fall around the robust line. The red dots are the ones flagged by the chosen method. Idea 5 of 5

So what do I do with them?

Idea 4 flagged 26 trips: the 12 mistakes and 14 mountain trips that are real. There are several ways to go on, and each one changes the results. We measure three things: how much fuel use goes up for each ton (the slope of the line), the fleet's average fuel use, and how much more a truck burns in the mountains. The black tick on each bar is the true value.

Choose what to do with the flagged trips

  • Keep them: use the data as it is.
  • Delete the flagged ones: remove all 26 trips, mistakes and mountain trips together.
  • Fix the mistakes: investigate each one. The decimal point is fixed by dividing by 10, gallons are converted to liters, and the misreported load is left as a missing value. The mountain trips stay.
  • Robust method: touch nothing and use the robust line and the median.

Putting it all together

The complete recipe

  1. Search each column with fences or with the robust median method, not with the mean and standard deviation.
  2. Search the combinations too: trips that don't fit the relationship between columns.
  3. Investigate each case with its context: route, truck type, equipment, who recorded it. Is it physically possible?
  4. Fix the mistakes with a known cause (decimal point, units) and mark the ones you can't recover as a "missing value".
  5. Don't delete real rare cases: they are the ones that teach the most. Add the column that explains them (mountain route).
  6. If you can't review them one by one, use robust methods that limit how much each point weighs.
  7. Write down what you did and why, and repeat it the same way on new data.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Data typed in by hand

Fill-ups, loads or mileage that someone types: misplaced decimals, mixed-up units, extra digits.

Different routes and conditions

Mountains, snow, traffic jams or a refrigerated truck: rare but real cases that a model needs to know about.

Fraud or breakdown detection

Here the outlier is exactly what you are looking for: a fill-up bigger than the tank, fuel use that shoots up.

Fleet reports

A monthly average fuel use can shift by several liters because of a single wrong record.

Connections

With the models of the ML series

Linear regression and K-means are very sensitive to outliers because they use squared distances. Decision trees and XGBoost are not very sensitive to them in the input columns, but they are in what they predict. The Kalman filter throws out readings that don't fit what it expects. And the "isolation forest" in the technical version uses trees the other way around: to find the points that are easiest to separate.

To be fair

What it costs and what you gain

What you gain

  • Results that don't depend on a handful of mistakes.
  • You discover rare cases that explain things (routes, truck types).
  • You find flaws in how the data is recorded.

What it costs

  • No method can tell a mistake from a rare case: you need context.
  • The thresholds (fences, 3.5) are a judgment call.
  • Reviewing by hand doesn't scale: you have to turn what you learn into rules.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Outlier
A value very far from the others. It can be a mistake or a real but uncommon case.
Ordinary line (least squares)
The line that makes the sum of the squared distances to the points as small as possible.
Robust method
A method that a few extreme values cannot move much.
Quartiles
The values with 25% and 75% of the data below them. Between them lies the middle 50%.
Fences
Limits built from the quartiles; anything outside them is suspicious.
Masking
When many outliers inflate the deviation and hide each other.
Residual
How far a point is from the line.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need them to understand the idea:

fences: Q1 − k·(Q3 − Q1) and Q3 + k·(Q3 − Q1) (k = 1.5 usually) classic z = (x − mean) / standard deviation ( > 3 suspicious ) robust z = 0.6745 · (x − median) / MAD ( > 3.5 suspicious ) MAD = median of |x − median| robust line (Huber): points with a large residual weigh less, in proportion to how far away they are
