Missing data

Almost no real table is complete: a driver skips a field, a sensor stops sending, coverage drops out. What you do with those gaps can change your conclusions as much as any model. And the question that decides everything is not how much data is missing, but why it is missing.

The setup

A company makes 2,000 deliveries to three kinds of customer: large warehouses, small stores and construction sites. When the truck is done, the driver logs in an app how many minutes they waited before being unloaded. The company wants two numbers to negotiate with its customers: the average wait and the percentage of deliveries that waited more than an hour.

But in about 600 deliveries (30%) the driver logged nothing. Since the example is simulated, we do know the wait for every delivery, so we can compare each way of handling the gaps against the truth: an average of 43.4 minutes and 21% of deliveries over an hour.

How to read the chart. It is a histogram: each bar counts how many deliveries had a wait in that range of minutes. The purple part is the ones the driver logged; the gray part, the ones they didn't (in real life we wouldn't see these). The green line is the true average wait, and the blue one is what we compute with the chosen method. The dashed line marks one hour.

Decision 1 (reality picks it): why is data missing?

  • By chance: the app sometimes fails for no reason (coverage, battery). It happens equally to any delivery.
  • Because of the customer type: at small stores, which are usually quick, drivers almost never log it. More is missing where waits are short, but the customer type is visible in the table.
  • Because of the wait itself: when the wait was short, the driver doesn't bother to log it, whatever the customer. What decides whether a value is missing is the missing value itself.

Decision 2 (you pick it): what do we do with the gaps?

  • Drop them: compute using only the logged deliveries.
  • Fill with the average: put the average wait of the logged deliveries into every gap.
  • Fill by customer: in each gap, put the wait of another logged delivery of the same customer type, picked at random.

Try this, in order:

  1. "By chance" with each of the three methods: the average comes out right with all of them, but "fill with the average" gets the over-an-hour share wrong.
  2. "Because of the customer type": dropping and filling with the average are both wrong; filling by customer gets it right.
  3. "Because of the wait itself": no method gets it fully right. This is the hard case.

What you just saw

The three reasons a value goes missing

Statisticians name them with acronyms that are worth knowing, because they show up everywhere:

  • MCAR (missing completely at random): the gap has nothing to do with anything. Dropping rows does not bias the results; you only lose data.
  • MAR (missing at random, meaning "at random once you know something else"): the gap depends on columns you can see, like the customer type. You fix it by using those columns to fill in.
  • MNAR (missing not at random): the gap depends on the very value that is missing. The data in the table cannot fully fix it: you need outside information (ask, measure another way) or you assume something and check how much the result changes.

Looking only at the table, you cannot know for sure which of the three cases you are in. You have to ask how the data is produced: who logs it, when the sensor fails, what happens when there is no coverage.

And a rule from topic 1: any fill (the average, the values for each customer type) is computed using only the training data, and applied the same way to the exam (the data held back to check the model) and to real use.

Idea 2 of 5

Filling with the average flattens the data

Filling in is better than dropping, but not all fills are equal. Back to the waits, in the "because of the customer type" case. Besides the wait, each delivery has a number of pallets (the more pallets, the longer the unloading). A good fill should keep three things as they are in reality: the average wait, how much the waits vary, and the relationship between pallets and wait.

How to read the chart. Histogram of the 2,000 waits after filling: in purple, the logged ones; in orange, the made-up values for the gaps. The gray line is the shape of the true waits. A good fill leaves the total (purple + orange) close to the gray line.

Idea 3 of 5

Gaps over time: repeat, connect or leave

In a sensor's series (here, the fuel tank level every 5 minutes) gaps come in stretches: the device loses its connection for 40 minutes. There are two common ways to fill: repeat the last known value or draw a straight line from the last value before the gap to the first one after it. Which one works depends on what happened inside the gap.

Idea 4 of 5

Being missing is information too

The engine temperature sensor stops sending mostly when the truck's electrical system is failing. And a failing electrical system is a warning sign of breakdowns. So the gap itself is an alert: among the 2,000 trips, those without a temperature have a breakdown in the following month four times as often as those with one.

If you fill the gap with the average temperature and forget about it, you erase that clue. The fix is simple: besides filling, add a "temperature was missing" column (yes/no) so the model can use it.

How to read the chart. On the left, the percentage of trips with a breakdown in the next 30 days, depending on whether the temperature is missing or not. On the right, the score of three models that predict breakdowns: the percentage of times that, comparing a trip that ended in a breakdown with one that didn't, the model gives the higher risk to the breakdown. 50% is a coin flip and 100% is always right (in the technical version it is called AUC). It is measured on trips the model did not see while learning.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Count the gaps per column and per combination of columns.
  2. Find out why they are missing: who or what produces the value and when it stops doing so.
  3. Compare rows with and without gaps: if they differ in other columns (customer type, vehicle age), the gaps are not random.
  4. Don't fill with the average blindly: it flattens variation and relationships.
  5. Fill using related columns (by group, by prediction) and keep the variation.
  6. Add a "was missing" column when the gap could be a clue.
  7. In time series, choose the fill based on what could have happened inside the gap, and don't fill gaps that are too long.
  8. Learn every fill from the training data only.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Data the driver logs

Waits, incidents, loads: people log what stands out and forget the routine. It is almost always the hard case.

Areas without coverage

Tunnels, valleys, rural areas: gaps cluster on certain routes, which are not like the others.

Failing sensors

A sensor that stops sending can be the first symptom of a breakdown: the gap is a signal.

Different devices

Trucks with older devices don't send some columns: the gap depends on the device model, which is known.

Connections

With the models of the ML series

XGBoost and boosted trees accept gaps directly: at each question they decide which side to send the trips with no value to, and so they use the gap as a clue. Regression and k nearest neighbors don't accept them: you have to fill first. In fact, k nearest neighbors can also be used to fill: each gap takes the average value of the most similar trips. And the Kalman filter fills gaps over time by predicting from what it knows about the motion.

To be fair

What it costs and what you gain

What you gain

  • You use all the available data, not just the complete rows.
  • You avoid conclusions biased by who leaves the gaps.
  • The gap itself can improve predictions.

What it costs

  • When the gap depends on the value itself, there is no perfect fix.
  • A filled-in value is not a measured value: you need to know which ones they are.
  • Complex fills must be kept identical in production.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Impute
Fill a gap with an estimated value.
MCAR
The value is missing by pure chance.
MAR
The value is missing because of something we can see in other columns.
MNAR
The value is missing because of its own value.
Complete-case analysis
Using only the rows without any gap.
Indicator column
A yes/no column that says whether the value was missing.
Multiple imputation
Filling several times in different ways and combining the results, to know how much uncertainty the gaps add.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need it to understand the idea:

complete rows with k columns and a share p of gaps in each (at random): (1 − p) to the power k e.g.: 8 columns at 5% → 0.95⁸ = 66% filling with the average: the average doesn't change, but the variation shrinks in proportion to the gaps (with 30% gaps, ~16% less standard deviation) multiple imputation (m fills): estimate = average of the m estimates uncertainty = average of the uncertainties + (1 + 1/m) · variation between fills
