Derivatives, gradient and the chain rule

A model learns by making small adjustments to its numbers and keeping the ones that lower the error. The derivative tells you which way to adjust before you try it, the gradient tells you that for all the numbers at once, and the chain rule lets you compute it even when the model has millions of pieces chained together.

The setup

A truck's GPS records at every moment how many kilometers it has covered. In half an hour it leaves the city, gets on the highway at 90 km/h, runs into a traffic jam and speeds up again. The curve below is that position, minute by minute.

The question: how fast is it going at one exact instant? With two GPS readings you can compute the average speed between them: kilometers covered divided by minutes elapsed. But that is an average; if the truck slows down between the two readings, the average does not tell you what the speedometer shows. The idea of the derivative is to bring the two readings as close together as possible and see which number the calculation approaches.

At the bottom, the speed at each minute: the orange dot is what the speedometer shows and the purple segment is the average speed between the two readings.

What you just saw

The derivative: the slope of the curve at a point

The average speed between two readings is how much the position changes divided by how much time passes: the slope of the line joining the two points. The closer the readings, the less time the truck has to change speed between them, and the calculation approaches a fixed number: the speed at that instant. That number it approaches is the derivative of the position. It is what the speedometer shows.

  • A curve seen up close looks like a straight line. The derivative is the slope of that line: the tangent.
  • It is a rate of change. "For every extra minute, so many extra kilometers." It works for any pair of quantities: dollars per km/h, liters per tonne, error per change in one of the model's numbers.
  • Its sign tells you which way the curve goes. Positive derivative: it goes up; negative: it goes down; zero: it is flat (at a peak or in a valley).

That last point is what machine learning uses. A model has adjustable numbers and an error that depends on them. The derivative of the error with respect to each number tells you whether raising that number makes things worse or better, and by how much. The ideas that follow go from speed to acceleration (1), the rules for computing derivatives without taking limits (2), the minimum where the derivative is zero (3), the gradient with several numbers at once (4), the chain rule (5), how a neuron learns backwards (6) and what happens when the chain is very long (7).

Idea 1 of 7

The derivative of speed: harsh braking

If the derivative of position is speed, the derivative of speed is acceleration: how many km/h the truck gains or loses each second. When it is very negative it is harsh braking, and telematics counts it as an alert (here, below −3 m/s²: losing about 11 km/h in one second).

The GPS gives the speed once per second, so acceleration is computed as in the toy: the difference in speed between two readings, divided by the time between them. How far apart should they be? Readings far apart average out the braking and hide it. Readings very close together react to every jitter of the GPS.

Idea 2 of 7

Rules so you don't take the limit every time

Bringing two points together until they almost touch always works, but for curves written as a formula there are shortcuts that give the exact derivative. With a few rules you can differentiate almost everything a model uses:

  • Powers: the derivative of x² is 2x; of x³, 3x². In general, the exponent comes down in front and you subtract one from it.
  • Sums and fixed numbers: the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives; a number that multiplies stays; a number that is added disappears (it changes nothing).
  • The exponential eˣ is its own derivative: it grows at a rate equal to its height. The logarithm has derivative 1/x: it grows more and more slowly.
  • The sigmoid, the S-shaped curve that turns any number into a percentage (the one in logistic regression), has a very handy derivative: p × (1 − p), where p is its height. It never goes above 0.25.

How to read the chart. At the top, the chosen function (blue) and its tangent at the orange point. At the bottom, the derivative: for each x, the slope of the upper curve at that x. The lower dot sits at the height of the slope of the tangent above.

Idea 3 of 7

The minimum is where the derivative is zero

What speed is best on the highway? Faster burns more diesel (air drag grows with the square of the speed). Slower costs more driver and truck time. Per 100 km, in this example:

cost = diesel ($1.60/L × (20 + 0.0016 × speed²) liters) + time (cost per hour × 100 / speed)

To the left of the minimum, going faster makes it cheaper (negative derivative); to the right, more expensive (positive derivative). Right at the bottom of the valley, the tangent is flat: zero derivative. That is why, to find the minimum of something, you look for where its derivative is zero.

How to read the chart. The blue curve is the total cost per 100 km; the dashed ones are its two parts: diesel (rises with speed) and time (falls). The black line is the tangent at the speed you choose: its slope is the derivative. The green line marks the cheapest speed.

Idea 4 of 7

With two numbers: the gradient, the uphill arrow

A model almost never has just one number. The line from topic 4, which gives fuel use from the load over 10 trips, has two: the height (fuel use when empty) and the slope (extra liters per 10 tonnes). Its error, the sum of squared errors, depends on both: it is a terrain with a valley.

You can differentiate by looking at one number at a time and holding the other still: "if I raise the height a little, how much does the error change?". Those are the partial derivatives. Together they form an arrow, the gradient, which points where the error rises fastest. To go down, you walk the opposite way: that is the gradient descent of the ML series. Here, what matters is where the arrow comes from.

Idea 5 of 7

The chain rule: rates multiply

Speed changes the diesel burned per hour, and diesel changes the spending in dollars. If each extra km/h burns 0.51 more liters per hour, and each liter costs $1.60, each extra km/h costs 0.51 × 1.60 = $0.81 more per hour. When one thing depends on another, which depends on another, the rates of change multiply. That is the chain rule.

It sounds obvious, and it is the key piece of deep learning: a neural network is a chain of simple calculations, and the derivative of the error with respect to any number inside it comes from multiplying the rates of each link.

Idea 6 of 7

How a neuron learns: the chain, backwards

A toy neuron predicts whether a delivery will arrive late from the number of traffic jams the truck has hit in the first half of the route. It has two adjustable numbers, a weight w and a bias b, and does four calculations in a row: it multiplies jams by weight, adds the bias, passes the result through the sigmoid (out comes a probability p) and computes the penalty: minus the logarithm of the probability it gave to what happened, the log loss from topic 14 and topic 15.

Forward, the values are computed from left to right. Backward, the derivatives of the penalty are computed from right to left: each box multiplies the derivative that reaches it by its own rate and passes it to the previous one. That is backpropagation: the chain rule applied from the end. With it you know, in a single pass, how much to adjust each number.

How to read the chart. Each box is a calculation. The big blue number is its value (forward). The orange line below it is the derivative of the penalty with respect to that value (backward): how much the penalty would change if that value went up a little. Above each arrow, in orange, the rate of that link, which is what gets multiplied on the way back.

Idea 7 of 7

Very long chains: gradients that vanish or explode

A deep network chains many layers, and on the way back the derivative is multiplied by a rate at each one. If the rates are below 1, the product shrinks at full speed: with the sigmoid, whose rate never goes above 0.25, ten multiplications take it below one millionth. The first layers barely get any signal and do not learn: the gradient vanishes. If the rates are above 1, the product grows out of control and the learning steps become huge: the gradient explodes.

That is why modern networks use pieces with a rate close to 1 (like the ReLU, which is 1 when it is active), connections that skip layers and gradient clipping.

How to read the chart. Each bar is a layer, from the last (on the right, where the error is computed) to the first (on the left). Its height is the size of the derivative that reaches it, on a logarithmic scale: each horizontal line is 10,000 times the one below. The green band is the zone where learning works well.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Derivative = rate of change: how much one thing changes when the other changes a tiny bit. It is the slope of the curve seen up close.
  2. With separate data points (GPS readings), it is computed with differences between nearby readings; not so far apart that they hide what happens, not so close that they amplify the noise.
  3. With formulas, you use the rules: powers, sums, exponential, logarithm, sigmoid.
  4. Minimums and maximums: where the derivative is zero.
  5. With many numbers, one derivative per number: the gradient points uphill; the model takes steps the opposite way, neither too short nor too long.
  6. Chained calculations: the rates multiply (chain rule).
  7. Backpropagation: forward, the values are computed; backward, by multiplying rates, the derivative of the error with respect to every number, all in one pass.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telematics

Harsh braking and acceleration

Harsh-driving alerts are derivatives: acceleration from the GPS speed or from the accelerometer. The spacing between readings and the noise filtering decide how many alerts come out.

Economical cruising speed

With the cost of diesel and of time as a curve, the cheapest cruising speed is the one where the derivative is zero. If diesel goes up or the customer pays to arrive sooner, the optimum moves.

Arrival-time models

The networks that estimate arrival time from traffic, route and driver learn by backpropagation: thousands of weights adjusted with the gradient of their error.

Cost sensitivity

"How much does monthly spending go up if diesel goes up 10 cents?" is a chained derivative: liters per km × km per month × $0.10. It is used for budgets and rates.

Connections

With the models of the ML series and the other topics

Gradient descent walks against the arrow of idea 4; linear regression and logistic regression are trained with that gradient (logistic regression has no closed formula, so it needs it no matter what). XGBoost uses the derivative of the error and also the derivative of the derivative (the curvature) to decide each tree. The Kalman filter, when the motion is not a straight line, uses derivatives to approximate it by straight lines at each step, just like the toy's zoom.

In this collection, the line and the valley of idea 4 are the ones from topic 4; the curvature of the valley is measured with the eigenvalues of topic 5; the peak of the likelihood in topic 14 is found with a zero derivative, and the neuron's penalty is the cross-entropy of topic 15, whose derivative comes out as simple as "probability minus what happened".

To be fair

What helps and where it misleads

What helps

  • A single idea, the rate of change, works for speeds, costs and models with millions of numbers.
  • The gradient tells you which way to improve without blindly trying every combination.
  • Backpropagation computes all the derivatives of a network in one backward pass, at a cost similar to computing the prediction.

Where it misleads

  • The derivative is local: it tells you what happens with small changes. With big nudges (+20 km/h) the chain falls short.
  • A zero derivative is not always the best point: it can be a local minimum, a maximum or a plateau.
  • With noisy data, differentiating amplifies the noise; with readings far apart, it hides what happened.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Derivative
The rate of change of one quantity with respect to another at a point: the slope of the curve seen up close.
Tangent
The line that touches the curve at a point with the same slope.
Limit
The number a calculation approaches when something (here, the spacing between readings) gets smaller and smaller.
Acceleration
The derivative of speed: km/h gained or lost per second (or m/s²).
Partial derivative
The derivative with respect to a single number, with the others held still.
Gradient
All the partial derivatives together: an arrow pointing where the function rises fastest.
Step size
How far you move against the gradient in each learning step. Too small: slow; too big: it overshoots.
Chain rule
If one thing depends on another that depends on another, their rates of change multiply.
Backpropagation
Computing the derivatives of a network from back to front, applying the chain rule at each calculation.
Vanishing gradient
A derivative that becomes tiny after multiplying many rates below 1: the first layers stop learning.
