Overfitting and regularization

A big network with few examples ends up learning them by heart, noise included, and fails on new ones. In topic 6 you saw it as a curve that went back up. Here we see why it happens and the tools to put a brake on it: stopping in time, keeping the weights small, switching neurons off at random, starting with weights of the right size and normalizing inside the network.

The setup

We want to predict a truck’s fuel consumption on a trip (in liters per 100 km) from its average speed. In town it burns a lot because of the stops; around 46 km/h it burns the least; on the highway it goes back up because of the air. To measure consumption precisely, the maintenance shop fitted a flow meter for a few weeks: there are only 20 measured trips to train on. And every measurement carries noise: the load, the wind and the driver change one trip’s consumption by about 2 liters.

The network is big for so little data: 1 input, two layers of 64 tanh neurons and 1 output, 4,353 numbers to fit. Another 30 validation trips are kept aside to judge it on data it hasn’t seen.

Nothing is trained in your browser here: we trained the network for real 25 times, 3,000 epochs each, once for each combination of the two brakes, always from the same starting weights. You choose which one to see and whether it stops in time.

Try this, in order:

  1. No brakes and no stopping: the network passes almost right over every training trip (error 0.12) and makes strange bends between them. On the validation trips it is off by 4.34 liters.
  2. Turn on “Yes, at the best epoch”: the best validation was at epoch 328, with 2.01. Look at what the curve was like then.
  3. Go back to “No” and raise the brake on weights to 1: without stopping, it ends at 2.26 and the curve comes out smooth.
  4. Lower the brake to 0 and switch off 30% of the neurons: it ends at 2.40.
  5. Combine brake 1 and 20% dropout: 2.01 with no need to stop.
  6. Now overdo it: brake 3 and 50% dropout. The network can no longer bend: it comes out almost straight and is off by 2.54 liters even on the training trips (2.86 on the validation trips).

What you just saw

Memorizing the exam versus understanding the subject

The network with no brakes hasn’t learned the shape of the consumption: it has learned the 20 trips, each with its noise. If a trip burned 2 extra liters because it was loaded and had a headwind, the network bends the curve to pass through it. That is overfitting: the training error keeps going down, while the validation error hits bottom and goes back up. You already saw it with polynomials in polynomial regression; a network, with thousands of numbers, can memorize much more.

Everything that holds it back is called regularization. No tool is free: a brake that is too strong leaves the network rigid, and it gets things wrong even on what it has seen.

And a warning: we chose by looking at the 30 validation trips, so that score is already a little “used up”. The real score comes from another 200 test trips that are looked at only once, at the end (explained in Split before you look): with the chosen recipe, 2.33 liters, when the true curve would give 2.18 on those same trips.

The ideas that follow: bias and variance (1), more data (2), stopping in time (3), small weights (4), switching neurons off (5), starting with the right weights (6) and batch normalization (7).

Idea 1 of 7

Too rigid or too jumpy

Imagine we repeat everything with another 20 trips: another flow-meter campaign, other days, other noise. A good model should come out similar each time and close to the truth. There are two ways to fail:

  • Bias: the model is so rigid it can’t take the true shape. A straight line will never draw a U. It is always wrong in the same way, with any sample.
  • Variance: the model is so flexible it copies the noise of each sample. Every campaign gives a different curve.

Idea 2 of 7

More data: the best brake of all

Memorizing 20 trips with 4,353 numbers is easy. Memorizing 300 is not: to pass through all of them it would have to make so many bends that it’s cheaper to learn the true shape. That’s why, when a network overfits, the first question is whether you can get more examples.

Idea 3 of 7

Stopping in time

At the start of training, the network learns the broad strokes: the U shape. Later, since there is nothing general left to learn, it starts learning what is particular to each trip: the noise. Early stopping takes advantage of that: the validation error is measured after every epoch, a copy of the weights is saved every time it improves, and at the end we keep the best copy.

When should training end? You don’t know whether the curve will come back down, so you wait a number of epochs without improvement, the patience, and then stop.

Idea 4 of 7

Small weights, smooth curves

To bend the curve sharply and pass through an odd trip, the network needs large weights: a neuron with large weights goes from −1 to +1 in very little space. Weight decay charges for having large weights: at every step, besides lowering the error, each weight shrinks a tiny bit toward zero. Only the weights that really lower the error on many trips survive large.

It is also called L2 regularization, because it is like adding to the error the square of the weights multiplied by a number λ that you choose.

Idea 5 of 7

Switching neurons off at random

Dropout is a curious idea: at every training step, each hidden neuron is switched off with some probability, here 30%. The ones left on have to manage without the others, so none of them can rely on one particular partner to memorize an odd trip. It is like a shop crew where some people are missing at random every day: everyone ends up able to do everything.

At every step a different “thinned” network is trained. When it is used, they are all switched on, and the full network behaves almost like the average of all the thinned ones. So that the sums don’t change size, during training what the active neurons output is multiplied by 1/0.7 ≈ 1.43: “inverted dropout”.

How to read the chart. On the left, the 128 hidden neurons (64 in each layer, in two 8 × 8 squares): the ones on in purple, the ones off in gray. On the right, what this step’s thinned network predicts (orange), those of the earlier steps (gray) and the full network, with all its neurons (green, thick). It is the toy network trained with 30% dropout and no brake.

Idea 6 of 7

Starting with weights of the right size

Before training, the weights are drawn at random. How big? Each neuron adds up many inputs multiplied by its weights. If the weights are too small, the signal shrinks at every layer until it disappears; if they are too large, it grows until it saturates the tanh units (which get stuck at ±1 and stop learning, as you saw in topic 2) or until it explodes with ReLU. With a 2-layer network you barely notice; with 20, it is everything.

The rule is to draw weights with a spread that depends on how many inputs each neuron has: the Xavier (or Glorot) rule for tanh and the He rule for ReLU, which makes up for ReLU switching off half the signal. It isn’t exactly a brake on overfitting, but without it the network doesn’t train well, and that’s why it is covered in this topic.

Starting weights

Small: standard deviation 0.01. Large: standard deviation 1. Xavier and He depend on the number of inputs of each neuron: with 256, between 0.06 and 0.10.

Idea 7 of 7

Batch normalization

Batch normalization corrects the size of the signal at every layer instead of trusting the starting weights: for each neuron, it takes the values it gives for all the examples in the batch, subtracts their mean and divides by their standard deviation. That way, each layer always receives numbers of a similar size, a larger learning rate can be used and the network trains faster. Since the batch mean changes a little from batch to batch, it adds some noise, and that slightly holds back overfitting.

There is a catch: when the network is used on a single trip, there is no batch to take a mean from. So, while it trains, it keeps a running average of the batches’ means and standard deviations, and when it is used (evaluation mode) it uses those instead of the batch’s own.

Putting it all together

The recipe against overfitting

  1. Split training, validation and test before looking at anything.
  2. Get more data if you can: it is the brake that never gets in the way.
  3. Initialize well: Xavier with tanh, He with ReLU (libraries do this by default, or nearly).
  4. Train while watching both curves and save the weights from the best validation epoch.
  5. Try brakes: weight decay and dropout, from less to more, and keep whatever validates best. If the training error shoots up, you’ve gone too far.
  6. Don’t trust small differences: with 30 trips, the validation score is very noisy.
  7. Look at the test set only once, at the end, and report that number.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Expensive labels

GPS data is plentiful, but the exact consumption, the cause of a breakdown or whether a hard brake was dangerous has to be measured or reviewed by someone. With hundreds of labeled examples and networks with thousands of weights, overfitting is the norm, not the exception.

Rare breakdowns

A breakdown model sees few breakdowns of each kind. With no brake, it learns that “truck 1043 in March” is a breakdown, instead of learning which readings announce one. Early stopping and weight decay are almost mandatory.

Validating on new trucks

If trips from the same truck land in both training and validation, the network can recognize the truck instead of learning the physics, and validation comes out too good. Split by truck or by date.

The forgotten evaluation mode

A classic mistake when putting a network into production: leaving it in training mode. Dropout keeps switching neurons off and normalization uses each batch’s statistics, so the same truck gets different predictions each time.

To be fair

What it gives you and what to watch out for

What it gives you

  • Big networks that work with little data, if they are braked well.
  • Early stopping is free: you only have to save the best weights.
  • Weight decay and dropout are added with one line of code.
  • Good initialization and batch normalization make very deep networks trainable.

What to watch out for

  • Too much brake leaves the network rigid: bias instead of variance.
  • Each brake has its own number (λ, the percentage, the patience) and it has to be tried on validation.
  • Choosing by looking at validation a lot also overfits to validation: that’s why the test set is kept apart.
  • Dropout and batch normalization behave differently in training and in use: you have to switch modes.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Overfitting
Learning the training examples by heart, noise included, instead of the general shape.
Regularization
Anything that holds back overfitting.
Capacity
How flexible a model is: how many different shapes it can take.
Bias
The error of a model that is too rigid, which is wrong in the same way with any sample.
Variance
How much the model changes from one sample to another because it copies its noise.
Early stopping
Keeping the weights from the epoch with the lowest validation error.
Patience
Epochs in a row without improving that you wait before stopping.
Weight decay (L2)
Shrinking all the weights a little at every step.
Dropout
Switching neurons off at random at every training step.
Initialization
How the weights are drawn before training (Xavier, He).
Batch normalization
Subtracting the batch mean and dividing by the batch standard deviation inside each layer.
Evaluation mode
How the trained network is used: without switching neurons off and with the saved averages.
Test set
Data that is looked at only once, at the end, to give the real score.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don’t need it to understand the idea. w is a weight, g its slope, η the learning rate and λ the brake:

weight decay: w ← w − η·λ·w − (Adam step with g) like adding to the error (λ/2)·(sum of all w²) dropout (p): in training h ← h · m / (1 − p) with m = 0 (prob. p) or 1 in use h ← h (mean of m/(1 − p) = 1) stopping: save the weights if the validation error is the lowest so far; stop after "patience" epochs without improving Xavier (tanh): weights with std ≈ gain · √(2/(inputs + outputs)) He (ReLU): weights with std √(2/inputs) normalize: x' = (x − batch mean)/batch std in use: with the running averages saved in training the toy: 20 trips, network of 4,353 numbers, 3,000 epochs, AdamW with rate 0.01 no brakes: training 0.12 validation 4.34 L/100 km best epoch 328: validation 2.01 brake 1 + dropout 20%: 2.01 test (200 trips, once): 2.33 the true curve: 2.18
