Recurrent networks and LSTM

A trip is not a photo: it is one reading after another, and each one makes sense because of the ones that came before. A recurrent network reads like that, step by step, and carries a memory that sums up what it has seen. The simplest memory fades in a few seconds. The LSTM adds a conveyor belt and three gates that decide what to keep, and it remembers a braking 10 seconds later as if it were happening now.

The setup

The truck's accelerometer measures, 10 times per second, how it accelerates forward, in m/s². Below zero, the truck is braking. Each segment starts with a start of 16 readings (1.6 s) in which the truck brakes, sometimes hard and sometimes gently. Then comes a gap: seconds of normal driving with vibrations, gentle braking, potholes and speed-ups.

At the end of the segment, and only then, we ask the network: "was there a harsh brake at the start?". It is a memory test. The network reads the readings one at a time, with no way to go back, so it has to carry the memory with it through the whole gap.

Two networks with 16 neurons compete: an RNN, the simplest recurrent network, and an LSTM, the one with a conveyor belt and gates. For each gap length we really trained them on 2,000 segments, and we test them on 1,000 new segments, half with a harsh brake and half without.

What you just saw

Reading one at a time, with a memory that travels along

A recurrent network is a single layer of neurons applied once per reading. At each step it receives two things: the new reading and its own output from the previous step. That output it passes to itself is its memory, the hidden state: a handful of numbers that sums up everything read so far. The weights are the same at every step, so the network works for segments of 16 readings or of 10,000.

The network in topic 8 looked at something else: a fixed window of readings, with a filter that slides across it. It sees short shapes very well, but not what happened outside its window. The recurrent network has no window: its memory can reach as far back as it lasts without fading.

The problem is that the RNN's memory fades. The ideas that follow: why a single neuron forgets (1), why training the RNN to remember is almost impossible (2), how the LSTM fixes it with its conveyor belt and its gates (3), and what these networks are used for when a short memory is enough (4).

Idea 1 of 4

A neuron that reads itself, and forgets

The simplest memory is a single neuron. At each step it adds up the new reading, multiplied by one weight, and its own previous value, multiplied by another weight w; then it squashes the result between −1 and 1 with the hyperbolic tangent from topic 2. The weight w says how much of its memory it keeps at each step.

Here you set the weights. We give the neuron two starts, one with a harsh brake and one with a gentle brake, followed by the same 5 s gap. If the memory is different in the two cases at the end, the neuron remembers what happened at the start.

Idea 2 of 4

Unrolled: the error has to travel back step by step

A recurrent network is trained like any other: with the backpropagation from topic 5. To see it, you "unroll" it: you draw one copy of the neuron per reading, all with the same weights, chained together. A segment of 66 readings is a network with 66 layers.

At the end, the network makes a mistake, and the error has to travel back, step by step, to the reading that held the answer. At each step it is multiplied by a number: the memory weight times the slope of the tangent. If that number is less than 1, the error shrinks at every step, like the vanishing gradient from topic 2; if it is greater than 1, it grows out of control and explodes.

How to read the chart. At the top, the unrolled network. At the bottom, for each reading of the segment, how much of the final error reaches it, really measured on 200 segments (1 is what reaches the last reading). The axis is logarithmic: each line means dividing by 10, 10,000 or 10 billion, depending on the scale. In gray the RNN and in orange the LSTM. The green band is the start, where the answer is.

When do we measure it?

Before training is what matters: if the error does not reach the start at the beginning, the network never learns to look at it.

no gap10 s

When the error explodes, the remedy is simple: gradient clipping. If the correction comes out too big, it is shrunk to a maximum size before applying it. In the technical version we train an RNN with a memory that amplifies: without clipping, one outsized step ruins it and it gets stuck at 49% accuracy; with clipping, it reaches 99%. Against the vanishing error, though, no clipping helps: you need a different kind of network.

Idea 3 of 4

The LSTM: a conveyor belt and three gates

The LSTM (long short-term memory) separates two things. The conveyor belt is the real memory: a number that passes from step to step without being squashed, like goods riding a conveyor belt. And three gates, each a number between 0 and 1 that the network computes at every step from the reading and from what it shows:

  • the forget gate: how much of the belt is kept (1 = all of it);
  • the input gate: how much of the new stuff is written onto the belt;
  • the output gate: how much of the belt is shown to the outside.

The belt is updated by adding: what was there, times the forget gate, plus the new stuff, times the input gate. If the forget gate is 1, the memory passes through intact, and so does the error that travels back during training. Here is an LSTM with a single neuron, really trained with 2.5 s gaps: 18 numbers in total. It gets 99.8% of the test segments right; the one-neuron RNN, 51.1%.

What this neuron has learned is a switch. While the truck does not brake hard, it slowly writes positive numbers and the belt goes up. When it brakes hard, it writes negative ones, the belt drops below zero, and then the input gate closes on its own: nothing that comes later can erase the memory. With the forget gate fixed at 0.9, the same neuron gets only 556 of 1,000 segments right.

The LSTM has a lighter cousin, the GRU, with two gates instead of three and no separate belt. In our test it is as accurate as the LSTM with 25% fewer numbers.

Idea 4 of 4

Three ways to use it, and when a short memory is enough

The network reads the same way in every case; what changes is when you ask it for the answer.

One answer at the end

Classify the whole segment: "was there a harsh brake at the start?", "was this trip risky?".

A label at every step

Mark every second: "braking here", "turning here", "stopped here".

Predict what comes next

At every step, the value a little later: the speed 3 s from now, the engine temperature 10 minutes from now.

To predict the speed 3 seconds from now you do not need to remember the beginning of the trip: the last few seconds are enough. There the RNN has no disadvantage. The two networks, with 16 neurons, trained on 1,000 two-minute trips (one speed reading per second), are almost equally accurate.

Putting it all together

How a recurrent network works

  1. It starts with its memory at zero: it has not read anything yet.
  2. It reads one reading and mixes it with its memory, with the same weights at every step.
  3. It updates the memory. In the RNN, the whole memory is rewritten at every step; in the LSTM, the gates decide what is erased, what is written and what is shown, and the belt is updated by adding.
  4. It repeats until the last reading, whether the segment is long or short.
  5. It answers: at the end (classify), at every step (label) or with the next value (predict), with an output layer like the one in topic 4.
  6. It is trained unrolled: the error travels back step by step to the beginning. In the RNN it vanishes within a few seconds; in the LSTM, the belt lets it through.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Arrival time

The network reads the trip GPS point by GPS point (speed, stops, road segment) and at every moment predicts how long is left to arrive. It remembers that the truck has spent half an hour in a traffic jam even if it is going fast now.

Temperature and breakdowns

With the history of engine load, rpm and temperature, an LSTM predicts the temperature a few minutes from now or the probability of a breakdown in the next few days. What matters may have happened hours earlier.

How each driver drives

A sequence of maneuvers (braking, speed-ups, turns, idling) says more than each maneuver on its own. The network sums it up in a driving profile and flags the risky trips.

The odd ones on the CAN bus

The network learns which truck messages usually come after which. If the next reading looks nothing like what it expected, it may be a broken sensor or a breakdown that is starting.

Connections

With the other topics

In this collection, the recurrent network is a normal layer from topic 3 used over and over, with its memory as an extra input. It is trained with the backpropagation from topic 5, unrolled, and the vanishing-gradient problem is the same one from topic 2, now along time. The batches, Adam and the learning rate are the ones from topic 6. The other way to read a signal, with a fixed window, is the convolutional network from topic 8. And if the input is words or categories, they are first turned into vectors with the embeddings of topic 10.

Carrying a state through time is not unique to networks. The Kalman filter also has a memory that is updated with every reading, but its rule is designed by a person from the physics of the truck. Markov chains move from one state to another without remembering anything else. The recurrent network learns from the data what to remember. Backpropagating through time is the chain rule from the math collection applied step by step. And in many tasks with long sequences, such as language, recurrent networks have made way for attention and Transformers, topic 11, which look at all the readings at once instead of carrying a memory.

To be fair

What it gives you and what to watch out for

What it gives you

  • It reads sequences of any length with the same weights.
  • It can answer at every step, on the fly, without waiting for the whole trip.
  • The LSTM and the GRU remember what matters for a long time: a 10 s gap and 99.2% accuracy in our test.
  • It takes little memory to use: you only need to keep its state, not everything it has read.

What to watch out for

  • It reads one at a time: the work on a segment cannot be split across many cores, and training it is slow.
  • The plain RNN forgets within a few seconds: with a 5 s gap, it is as accurate as a coin.
  • The exploding error ruins training if it is not clipped.
  • Even the LSTM sometimes gets stuck: without opening the forget gate at the start, two of the three runs with a 10 s gap stayed at 50%.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Recurrent network (RNN)
A network that reads a sequence step by step and passes its own output to itself as memory.
Hidden state
The network's memory: a few numbers that sum up what it has read so far.
Unroll
Drawing the network as a chain of copies, one per step, all with the same weights.
Backpropagation through time
Carrying the error from the end of the sequence backward, step by step, to correct the weights.
Vanishing gradient
The error that becomes tiny when it is multiplied by small numbers at every step back.
Exploding gradient
The opposite: the error that grows out of control when it is multiplied by large numbers.
Gradient clipping
Shrinking the correction when it comes out too big, before applying it.
LSTM
A recurrent network with a memory conveyor belt and three gates: forget, input and output.
Gate
A number between 0 and 1 that the network computes at every step and that multiplies what it lets through.
GRU
A lighter version of the LSTM, with two gates and no separate belt.
