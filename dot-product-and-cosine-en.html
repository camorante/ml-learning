Dot product and cosine similarity

Multiply in pairs and add up. It is grade-school arithmetic, and yet it sits inside almost every machine learning model: it computes costs, tells you whether two arrows point the same way, compares patterns and decides whether a truck goes in for an inspection.

The setup

A truck makes a trip: some kilometers in the city, some on rural roads and some on the highway. Each kind of road has its own price per kilometer (fuel, wear, tolls): the city costs more because of traffic jams, the highway has tolls and the rural road is the cheapest.

We have two lists of three numbers, in the same order: the kilometers and the prices. A list of numbers like this is called a vector, and it can be drawn as an arrow (we saw this in topic 1, vectors and distances). How much does the trip cost?

How to read the chart. Each leg is a rectangle: its width is its kilometers and its height is its price per kilometer. Its area is what that leg costs. Below, the total bar puts the three costs one after another: city, rural road and highway.

Try this, in order:

  1. Look at the starting math: 40 × 0.60 + 120 × 0.40 + 200 × 0.50 = 24 + 48 + 100 = $172.
  2. Raise the city price from $0.60 to $0.80/km: the total only goes up $8, because there are few city kilometers.
  3. Set it back to 0.60 and raise the highway price from 0.50 to 0.70: now it goes up $40. The same price change weighs more where there are more kilometers.
  4. Set the city kilometers to 0: its rectangle disappears and its price no longer matters (0 times anything is 0).

What you just saw

Multiply in pairs and add up: that is the dot product

You took two lists of the same size, multiplied the first number of one by the first number of the other, the second by the second and the third by the third, and added up the results. That calculation has a name: the dot product (or scalar product), and it is written with a dot in the middle: kilometers · prices = $172.

Three things worth remembering:

  • The result is a single number, not another list.
  • The two lists must be in the same order: if you pair the city kilometers with the highway price, the result means nothing.
  • Each pair weighs according to the size of both numbers: a high price with few kilometers weighs little.

So far, arithmetic. The surprising part is that this same calculation has a picture: it tells you whether two arrows point the same way. The five ideas that follow all come from there.

Idea 1 of 5

The picture: do they point the same way?

A truck drives at 75 km/h. Its velocity is an arrow: 60 km/h toward the east and 45 km/h toward the north, that is, the vector (60, 45). The wind is blowing, and it is another arrow. If you take the dot product of the two (60 × the wind's east part + 45 × its north part), the number you get tells you how they get along: large and positive if the wind pushes from behind, zero if it blows from the side and negative if it blows head-on.

How to read the chart. The blue arrow is the truck and the orange one is the wind (you can drag its tip). The arc marks the angle between the two. The thick shadow on the truck's line is the part of the wind that helps (green) or works against it (red).

Idea 2 of 5

The shadow: how much of a trip goes toward the warehouse

A truck drives 50 km, but not in a straight line toward the warehouse. How many of those kilometers actually brought it closer? Imagine the sun is right above the road that leads to the warehouse: the shadow of the trip on that road is how far it advanced toward it. That shadow is computed with a dot product: the trip · the warehouse direction (an arrow of length 1).

How to read the chart. The gray line goes from the start toward the warehouse (the square). The orange arrow is the trip (you can drag its tip). The dashed line drops at a right angle to the road: where it lands, the shadow ends, in green if the trip brought you closer and in red if it took you farther away.

Idea 3 of 5

Cosine similarity: compare the shape, not the size

Each truck has a usage profile: hours per week in the city and hours on the highway. We want to find the truck that works most like another one. There are two ways to measure it:

  • Distance: the length of the straight line between the two points (we saw it in topic 1). It pays attention to size: a truck that works twice as much ends up far away.
  • Cosine similarity: looks only at the angle between the two arrows. It is 1 if they point the same way (same split between city and highway) and 0 if they form a right angle. It is computed as the dot product divided by the lengths of the two arrows, so that size does not count.

How to read the chart. Pick a reference truck above (orange arrow). Each point is a truck: further right, more hours in the city; further up, more hours on the highway. The dashed purple line joins it to the closest truck by distance. The blue arrow is the most similar one by cosine, and the arc is the angle between the two.

Idea 4 of 5

A linear model is a dot product

A simple breakdown model gives each truck a score: it multiplies each measurement by a weight (how much that measurement matters), adds them up, and adds a fixed number, the bias. That is: score = weights · data + bias. If the score comes out positive, the truck goes in for an inspection.

Here each truck has two measurements: engine vibration (in mm/s) and hours since the last inspection (in hundreds of hours). All the trucks with a score of zero form a straight line: the boundary between "to inspection" and "keeps rolling".

How to read the chart. Each point is a truck from last week: orange if it had a breakdown, purple if not. The shaded area is where the model raises an alert (positive score). The blue arrow is the weights: it always forms a right angle with the boundary and points toward where the score goes up. Points with a red ring are mistakes. The hollow square is a new truck.

This is what happens inside linear regression (the score is the prediction itself), logistic regression (the score is turned into a risk percentage) and support vector machines (they pick the boundary with the widest margin). And a neuron in a neural network does exactly this calculation and then passes the result through a curve; neural networks will get their own collection.

Idea 5 of 5

Finding look-alikes at scale

The maintenance shop has thousands of handwritten incident reports. A new one comes in and we want the most similar old ones, to see how they were fixed. Searching for matching words fails: "squeal when braking" and "noise when pressing the brake" barely share any words and talk about the same thing.

The usual solution is an embedding: a model turns each text into a list of numbers (a vector) so that texts with similar meaning give arrows that point the same way. Then you search with cosine similarity. Here we use only three numbers per text, set by hand so they are easy to follow: how much it talks about brakes, about electrics and about the engine. A real model uses hundreds of numbers and learns them by reading millions of texts.

How to read the bars. At the top you pick the new incident. Each row is an old incident with its three numbers (brakes · electrics · engine). The bar is the cosine similarity with the new one: full is 1 (they point the same way), empty is 0 (they have nothing in common). In green, the most similar one.

This is how search engines that search "by meaning" work, and the systems that give an AI assistant the relevant documents before it answers. And inside large language models, the attention mechanism decides which words to pay attention to using dot products between vectors: the same ones as in this topic, at a gigantic scale.

Putting it all together

The complete recipe

  1. Write each thing as a list of numbers, always in the same order (km per kind of road, hours per time slot, truck measurements).
  2. Dot product = multiply in pairs and add up. It gives a single number.
  3. Its sign tells you where they point: positive, same side; zero, right angle; negative, opposite sides.
  4. With an arrow of length 1, it gives the shadow: how much of one thing goes in the direction of the other.
  5. Divided by the two lengths, it gives the cosine: similarity of shape, regardless of size.
  6. With weights and a bias, it is a linear model: a score and a straight boundary.
  7. At scale, it finds look-alikes: vectors of texts or trips and the highest cosine.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Cost of each trip

Kilometers per kind of road · price per kilometer, hours per time slot · cost per hour: the cost of thousands of trips is thousands of dot products.

Similar trucks and routes

Usage profiles compared by cosine: find trucks that work the same way even if one does twice the hours, to compare fuel use or plan maintenance.

Score-based alerts

Vibration, temperature, hours and fault codes, each with its own weight: a linear score decides which trucks go in for an inspection.

Similar incidents

Shop reports and driver notes turned into vectors: when an incident comes in, the old ones with the same problem show up even if they are worded differently.

Connections

With the models of the ML series

Linear regression, logistic regression and support vector machines are weights · data + bias (idea 4). K-nearest neighbors can use distance or cosine to decide who counts as a neighbor (idea 3). Principal component analysis is shadows on a few well-chosen directions (idea 2). And it all starts from the vectors and distances of topic 1. If the columns have very different scales, it is a good idea to scale them first.

To be fair

Where it helps and where it misleads

Where it helps

  • It is an extremely fast calculation: computers do billions of them per second.
  • The cosine ignores size: it compares the patterns of big and small trucks.
  • The same idea works for costs, directions, look-alikes and models.

Where it misleads

  • If one column has huge numbers (meters versus hours), it calls all the shots by itself.
  • The cosine does not see size: sometimes it matters that a truck works twice as much.
  • A list of all zeros has no arrow, and the cosine cannot be computed.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Vector
A list of numbers in a fixed order; it can be drawn as an arrow.
Dot product (scalar product)
Multiplying two vectors in pairs and adding up. It gives a number.
Length (norm)
How long the arrow is. It is the square root of the dot product of a vector with itself.
Perpendicular (orthogonal)
Two arrows at a right angle. Their dot product is 0.
Projection
The shadow of one arrow on the line of another.
Cosine similarity
The dot product divided by the two lengths: from −1 (opposite) to 1 (same direction).
Weight and bias
How much each measurement matters in a linear model, and the fixed number added at the end.
Embedding
A vector that a model gives to a text (or something else) so that similar things point the same way.
