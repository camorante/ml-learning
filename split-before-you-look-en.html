Split before you look

To find out whether a model will work, you give it an exam with data it has never seen. That slice of data has to be set aside before anything else, and it has to be chosen well. If it is set aside badly, the exam will say the model is excellent, and the model will fail on its first day of real use.

The setup

We have a fleet of 60 trucks and a table with 330 days of history. Each row is one truck on one day, with its kilometers, the load, the idle time (engine running while stopped), the slope of the route, the temperature, the odometer (the total distance counter) and how much fuel it used that day.

We want a model, a program that learns from that table, so that every morning it says how many liters per 100 km each truck will use that day. A typical truck uses about 35.

Before using it for real, we need to know how wrong it gets. For that we give it an exam: we hide some of the days, the model learns only from the rest, and then it predicts the hidden days without seeing them. Its average error on those days is what the exam promises. After that we use it for real over the next 70 days and measure what happens. A good exam promises the same thing that later happens.

How to read the chart. The strip at the top is the 400 days, from left to right. Blue: days the model learns from. Purple: days hidden for the exam. Orange: the days of real use, which haven't happened yet today. Below, two bars: the error the exam promises and the actual error. The shorter the bar, the smaller the error.

Decision 1: which days do we hide for the exam?

  • Random single days: 1 in every 5 days is picked by lottery, spread over the whole history. This is the default in many tutorials.
  • The last 60 days: the most recent days are hidden (day 270 to 329) and the model learns only from the earlier ones.

Decision 2: do we give it the column "liters it will refuel tomorrow"?

The historical table has a column with the liters each truck refueled the next day. Since what it burns today is what has to be put back tomorrow, that column almost holds the answer. The problem: on the morning the model makes its prediction, tomorrow hasn't come yet and that column is empty.

Try this, in order, and read the message below each time:

  1. Random single days · Don't give it: the exam promises a somewhat smaller error than the real one.
  2. The last 60 days · Don't give it: the exam promises almost exactly the real error.
  3. Either one · Give it: the exam promises near perfection, and then the model fails badly.

What you just saw

The exam has to look like real use, and it can't come with the answers

That hidden slice is called the test set, and the part the model learns from is the training set. It works like an exam at school: the grade means something if the questions look like what's coming and the student hasn't seen the answers beforehand. The toy breaks both conditions:

  • Random single days: every hidden day has neighboring days the model did see, from the same truck. To guess Tuesday, it already knows Monday and Wednesday, and fuel use on one day is very similar to the days next to it. In real use it won't have "Wednesday": it always predicts forward. That's why the exam is easier than reality and promises too much.
  • Tomorrow's liters: in the exam the column is filled in, and the model learns to copy it. In real use it's empty, and the model loses its "cheat sheet". This is called data leakage: information that won't exist at prediction time sneaks in while the model learns or is tested.

That's where the title of this topic, "split before you look", comes from: the slice for the exam is set aside before anything else, before exploring or cleaning the data, and it's set aside so that it looks like real use.

Idea 1 of 5

A short exam gives a very noisy grade

Here we switch examples to make it easier to see: a model that answers yes or no, for example "will this truck need to go to the maintenance shop this month?". Imagine it truly gets 9 out of 10 right (90%). Its exam grade is the percentage of exam cases it gets right.

That grade doesn't always come out at 90%: it depends on which cases it gets, just like flipping a coin 10 times doesn't always give 5 heads. With few cases, luck counts a lot.

Idea 2 of 5

If the future is what you'll predict, test with the future

This is decision 1 from the toy above, seen up close with a single truck. Its fuel use changes little by little: the tires wear down, the driver changes, winter arrives. That's why fuel use on one day is very similar to the day before and the day after.

With random single days, each exam day ends up surrounded by days the model already saw, and it only has to "fill in the gap" between two known values. By hiding the last days, the exam looks like real use: predicting days that come after everything it has seen.

Large purple dots: days hidden for the exam. The bars below are the average error over the whole fleet (60 trucks) in each case, and the orange one is the error in real use.

Idea 3 of 5

If new trucks are coming, test with new trucks

A different situation: the company is going to buy trucks, and the model will have to predict trucks it has never seen. Each truck has its quirks: this one uses 2 L more than average because of its engine, that one less.

If you hide random single days, every truck in the exam also shows up among the days it learns from, so the model already knows its quirks and does better. With a new truck it won't know any. For the exam to look like that, you have to hide whole trucks: all of their days.

How to read the chart. Each row is a truck and each square is one of its days (16 trucks and 40 days are shown). Blue: days for learning. Purple: exam days. The three orange rows at the bottom are trucks that will be bought later. The bars compare what each exam promises with the real error on those new trucks.

Idea 4 of 5

The question that fixes almost everything: would I know it at that moment?

Each column in the table is a piece of data the model can use. Before giving it one, picture the exact moment the model makes its prediction: in the morning, before the truck leaves. Does that data already exist at that time? If it doesn't, it's data leakage (see above). The most dangerous ones are columns that look normal in the historical table but were filled in later.

How to use it. Pick a column to add to the basic model (the one that only has km, load, idle time, slope, temperature and odometer). The two gray bars are the basic model: what the exam promises and what happens in real use. The colored ones are the model with the added column: blue, what the exam promises; orange, what really happens. A good column shortens both colored bars. A leak shortens the blue one and lengthens the orange one. In every case the exam is the last 60 days.

Idea 5 of 5

Looking at all the data to make decisions is cheating too

Before training, it's common to keep only the columns that look useful. That step also "learns" from the data, and if it looks at the exam days, it cheats even though the model never sees them.

An extreme case to make it visible: 100 trips, each with 2,000 columns of random numbers (unrelated to anything), and a yes/no answer decided by a coin flip. Nothing can predict a coin: the honest result is 50% right. But with 2,000 columns, some will look like the coin by pure chance, in the exam trips too.

The buttons change which trips are used to pick the 20 columns the model then uses: all the trips (including the exam ones) or only the trips for learning.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Split first, before exploring, cleaning or choosing anything. Whatever you look at in the test set is no longer a test.
  2. Imitate real use: if you'll predict the future, the test is the last days; if new trucks will arrive, the test is whole trucks.
  3. Check every column with the question "does it exist at prediction time?".
  4. Learn everything from the training set: averages for filling gaps, scales, chosen columns. The test set is only used at the end.
  5. Use a large enough test set or repeat the split several times to know how much noise the grade has.
  6. Be suspicious of results that are too good: they're almost always a leak.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Forecasts

Fuel use, kilometers, engine hours: always split by date, with a gap between training and test if the features use windows of several days.

New customers and trucks

A model that will be sold to new fleets is tested with whole fleets set aside, not with mixed-up trips.

Breakdown prediction

Maintenance shop records are closed days after the breakdown. Using "closing date" or "parts replaced" as a feature is leaving the answer in the exam.

Data that arrives late

Readings resent hours later: the final table has them, but at prediction time they hadn't arrived.

Connections

With the models of the ML series

In this topic the model is a set of boosted trees, like XGBoost, but everything applies just the same to any other: linear regression, nearest neighbors (the ones that take the most advantage of neighboring days) or Gaussian processes. The more flexible models get more out of a leak, so with them you have to be even more careful.

To be fair

What it costs and what you gain

What you gain

  • An error figure you can trust before putting anything into service.
  • Leaks are caught before the model is used for real.
  • It lets you compare models fairly.

What it costs

  • You train with less data (made up for by retraining on everything at the end).
  • Splitting by date or by truck usually gives worse errors: it's not the model, it's the truth.
  • You have to think about how the model will be used before touching the data.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Training (train)
The part of the data the model learns from.
Validation
A part set aside to choose between models and settings. Because it's used many times, it ends up "worn out".
Test
The part that is looked at only once, at the end, to give the error figure.
Model
A program that learns from a table of examples to make predictions about new cases.
Production (real use)
When the model is already running and predicting with real data that didn't exist when it was trained.
Data leakage
When information that won't exist at prediction time sneaks into training or evaluation.
Time-based split
Training on the past and testing on what came after.
Group split
All the data from one truck (or fleet, or driver) goes together into training or into the test.
Cross-validation
Repeating the split several times with different slices and averaging, so the grade has less noise.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need them to understand the idea:

noise of an accuracy grade measured with n cases: margin ≈ 2 × √( p × (1 − p) / n ) p = 0.9 and n = 100 → ± 6 points p = 0.9 and n = 1,000 → ± 2 points leakage rule: a column x is OK if moment x becomes known ≤ moment of prediction
