Hypothesis tests and p-values

Ten trucks take an eco-driving course and burn 1.6 L/100 km less than ten others. Does the course work, or was it luck? A hypothesis test answers one very specific question: if the course did nothing at all, how often would a difference like this show up by chance?

The setup

A trucking company wants to know whether an eco-driving course saves diesel. It runs an A/B test: out of 20 similar trucks, it draws 10 at random whose drivers take the course; the other 10 carry on as usual. A month later it looks up each truck's average fuel use in the telemetry.

The course trucks average 30.4 L/100 km and the others 32.0: a saving of 1.6. But trucks differ a lot from one another (routes, loads, the truck itself), and with only 10 per group, the random draw could have put the thriftiest trucks in the course group just by chance. How can we tell?

The idea: assume the course does nothing. Then the labels “course” and “no course” don’t matter, and any other split of the 20 trucks would have been just as likely. So we shuffle the labels and see what difference comes out.

How to read the chart. At the top, each dot is a truck, placed by its fuel use (liters per 100 km). The color is its real group: purple no course and orange course. The row it sits in is the label it has now: when you shuffle, trucks change rows but not colors. The vertical line in each row is its mean. At the bottom, the histogram of the differences in means (“no course” minus “course”) of all the shuffles; the dashed lines mark the real difference, 1.6, on both sides, and in red are the shuffles that reach that far or beyond.

Shuffle the labels

Each shuffle hands out the 20 labels (10 “course” and 10 “no course”) at random among the 20 trucks, as if the draw were done again, and computes the difference in means. It is what you would get if the course did nothing.

Back to the real data

Puts every truck back under its real label and erases the shuffles.

Try this, in order:

  1. Look at the real data: the orange dots sit a bit further left (they burn less), but they mix quite a lot with the purple ones.
  2. Press “Shuffle once” four or five times: the difference comes out sometimes positive and sometimes negative, almost always smaller than 1.6.
  3. Press “+1,000”: the histogram takes on a bell shape around 0. Count how many shuffles reach 1.6 or more.
  4. Press “+10,000”: the percentage settles near 3.1%. That number is the p-value.

What you just saw

The p-value: how surprising the result is if nothing were going on

A hypothesis test always takes the same three steps:

  • Assume nothing is going on. “The course doesn’t change fuel use.” That assumption is called the null hypothesis. The opposite (“it does change it”) is the alternative hypothesis.
  • See what results chance alone would give if the null were true. Here, by shuffling the labels: the differences land around 0, nearly all between −1.5 and +1.5.
  • Look at where the real result falls. The fraction of chance results as extreme as the real one or more is the p-value. Here, 3.1%: if the course were useless, a difference of 1.6 or more (in either direction) would come out in about 3 draws out of 100.

A small p-value means the result would be rare if nothing were going on, so the “nothing is going on” explanation loses strength. It is customary to call a result with a p-value below 5% “statistically significant”. But the p-value has traps: it is not the probability that the course doesn’t work (idea 2), with few trucks real effects slip through (ideas 3 and 4), “significant” does not mean “important” (idea 5), and you can manufacture a significant result without meaning to by trying many things (idea 6) or by checking every day (idea 7).

Idea 1 of 7

The bell of luck, and whether one side counts or two

Shuffling thousands of times gives a bell shape. You don’t need to shuffle to know it: in topic 11 we saw that a difference in means wobbles in a bell shape with a width called the standard error, computed from the spread of the data and the number of trucks. Here it is 0.69 L/100 km. With little data you use the slightly fatter-tailed bell of Student’s t. That formula is the famous t-test.

One decision remains: what counts as “as extreme”? If, before starting, all you cared about was whether the course saves fuel, you count only differences of 1.6 or more on the saving side: a one-sided test. If it would also matter that the course worsened fuel use (say, slower drivers who lug the engine), you count both sides: a two-sided test, the usual one.

How to read the chart. The gray bars are all the possible ways to split the 20 trucks into two groups of 10 (there are 184,756) and the difference each one would give. The blue curve is the t-test bell, which matches it closely without any shuffling. The black line is the observed saving and the red areas are the results as extreme or more: their size is the p-value.

Idea 2 of 7

What the p-value doesn’t tell you: the probability that the course is useless

It is the most widespread mistake: reading “p = 3%” as “there is a 3% probability that the course doesn’t work”. That’s not it. The p-value is computed assuming the course doesn’t work: it is the probability of the result if the null is true, not the probability that the null is true. They are two different questions, like “how likely is rain if it’s cloudy?” and “how likely is it cloudy if it’s raining?” (topic 9 and Bayes’ theorem).

To see the difference, imagine a company that tests 1,000 ideas (courses, alerts, tires, routes) with A/B tests, and calls everything that comes out with p < 5% a “win”. How many of those “wins” are false? It depends on two things the p-value can’t see: how many ideas really work, and how big the tests are.

How to read the chart. Each square is one tested idea. Solid green: it works and the test detects it. Hollow green: it works, but the test misses it. Red: it doesn’t work, but p < 5% comes out by luck (a false alarm). Gray: it doesn’t work and the test says nothing.

What are the tests like?

With a real saving of 1 L/100 km, a test with 10 trucks per group detects it only 29 times out of 100 (idea 3); with 41 per group, 81 out of 100.

What the p-value does tell you

  • How much the result clashes with the idea that nothing is going on: the smaller it is, the rarer it would be by chance.
  • If you use the rule “act when p < 5%”, ideas that don’t work will get through the filter 5% of the time, no more.

What it doesn’t tell you

  • The probability that the null is true (or that the course works). For that you need to know how many ideas usually work: it is a Bayesian question.
  • How much the course saves: a tiny p-value can come from a tiny saving measured with a huge amount of data (idea 5).
  • That a large p-value proves there is no effect: the test may have been too small to see it (idea 4).

Idea 3 of 7

Two ways to be wrong: false alarms and effects that slip through

Using the rule “if p < 5%, the course works” means setting a threshold. With any threshold there are two possible errors:

  • Type I error (false alarm): the course does nothing, but the test comes out significant by luck. It happens exactly 5% of the time: that 5% is the significance level, called α (alpha).
  • Type II error (an effect that slips through): the course does save fuel, but the test doesn’t reach the threshold. Its probability is called β (beta), and the opposite, the probability of detecting the effect, is the power.

Idea 4 of 7

With few trucks you see nothing (and what you do see is exaggerated)

Power depends on three things: the size of the real effect (a big saving shows up sooner), how much the trucks vary, and how many there are. Since the standard error shrinks with the square root of the number of trucks (topic 11), detecting an effect half as large takes four times as many trucks.

That’s why the number of trucks is decided before the test: you pick the smallest saving worth detecting and work out how many trucks give 80% power. An underpowered test doesn’t just miss real effects: when it happens to come out significant, the measured saving is usually badly exaggerated, because only the measurements that came out high clear the threshold.

How to read the chart. The blue curve is the power (out of 100 tests, how many detect the real saving) by trucks per group, on a logarithmic scale. The dashed line is the usual 80% and the green line marks how many trucks you need to get there. The orange dot is your choice.

Idea 5 of 7

Significant is not important: look at the interval, in dollars

“Statistically significant” only means “hard to explain by chance”. With data from thousands of trucks, even a saving of 0.1 L/100 km comes out significant. Is it worth it? The course costs $300 per driver; at 100,000 km a year and diesel at $1.65/L, each 0.1 L/100 km is $165 a year per truck. To pay for the course you need to save at least 0.18 L/100 km.

What answers that is the confidence interval of the difference (topic 12): it tells you how much the course may save. And it carries the p-value inside: the 95% interval leaves out 0 exactly when p < 5%.

How to read the chart. The thick line is the 95% interval of the saving, with the measured saving in the circle; below the axis, the same in dollars per truck per year. The 0 line is “the course does nothing” and the green zone is the savings that pay for the course.

Idea 6 of 7

Test 20 things and one will come out “significant”

The course report doesn’t look at fuel use alone: also idling, hard braking, harsh acceleration, speed, breakdowns… up to 20 metrics. If the course is useless, each one has a 5% chance of coming out significant by luck. With 20, the odd thing is for none to come out: at least one does in 64% of useless courses. If the report then mentions only the one that came out (“the course reduces hard braking!”), it is a false alarm dressed up as a discovery. Searching through many tests until one comes out is called p-hacking.

Corrections raise the threshold when you run many tests. Bonferroni divides the 5% by the number of tests (0.25% each). Holm does the same, but relaxes the threshold step by step once the clearest test clears it. Benjamini-Hochberg is less strict: it doesn’t prevent every false alarm, but limits what fraction of the “discoveries” are false.

How to read the chart. Each row is one metric of the course, sorted from the smallest p-value to the largest (logarithmic scale: further left, more “surprising”). Idea 7 of 7

Checking every day and stopping when it shows up: another way to cheat without meaning to

The course test is planned for 30 days. But it is tempting to check the p-value every morning and, on the day it drops below 5%, stop and announce that it works. It seems innocent: the result was significant. The problem is that the p-value goes up and down from one day to the next by chance, and with 30 chances to cross the threshold, it crosses it far more than 5% of the time. This is called peeking.

How to read the chart. A test in which the course does nothing: each day the data of 20 trucks per group is added and the p-value is recomputed with everything collected so far (vertical axis, logarithmic scale). The dashed line is 5%. The red dot marks the first day it crosses: that’s where you would have stopped and announced a false saving. In gray, the previous tests.

The fix is to set in advance how many days the test lasts and look at the result only at the end. If you need to be able to stop early (for example, if the course made fuel use much worse), there are sequential methods that spread the 5% across the looks, with a stricter threshold at the start.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Before you start, in writing: what is measured (one primary metric), the null hypothesis, whether the test is one-sided or two-sided, the threshold (5%) and how many days it lasts.
  2. Work out how many trucks you need to detect the smallest saving worth having, with 80% power.
  3. Randomize who gets the change: that way the only thing that sets the groups apart, besides chance, is the change.
  4. Compute the p-value at the end: t-test, shuffling labels, or whichever test fits (percentages, ranks).
  5. Report the interval with it and translate it into dollars: significant is not the same as profitable.
  6. If you look at many metrics, correct (Holm or Benjamini-Hochberg) or state clearly which one was the primary.
  7. Don’t read the p-value as the probability that the effect is real, nor a large p-value as proof that there is no effect.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

An idling alert in the app

With the alert, 15.1% of 2,000 trips have more than 10 minutes of idling; without it, 18.0%. A test of percentages gives p = 1.5%. Careful: trips by the same truck resemble each other, and if trucks (not trips) are randomized, the analysis must be per truck.

Low-rolling-resistance tires

Before the test you work it out: to see a saving of 0.5 L/100 km with trucks that vary by 1.6, you need about 161 per group. With 20, a “no effect” test proves nothing.

Is the new model better?

A new breakdown model gets 76.1% right versus 74.4% for the current one on the same 2,000 trucks. Since they are the same trucks, you compare the cases where they disagree (McNemar’s test): p = 2%, where a test that treated them as independent would give 21%.

The dashboard with 50 metrics

A dashboard that compares 50 metrics per depot every week and flags the “significant” ones in red lights up about 2 or 3 false alarms a week even when nothing changes. Benjamini-Hochberg keeps them in check.

With the models of the ML series and the other topics

This topic brings together almost the whole collection: the standard deviation of topic 7 and the normal bell of topic 8 give chance its shape; the test of percentages is the χ² test of independence from topic 9; correlation has its own p-value too; the standard error and the central limit theorem of topic 11 give the width of the bell, and the intervals of topic 12 are the other side of the p-value. In the ML series, linear regression and logistic regression give a p-value for each coefficient (“does this feature add anything?”), with the same traps: with many features, one comes out significant by luck. Bayes’ theorem answers the question the p-value doesn’t: the probability that the effect is real. In “Preparing the data”, splitting before looking is the same discipline as fixing the test before seeing the data; imbalanced classes bring the same false alarms and effects that slip through, under other names (false positives and negatives), and the complete project decides whether a new model is better than the previous one, which is a hypothesis test.

To be fair

Where it helps and where it misleads

Where it helps

  • It protects you from celebrating flukes: with 10 trucks per group, differences of 1 L/100 km come up on their own all the time.
  • It forces you to plan: what to measure, how many trucks and how many days, before starting.
  • Shuffling labels works for almost any figure, with no formulas.

Where it misleads

  • It is misread almost every time: it is not the probability that the null is true, nor does it measure the size of the effect.
  • The 5% is a custom, not a law: 4.9% and 5.1% say practically the same thing.
  • Testing many things, checking every day or choosing the analysis after seeing the data turns it into a worthless piece of paper.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

A/B test
Splitting the trucks into two groups at random, applying a change to only one, and comparing.
Null hypothesis
The boring assumption: “the change does nothing”. The test tries to rule it out.
Alternative hypothesis
The opposite: “the change does something” (in one direction or in either).
p-value
If the null were true, the probability of a result as extreme as the one observed, or more.
Significant
With a p-value below the chosen threshold (usually 5%). It does not mean important.
Significance level (α)
The threshold: the fraction of false alarms you accept when nothing is going on.
Type I and type II errors
A false alarm (seeing an effect that doesn’t exist) and an effect that slips through (missing one that does exist).
Power
The probability of detecting a real effect of a given size. You aim for 80% or more.
One-sided and two-sided
Counting as “extreme” only results in one direction, or in both.
Permutation test
Shuffling the group labels thousands of times to see what differences chance gives.
t-test
The formula that gives the p-value of a difference in means using Student’s t bell.
p-hacking
Trying many metrics, analyses or stopping points until something comes out significant.
Bonferroni, Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg
Corrections that raise the threshold when many tests are run at once.
