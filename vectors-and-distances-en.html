Vectors and distances

To a computer, a truck is a list of numbers. If you draw that list as an arrow, you can measure how far one truck is from another. That measurement is the basis for finding "the most similar one", for grouping things, and for spotting what's unusual.

The setup

A fleet has 12 trucks. For each one we keep just two numbers, monthly averages: how many stops it makes per day (deliveries, pickups) and how many hours it works per day. With those two numbers, each truck is a point on a plane: so many stops to the right and so many hours up.

The question is simple: which truck is most similar to a given one? It's useful, for example, to pick which truck replaces another that goes into the maintenance shop. The answer will be the point that is closest.

How to read the chart. Each orange point is a truck, with its number. The blue point is the reference truck; the blue arrow starts at zero and reaches it, and the two purple lines are its two numbers: how far it goes to the right (stops) and how far it goes up (hours). The green truck is the most similar one, and the green line is the path measured to it. You can tap any truck to make it the reference, or drag the blue point wherever you like.

Which truck is the reference?

It's the truck we're looking for a match for. If you drag the blue point, it becomes an imaginary truck: a new one that isn't in the fleet yet.

How do we measure distance?

  • Straight line: as the crow flies, diagonally if needed.
  • By blocks: like a truck in a city with a grid of streets: first horizontally and then vertically, adding up the two legs.

For now, one stop counts the same as one hour. In idea 4 we'll see that this is a decision, and an important one.

Try this, in order:

  1. Start with truck 2 (urban delivery). The most similar is truck 1, which makes half a stop more and works half an hour less.
  2. Pick truck 5 and switch between "straight line" and "by blocks": the most similar one goes from truck 6 to truck 7. The way you measure changes the answer.
  3. Drag the blue point to an empty area, for example to 14 stops and 11 hours. The most similar one is far away: a truck like that would be unusual in this fleet.

What you just saw

Similar means close

You turned each truck into two numbers and drew those numbers as a point. From there, "which truck is most similar?" becomes "which point is closest?", and that can be calculated.

Many machine learning methods work like this under the hood: nearest neighbors decides by looking at the most similar ones; k-means groups what is close together. For them to work well you need to understand four things: what exactly that arrow is, how its length is measured, how many ways there are to measure a distance, and what happens when the numbers are in different units or when there are many of them.

Idea 1 of 5

A data point is a list of numbers, and a list of numbers is an arrow

A list of numbers in a fixed order (first the stops, then the hours) is called a vector. You can draw it as an arrow that starts at zero. With arrows you can do two operations that make sense in real life:

  • Add: Monday's work plus Tuesday's work is the work of both days. You add stops to stops and hours to hours. In the drawing, it means placing one arrow right after the other.
  • Multiply by a number: twice the work is twice the stops and twice the hours. The arrow points the same way and is twice as long.

How to read the chart. Horizontally, stops; vertically, hours. The orange arrow is Monday. When adding, the purple one is Tuesday, placed at the end of Monday's, and the blue one is the total. When multiplying, the blue one is Monday multiplied by the number you choose.

Idea 2 of 5

What an arrow measures: the Pythagorean theorem

Imagine a truck that leaves the warehouse and, on a grid street map, ends up 3 blocks east and 4 blocks north. Its vector is (3, 4). How far is it from the warehouse, in a straight line?

The two numbers and the arrow form a triangle with a right angle. Pythagoras says that the long side squared is the sum of the other two squared: 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25, and the square root of 25 is 5. That measurement is called the length (or norm) of the vector.

How to read the chart. The thin lines are the streets. The gray square is the warehouse. The orange line is how far the truck goes east and the purple one, north. The blue arrow is the straight-line distance. Drag the tip of the arrow (the blue point) to another intersection.

Idea 3 of 5

The distance between two trucks is the length of their difference

To know how far B is from A, you subtract their numbers (how much you have to move in each direction to go from A to B) and measure the length of that arrow. There are several reasonable ways to measure it:

  • Straight line (Euclidean distance): Pythagoras, as in idea 2.
  • By blocks (Manhattan distance): you add up the two legs, like a truck that can only drive along grid streets.
  • By the longest side (maximum distance): only the largest of the differences counts. It's useful when what matters is the worst case: "no number may differ by more than so much".

How to read the chart. Drag trucks A and B. The green line is the path being measured. The filled blue shape contains all the points that are the same distance from A as B is: with the straight line it's a circle; by blocks, a diamond; by the longest side, a square. The other two shapes appear dashed for comparison.

Idea 4 of 5

Units rule

Let's change one number: instead of hours, we use kilometers per day. Kilometers range from 130 to 750; stops, from 1 to 13.5. If you compute the distance as is, a difference of 40 km weighs the same as a difference of 40 stops, and 40 stops don't exist in this fleet. Result: kilometers decide everything and stops barely count.

The fix is to scale (standardize): from each number you subtract its mean and divide by its deviation, which is how far that number usually strays from the mean in the fleet. In this fleet, the deviation of the km is about 200 km and that of the stops, about 4.25 stops. After scaling, "200 km more" and "4.25 stops more" weigh the same.

How to read the chart. Horizontally, kilometers per day; vertically, stops per day. The blue point is the reference and the green one, its most similar truck. The light blue area is all the places that would be at the same distance as that truck or closer. Without scaling, the area is a narrow, very tall strip: only being close in kilometers matters, stops make no difference. When you scale, it becomes an oval that looks at both.

Do we scale?

Not scaled: km and stops as they are. Scaled: each number measured in deviations.

Reference truck

Start with 2 and then try 5, 8 and 12.

That's why the data is almost always scaled before using distances: the topic preparing the features in "Preparing the data" explains it, and it's the first warning of the nearest neighbors method.

Idea 5 of 5

With many numbers, everything is equally far away

A real truck doesn't have two numbers, it has dozens or hundreds: speeds, hard brakes, fuel use, temperatures, hours per time slot… The formulas are the same: with 3 numbers, Pythagoras adds three squares; with 100, it adds a hundred. But something surprising happens: the more numbers, the more all distances look alike. The closest and the farthest end up at almost the same distance, and "the most similar one" stops meaning anything. It's called the curse of dimensionality (each number is a dimension).

How to read the chart. 120 made-up trucks are scattered at random and the 7,140 distances between every pair are measured. The bars count how many distances there are of each size, compared with the average distance (the 1 in the middle). The green line marks how far a truck's nearest neighbor is and the red one, the farthest. The height of the bars adjusts automatically.

Putting it all together

The recipe for measuring similarity

  1. Turn each thing into a list of numbers, always in the same order: that's its vector.
  2. Choose which numbers go in. Only the ones that matter for the similarity you're after: a number that has nothing to do with it only adds noise.
  3. Put them on the same scale (standardize) so none of them takes over because of its units.
  4. Choose how to measure: straight line by default; by blocks if there are extreme values or many numbers; by the longest side if the worst case matters.
  5. Find the closest ones or the ones that are far from everything, depending on the question.
  6. With many numbers, check that the closest one is clearly closer than the rest. If not, reduce the number of dimensions.

In the real world

Where distances are used in fleet telemetry

Similar trips

Each trip is a vector (km, duration, stops, fuel use, departure time). Finding the trips closest to a new one gives an estimate of its duration or its fuel use.

An odd truck

A truck that is far from all the others (fuel use, temperatures, vibrations) deserves an inspection: it could be a breakdown, a badly calibrated sensor or misuse.

Grouping routes

k-means puts nearby routes into groups (urban delivery, regional, long haul) to plan vehicles, shifts and maintenance by type of use.

A replacement driver

If a driver is out, you look for another one with a close history: same areas, schedules, vehicle type and driving style.

Connections

With the models of the ML series and with "Preparing the data"

Nearest neighbors decides using the distances in this topic. k-means groups nearby points and computes the center of each group as an average of arrows. Support vector machines look for the boundary that is farthest from both groups, and their "kernel" version compares points with a distance. Principal component analysis reduces many numbers to a few, which helps against the curse of dimensionality. To scale the data properly, see the topic preparing the features. The next topic in this collection, the dot product, measures which way two arrows point instead of how far apart they are.

To be fair

What helps and where it misleads

What helps

  • A very intuitive idea: similar = close. It can be explained with a drawing.
  • It works with anything that can be described with numbers.
  • There's nothing to train: you just store the data and measure.

Where it misleads

  • The units silently decide who is "the most similar one".
  • A number that has nothing to do with what you're after spoils it all the same.
  • With many numbers, all distances look alike.
  • Measuring against millions of points is slow if the search isn't organized well.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Vector
A list of numbers in a fixed order. It's drawn as an arrow from zero.
Component
Each of the numbers in the vector (the stops, the hours).
Dimension
How many numbers the vector has. Two numbers, two dimensions: a plane.
Length (norm)
How long the arrow is. In a straight line, with Pythagoras.
Euclidean distance
The straight-line distance.
Manhattan distance
The distance by blocks: the sum of the differences.
Maximum distance
Only the largest of the differences counts.
Standardize
Subtract the mean and divide by the deviation, so that all numbers weigh about the same.
Nearest neighbor
The point at the smallest distance from a given one.
Curse of dimensionality
With many numbers, all distances become alike.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need them to understand the idea:

truck A = (a1, a2) truck B = (b1, b2) sum A + B = (a1 + b1, a2 + b2) times k k · A = (k·a1, k·a2) straight-line length: √(a1² + a2²) (3, 4) → √25 = 5 straight-line distance: √((a1−b1)² + (a2−b2)²) distance by blocks: |a1−b1| + |a2−b2| maximum distance: the larger of |a1−b1| and |a2−b2| standardize: z = (number − mean) / deviation km: mean 368.3 deviation 200.1 stops: mean 7.7 deviation 4.25 with n numbers, the same with n terms: √((a1−b1)² + (a2−b2)² + … + (an−bn)²)
