The neuron

The smallest piece of a neural network is a very simple calculation: it multiplies each piece of data by a weight, adds it all up, adds a starting number and says “yes” or “no” depending on the result. With that, it draws a straight line that separates two groups, and it can learn that line by correcting itself every time it gets something wrong.

The setup

A fleet manager wants to know which driving days are worth reviewing with the driver (to give tips for smoother, cheaper driving). For each day, the telematics gives two numbers: the harsh braking events per 100 km and the hours the engine spent idling (running with the truck standing still).

The manager has already reviewed 20 days by hand and marked 10 of them as “review”. Now they want an automatic rule. Their first idea is a points system: 1 point for each harsh brake, 0.2 points for each hour of idling, and you start at −6 points. If the total ends up above 0, the day gets reviewed. That, exactly, is an artificial neuron.

How to read the chart. Each dot is one day: further right means more harsh braking; higher up means more idle hours. The orange dots are the days the manager marked for review, and the purple ones are the days that are fine. The blue line is the neuron’s boundary: days in the orange zone score more than 0 points and get reviewed; days in the purple zone don’t. A red ring marks every day on the wrong side.

What you just saw

A neuron is a weighted sum that makes a decision

The neuron in the toy always does the same thing with each day: it multiplies each number by its weight, adds up the results and adds the bias. If the total is above 0, it says “review”; if not, “OK”. The weights and the bias are its adjustable numbers: changing them changes the way it decides.

  • Its boundary is a straight line. The days that score exactly 0 form a straight line on the chart. On one side it says “yes” and on the other “no”.
  • The weights turn the line and the bias shifts it. With two numbers, that is enough to put it anywhere.
  • It can learn on its own. If it gets a day wrong, it moves its weights a little toward the right answer. By repeating this, it finds a line that separates all 20 correctly.

The neural networks that recognize images or predict arrival times are thousands or millions of these pieces connected together. The ideas below go step by step: the calculation inside (1), the “yes or no” turned into a probability (2), how it learns (3), why there are many valid lines (4) and what a single neuron cannot do (5).

Idea 1 of 5

Inside: multiply, add up and decide

The trained neuron gives 0.83 points per harsh brake, 1.28 per hour of idling and starts at −6. For one particular day, the calculation has three steps: each number is multiplied by its weight; everything is added together with the bias (the score); and the score goes through a “switch” that says “review” if it is greater than 0.

Multiplying number by number and adding up is called the dot product (you saw it in math topic 2): it measures how much the day looks like what the neuron is looking for.

Idea 2 of 5

From “yes or no” to a probability

The switch is abrupt: a day with a score of 0.01 gets reviewed and another with −0.01 doesn’t, even though they are almost the same. If instead of the step you use an S-shaped curve, the sigmoid, the neuron gives a percentage: close to 100% if the score is very positive, close to 0% if it is very negative, and exactly 50% if it is 0, on the same line as before.

A neuron with a sigmoid that learns by comparing its percentages with what actually happened is exactly a logistic regression, the model from the ML series. Other possible curves (and why they are needed) are the subject of activation functions.

How to read the chart. Horizontally, each day’s score; vertically, the neuron’s answer. The dashed gray line is the step (0% or 100%) and the blue curve is the sigmoid. Each dot is one of the 20 days (orange: marked for review; purple: fine), placed on the sigmoid. Idea 3 of 5

Learning from mistakes: the perceptron rule

In 1958, Frank Rosenblatt proposed the perceptron: a neuron with a step and a rule for learning its weights. The neuron looks at the days one by one. If it is right, it changes nothing. If it is wrong, it moves each weight a tiny bit in the direction that would have fixed that mistake:

If it should have said “review” and said “OK”: each weight + 0.1 × its number; bias + 0.1.

If it should have said “OK” and said “review”: each weight − 0.1 × its number; bias − 0.1.

The 0.1 is the step size. When it finishes a pass through the 20 days, it starts another, until a whole pass goes by without a mistake. If there is any line that separates the two groups correctly, it has been proven that the rule finds it in a finite number of corrections.

How to read the chart. The blue line is the current boundary and the dashed gray one is the boundary before the last correction. The thick black ring marks the day the neuron is looking at. It starts from the manager’s first idea (1 point per harsh brake, 0.2 per hour, −6 to start).

Idea 4 of 5

Many lines get all 20 right: which one is best?

The perceptron stops as soon as it stops making mistakes, even if the line barely grazes some day. If the days are shown to it in a different order, it ends up with a different line, also with no mistakes. They all get the 20 days it knows right, but not all of them do equally well on new days, which is what matters.

To check, each line is tested on 1,000 new days from the same fleet (the small dots in the background). The middle line, the one that leaves the most free space on both sides, is the idea behind support vector machines.

How to read the chart. The big dots are the 20 training days; the small dots, the 1,000 new ones. Each purple line is a perceptron trained on the 20 days in a different order; the blue one is the latest. The dashed green line is the middle line.

Idea 5 of 5

What a straight line cannot separate

Another fleet problem: flagging trips whose fuel use doesn’t match the load. A more heavily loaded truck burns more, so normal trips form a sloping band. The odd ones are above it (burning too much: a breakdown, low tire pressure, a leak) or below it (burning too little: the load or the refueling was logged wrong).

A neuron can only draw one line and flag one side of it. The odd trips are on both sides of normal, so part of them always gets away. It is the same problem as the famous “exclusive OR” (XOR), which in 1969 made people doubt neural networks for years. The solution is to combine several neurons: you will see it in topic 3, the multilayer network.

How to read the chart. Each dot is a trip: horizontally, the load in tonnes; vertically, the fuel use in liters per 100 km. Purple: normal fuel use for its load. Orange: doesn’t match. The orange zone is where the neuron raises an alert; a red ring marks every mistake (an extra alert or an odd trip that gets away).

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Input data: numbers that describe the case (harsh braking, idle hours…).
  2. Weighted sum: each number times its weight, all added up, plus the bias. That is the score.
  3. Activation: a step to decide “yes or no”, or a sigmoid to give a percentage.
  4. Boundary: the scores equal to 0 form a straight line (with more numbers, a plane). The weights turn it and the bias shifts it.
  5. Learning: each mistake moves the weights a little toward the right answer, pass after pass.
  6. Checking with new cases: getting the known examples right is not enough; several lines get them right.
  7. Knowing the limit: if what you are looking for doesn’t lie on just one side of a line, you need several neurons.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telematics

Driver score

The driving scores that many platforms give are weighted sums: harsh braking, harsh acceleration, speeding and idling, each with its own weight. A neuron learns those weights from days that have already been reviewed.

Maintenance alert

Engine temperature, hours since the last inspection, fault codes: a sigmoid neuron combines them into a risk percentage, and the maintenance shop decides above which risk to call the truck in.

Data that doesn’t add up

Fuel use versus load, GPS kilometers versus the odometer: normal usually forms a band, and a single neuron is not enough to watch both sides (idea 5).

The building block of big models

The networks that estimate arrival times or detect drowsiness on a camera are made of thousands of neurons like this one, organized in layers.

With the other topics

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • It is extremely fast and easy to understand: each weight says how much each number counts.
  • If the two groups can be separated by a straight line, the perceptron is guaranteed to find it.
  • With a sigmoid it gives percentages, which are useful for decisions that involve costs.

The not so good

  • It only separates with a straight line (or a plane): the band in idea 5 defeats it.
  • The perceptron stops at the first line that works, not the best one, and if the groups overlap it never finishes.
  • If the numbers are on very different scales, it takes much longer to learn.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Artificial neuron
A calculation: a weighted sum of the data, plus a bias, and an activation at the end.
Weight
How much one number counts in the sum. It can be negative (the number subtracts).
Bias
The starting number of the sum. It shifts the boundary.
Score
The result of the weighted sum plus the bias, before the activation.
Activation
What is done with the score at the end: a step (yes or no) or a sigmoid (percentage).
Decision boundary
The cases with a score of 0: a line that separates the “yes” from the “no”.
Perceptron
A neuron with a step plus Rosenblatt’s rule for learning from its mistakes.
Pass (epoch)
One full run through all the training examples.
Linearly separable
When there is some straight line that leaves all the “yes” on one side and all the “no” on the other.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don’t need it to understand the idea:

score z = w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + b (weighted sum + bias) step review if z > 0 sigmoid p = 1 / (1 + e^(−z)) (z = 0 → p = 50%) boundary w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + b = 0 (a straight line) perceptron, when it gets a day wrong: wᵢ ← wᵢ + 0.1 · s · xᵢ b ← b + 0.1 · s s = +1 if it should have said “review”, −1 if it should have said “OK” first idea: w = (1, 0.2) b = −6 → 5 of 20 on the wrong side after learning: w = (0.83, 1.28) b = −6.0 → 0 of 20 (20 corrections, 6 passes) day with 4.1 harsh brakes and 2.2 h: 0.83 × 4.1 + 1.28 × 2.2 − 6.0 = 3.40 + 2.82 − 6.00 = 0.22 → review, p = 55%
