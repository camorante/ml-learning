Missing data, for programmers

Rubin's taxonomy with the missingness indicator, when complete-case analysis is unbiased, what single imputation does to variances and correlations, multiple imputation and Rubin's rules, chained (MICE) and nearest-neighbor imputation, the missingness indicator as a feature, and sensitivity analysis for the MNAR case. With a from-scratch module that matches scikit-learn, a coverage simulation and a worked example by hand.

Summary

  • The missingness mechanism is modeled with the indicator . Under MCAR, the complete cases are a random sample. Under MAR, the information to correct the bias is in the observed columns. Under MNAR, it isn't.
  • Mean imputation doesn't change the mean but shrinks the variance by a factor and attenuates correlations. Regression imputation inflates them. Both underestimate standard errors.
  • Multiple imputation with Rubin's rules propagates the uncertainty of the gaps: total variance .
  • When the gap is informative, an indicator column or a model with native missing-value handling (boosted trees) exploits it.
  • MNAR cannot be tested with the data: you run a sensitivity analysis (delta adjustment) or look for external information.

§1 · Mechanisms

Rubin's taxonomy

Let be the complete data matrix, split into observed and missing, , and the indicator matrix ( if is observed). Rubin (1976) classifies the mechanism by what depends on:

  • MCAR: nothing. Example: random packet loss.
  • MAR: only the observed data. Example: at small stores (visible) less gets logged.
  • MNAR: the unobserved data. Example: short waits don't get logged.

Under MAR, and if the parameters of and are distinct, the mechanism is ignorable for the likelihood: inference based on the likelihood of (or on imputations that condition on the observed data) is valid without modeling .

Checks. MCAR vs. MAR can be tested: if a column's missingness is predicted by the others (a logistic regression of on the observed columns, or Little's test), it is not MCAR. MAR vs. MNAR cannot be tested with the data: the difference lies in the values you can't see. Only knowledge of the process, external data or a follow-up study on a sample of the missing cases settles it.

§2 · Dropping

Complete-case analysis

Dropping rows with any gap (listwise deletion) is what many libraries do by default. Its properties:

  • MCAR: unbiased, but with a loss of power. With independent columns with a share of gaps, of the rows remain.
  • MAR in general: biased for means and proportions (§9 for the fleet: 48.0 min vs. 43.4).
  • A useful exception: in a regression of on , if missingness depends only on the (not on ), the complete-case coefficients remain unbiased: the model is conditional on .

The pairwise alternative (each statistic with the available pairs) can produce covariance matrices that are not positive semidefinite: avoid it except for exploration.

There is a problem common to all of them: they treat the filled values as if they had been measured. Standard errors come out too small because they ignore the uncertainty of the fill, and confidence intervals don't cover what they claim. The widget measures this with 1,000 simulations.

§4 · Multiple imputation

Several fills and Rubin's rules

You generate complete datasets by drawing the gaps from their predictive distribution , including the uncertainty in the imputation model's parameters (for example, fitting it on a bootstrap of the observed data or drawing from its posterior). You analyze each one and combine the estimates with their variances :

The fraction of missing information, , tells you how much the gaps weigh in the uncertainty. Intervals use a with degrees of freedom (the Barnard-Rubin small-sample correction).

MICE (multivariate imputation by chained equations) handles several columns with gaps at once: start with the mean and, column by column, fit a model of each one on all the others and re-impute, iterating until it stabilizes. scikit-learn's IterativeImputer implements it; with sample_posterior=True and several seeds you get multiple imputations.

§5 · For prediction

Missingness indicator and native gaps

For inference (estimating a mean, a coefficient) the goal is to remove bias. For prediction the goal is different: if missingness correlates with the target, it is a useful feature. Two ways to use it:

  • Indicator: impute (for example, with the median) and add as a column (SimpleImputer(add_indicator=True), MissingIndicator). In classical statistics the indicator method is discouraged for estimating effects, because it introduces bias, but for prediction it is legitimate and often better.
  • Native handling: XGBoost, LightGBM and HistGradientBoosting learn at each split which child to send the gaps to (the "default direction" from the XGBoost topic).

In the fleet (engine temperature missing mostly when the electrical system is failing, which in turn signals breakdowns), the AUC for predicting a breakdown within 30 days is 0.766 filling with the mean, 0.835 adding the indicator and 0.821 with a boosted tree with native gaps. Requirement: the missingness mechanism must be the same in production. If a firmware change makes the sensor stop failing that way, the indicator loses its value overnight (drift, topic 8).

§6 · MNAR

Sensitivity analysis: the delta adjustment

Under MNAR there is no "correct" method with the available data. The honest approach is to parametrize the departure from MAR and see how much the conclusion changes. The delta adjustment imputes under MAR and then shifts the imputed values by an amount :

You present the result as a function of and discuss which range of is plausible according to experts or external data ("unlogged waits are at most 15 minutes shorter"). If the conclusion doesn't change across the whole plausible range, it is robust. More formal alternatives: selection models (Heckman) and pattern-mixture models, which share the same limitation: they rely on untestable assumptions.

§7 · Time series

Carry forward, interpolate or smooth

In series, the temporal context is the best predictor of the gap:

  • Carry forward (ffill): suitable for states that only change through events (ignition, door, configuration). Biased for quantities that drift.
  • Linear or spline interpolation: for smooth quantities in short gaps. It uses information from the future (the value after the gap): in real time it isn't available, and in training it can leak information about the target (topic 1).
  • Kalman smoothing (RTS): fills with the dynamic model and gives a variance for each filled value, which grows toward the middle of the gap (Kalman topic).

Always with a gap limit (limit= in pandas): above a certain duration, or if an event could have happened inside (refueling, a stop), the gap is left flagged.

§8 · From scratch

Imputers verified against scikit-learn

miss.py

import numpy as np

def imputar_media(x):
    x = np.asarray(x, float).copy(); x[np.isnan(x)] = np.nanmean(x); return x

def imputar_grupo_sorteo(x, g, seed=0):
    """Hot deck: each gap gets a random observed value from its own group."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed); x = np.asarray(x, float).copy(); g = np.asarray(g)
    for k in np.unique(g):
        m = g == k; obs = x[m & ~np.isnan(x)]; hu = np.where(m & np.isnan(x))[0]
        x[hu] = rng.choice(obs, len(hu), replace=True)
    return x

def imputar_regresion(x, Z, ruido=False, seed=0):
    """Linear regression on Z; with ruido=True adds a randomly drawn residual (stochastic regression)."""
    x = np.asarray(x, float).copy(); A = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(x)), Z]); m = np.isnan(x)
    b = np.linalg.lstsq(A[~m], x[~m], rcond=None)[0]; x[m] = A[m] @ b
    if ruido:
        res = x[~m] - A[~m] @ b; x[m] += np.random.default_rng(seed).choice(res, m.sum())
    return x

def nan_euclidean(X, Y):
    X, Y = np.asarray(X, float), np.asarray(Y, float); p = X.shape[1]
    D = np.full((len(X), len(Y)), np.nan)
    for i in range(len(X)):
        ok = ~np.isnan(X[i]) & ~np.isnan(Y); d2 = np.where(ok, (Y - X[i]) ** 2, 0).sum(1); n = ok.sum(1)
        D[i] = np.where(n > 0, np.sqrt(p / np.maximum(n, 1) * d2), np.nan)
    return D

def imputar_knn(X, k=5):
    X = np.asarray(X, float); out = X.copy(); M = np.isnan(X)
    for j in range(X.shape[1]):
        rec = np.where(M[:, j])[0]; don = np.where(~M[:, j])[0]
        if not len(rec): continue
        D = nan_euclidean(X[rec], X[don])
        for a, i in enumerate(rec):
            d = D[a]; ok = ~np.isnan(d)
            if not ok.any(): out[i, j] = X[don, j].mean(); continue
            out[i, j] = X[don[ok][np.argsort(d[ok], kind="stable")[:k]], j].mean()
    return out

def imputar_encadenado(X, max_iter=10, tol=1e-3):
    """Deterministic MICE with linear regression: mean start, columns from fewest to most gaps."""
    X = np.asarray(X, float); M = np.isnan(X); Xt = np.where(M, np.nanmean(X, 0), X)
    orden = [j for j in np.argsort(M.sum(0), kind="mergesort") if M[:, j].any()]
    lim = tol * np.max(np.abs(X[~M]))
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        prev = Xt.copy()
        for j in orden:
            otras = np.delete(np.arange(X.shape[1]), j); A = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), Xt[:, otras]])
            b = np.linalg.lstsq(A[~M[:, j]], Xt[~M[:, j], j], rcond=None)[0]
            Xt[M[:, j], j] = A[M[:, j]] @ b
        if np.linalg.norm(Xt - prev, ord=np.inf) < lim: break    # matrix infinity norm, as in sklearn
    return Xt

def rubin(estimaciones, varianzas):
    q = np.asarray(estimaciones, float); u = np.asarray(varianzas, float); m = len(q)
    qb = q.mean(); W = u.mean(); B = q.var(ddof=1); T = W + (1 + 1 / m) * B
    return qb, T, W, B

Under MCAR, only mean imputation gets it wrong (in the tail and in the correlation). Under MAR, the methods that condition on the customer correct the bias of the mean (48.0 → 43.0–43.2) and stochastic regression also recovers the standard deviation. Under MNAR they all end up about 3 minutes off the truth: the remaining bias can only be addressed with the delta adjustment from §6 or with external data (for example, the wait measured by GPS as time stopped at the customer).

§10 · Checklist

Before filling in

  • Percentage of gaps per column and joint patterns (which columns go missing together).
  • Each column's missingness modeled from the others: if it can be predicted, it is not MCAR.
  • Mechanism reasoned from the process that produces the data; MNAR discussed explicitly.
  • No mean imputation if spread, tails or relationships matter.
  • For inference: multiple imputation with Rubin's rules; for prediction: imputation inside the pipeline (training data only), plus an indicator or native gaps.
  • Series: method chosen by the type of quantity, and a gap-length limit.
  • Model evaluated separately on rows with and without gaps.
  • Under MNAR: sensitivity analysis over a plausible range of δ.

§11 · Worked example

Eight deliveries, three gaps and Rubin's rules

Complete cases, mean, group mean and multiple imputation with three fills, all by hand.

The setup. A dispatch manager wants last week's average wait at a customer that has one warehouse and several stores. Of eight deliveries, three have no wait logged, and two of them are at stores.

Example map

Eight deliveries across two customer types; three waits are missing, mostly at the stores, which are the quick ones. We compute the average wait four ways: with complete cases only, filling with the mean, filling by group, and with multiple imputation, where we combine three fills with Rubin's rules.

    They mimic the simulation: warehouses wait around an hour and stores around a quarter of an hour. The gaps are MAR: half are missing at the stores and one in four at the warehouses. Since this is an example, we know the missing values: 58 at the warehouse, 18 and 24 at the stores. The true average wait is minutes (the sum of the eight real waits is 304).

  2. Complete cases only

    The math

    What it means: almost 3 minutes too high. Among the logged data there are 3 warehouses and 2 stores, when in reality there are 4 and 4: the warehouses, which are slow, weigh more than they should. That is the MAR bias.

  3. Filling with the mean: same mean, less spread

    In words

    We put 40.8 in the three gaps. The mean doesn't change, because we add the mean itself three times. But the three new values sit exactly at the center, so the standard deviation drops.

    The math

    Where does the square root come from?

    The sum of squares around the mean is the same (the filled values contribute 0), but it is divided by instead of . With 30% gaps, the standard deviation shrinks by about . The true one, with the 8 real values, is 20.2.

    What it means: the mean is still biased (40.8) and on top of that the data look more homogeneous than they are. Any confidence interval computed this way comes out too narrow.

  4. Filling with each group's mean

    The math

    What it means: 37.0 vs. a true 38.0. The bias almost disappears because we use what causes the gaps: the customer type. The remaining minute is chance (eight values is very few). But the fills still sit at the center of each group: the spread is still underestimated.

  5. Multiple imputation: three fills by random draw

    In words

    Instead of a single fill, we make three. In each one, every gap gets a real wait drawn at random from the logged ones in its own group. That way the fills have the natural variation and, on top of that, they change from one fill to the next, which measures how much we don't know.

    In words

    The final estimate is the average of the three. Its uncertainty adds two things: the normal uncertainty of each mean (W) and how much the means change from one fill to another (B), which is the uncertainty the gaps add.

ejemplo_ocho_descargas.py · reproduces the numbers above

import numpy as np
from miss import imputar_media, rubin

y = np.array([50, 62, 56, np.nan, 15, 21, np.nan, np.nan]); g = np.array(["a"] * 4 + ["t"] * 4)
real = np.array([50, 62, 56, 58, 15, 21, 18, 24.])
print(real.mean(), np.nanmean(y))                                               # 38.0 40.8
print(round(np.nanstd(y, ddof=1), 1), round(imputar_media(y).std(ddof=1), 1))   # 21.3 16.1
gm = {k: np.nanmean(y[g == k]) for k in "at"}
print(np.where(np.isnan(y), [gm[k] for k in g], y).mean())                      # 37.0
q, u = [], []
for r in ([50, 15, 21], [62, 21, 21], [56, 15, 15]):                            # draws by group
    z = y.copy(); z[np.isnan(z)] = r; q.append(z.mean()); u.append(z.var(ddof=1) / len(z))
qb, T, W, B = rubin(q, u)
print(np.round(q, 2), np.round(u, 1))                    # [36.25 38.5  36.25] [50.  53.8 57.5]
print(round(qb, 2), round(W, 1), round(B, 2), round(T, 1), round(np.sqrt(T), 2))  # 37.0 53.8 1.69 56.0 7.48

What would change in a real system

  • Many more imputations (20 or more), drawing the parameters too, not just the values.
  • More columns in the imputation model (pallets, time of day, day of the week): anything that predicts the wait or the missingness.
  • Check the mechanism: if drivers don't log short waits, no fill from the table is enough; the wait measured by GPS is the solution.
