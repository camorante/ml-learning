Markov chains

A system that jumps from one state to another with certain probabilities, where the only thing that matters for the next jump is where it is right now. That alone is enough to make long-term predictions and, with one trick, to guess what you can't see.

Before we start

All that matters is the square you're on

In a board game with dice, your next move depends on the square you're on and on the dice. It doesn't depend on how you got there: it makes no difference whether you crept forward slowly or just fell into a trap. That "lack of memory" is what defines a Markov chain.

Something similar happens with a truck if we look at it every hour: it's on the road, stopped (traffic, a break) or loading. By counting in the history how many times it went from each state to each other state, we get the probabilities of the jumps. With them alone, and no other data, we can predict where it will be and how much time it spends doing each thing.

Idea 1 of 5

The whole chain fits in one table

Each row says: if it's in this state now, how likely it is to be in each state one hour from now. Each row adds up to 100%. That table is the entire model.

Try changing a single cell: the probability of going from "on the road" to "loading". The bars below show how time is split in the long run (the small tick marks the split for the original table). More loading means less road time, but also more stops, because from "loading" the truck sometimes goes to "stopped".

Idea 2 of 5

Predicting hours ahead, and forgetting the start

If we know it's in a given state now, the table tells us how the probability is split one hour from now. Applying it again gives the split two hours from now, and so on. Pick the starting state: in the first few hours it makes a big difference, but after about 6 hours all the columns end up the same. In the long run, the chain forgets where it started.

Idea 3 of 5

How long until the next load?

The same table answers questions about time. For example: on average, how many hours go by from the moment a truck is on the road until it loads again? Simulate lots of trucks and look at the spread. The average comes out at about 8 hours, and there's an exact calculation that gives it without simulating, which you'll find in the technical version. But notice how variable it is: some load the very next hour and others take more than a day.

Idea 4 of 5

Guessing what you can't see: the hidden model

In reality, nobody sends us a "loading" label. What we get is the GPS speed every 15 minutes (still, slow or fast), and it isn't reliable: a truck on the road can crawl in a traffic jam, and a stopped one can show a bit of speed. The true state is hidden.

The naive rule looks at each reading on its own. The hidden Markov model combines two things: what the GPS usually shows in each state, and how long the states usually last. A single "slow" reading in the middle of a fast trip is most likely still road time. The Viterbi algorithm finds the most likely complete history.

Idea 5 of 5

Its weak spot: it has no memory

Having no memory is also a problem. The chain doesn't know how long the truck has been loading, so every quarter hour it rolls the dice as if loading had just begun. The result is that it predicts lots of very short loads. In reality, a load almost always lasts between 1.5 and 3.5 hours.

The fix is to give it some memory by splitting the state into stages: "loading, just started", "halfway" and "finishing". The truck has to go through all three, and the total duration looks much more like the real one.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Define the states and a fixed time step (every hour, every 15 minutes).
  2. Count the transitions in the history: how many times it went from each state to each other state.
  3. Divide each row by its total: that's the probability table.
  4. Predict by applying the table step by step, or go straight to the long-run split.
  5. If you can't see the state, add what you observe in each state and use the hidden model (Viterbi).
  6. Check the durations: if they don't look like the real ones, split the states into stages.

In the real world

What is it used for?

Fleet utilization

What fraction of the time vehicles spend on the road, stopped or in the maintenance shop, and how that would change with a different maintenance policy.

Hidden states in sensors

Recognizing activities (driving, idling, loading) from speed and engine data, or detecting the type of road from the GPS.

Text and speech

For decades, speech recognition and autocomplete were built on Markov models. They're still the foundation of many alignment algorithms.

Timing and reliability

Average time until a breakdown, machine availability, queues at a loading dock.

To be fair

The good and the not-so-good

The good

  • Simple: a table you estimate by counting.
  • Answers lots of questions with exact calculations: the future, the long run, waiting times.
  • Easy to explain to anyone with a diagram of arrows.
  • The hidden version cleans up noisy sensor sequences.

The not-so-good

  • No memory: durations come out unrealistic unless you split states into stages.
  • It assumes the probabilities don't change (not with the hour, not with the day).
  • With many states you need a huge amount of history to estimate the table.
  • A hidden model learned without labels can produce states that are hard to interpret.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

State
Each possible situation of the system (on the road, stopped, loading).
Transition
The jump from one state to another (or to the same one) in one time step.
Transition matrix
The table with all the jump probabilities. Each row adds up to 100%.
Markov property
The future depends only on the current state, not on the path taken to get there.
Stationary distribution
The long-run split of time, which you reach no matter where you start.
Hidden Markov model
A chain whose state you can't see. You only see signals that depend on it.
Viterbi
The algorithm that finds the most likely sequence of hidden states.
For the curious: the formulas behind it

You don't need them to understand the idea:

probability of going from A to B = (times it went from A to B) / (times it was in A) split 1 hour from now = split now × table split n hours from now = split now × table × table × … (n times) long-run split π: the one that doesn't change when multiplied by the table (π × table = π) average time between two visits to a state = 1 / (its share of the long-run split)
