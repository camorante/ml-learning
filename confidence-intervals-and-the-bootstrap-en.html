Confidence intervals and the bootstrap

“This truck burns 32.4 L/100 km on average.” Is that based on 10 trips or 1,000? The number is the same, but it doesn't deserve the same trust. An interval says how wrong a number could be: with a formula when there is one and, when there isn't, by resampling the data itself.

The setup

A trucking company wants to know the average fuel use of one truck model on its regional routes. Every trip gives a different number (more load, more wind, another driver), so they write down the fuel use of a handful of trips and take the average.

The catch: with other trips they'd get another average. How much can you trust the one you got? The answer is a confidence interval: the average, plus or minus a margin. This toy has a trick: since the trips are simulated, we know the true average fuel use of this model, and you can reveal it to see whether the interval gets it right.

How to read the chart. At the top, each orange dot is a trip, placed by its fuel use (the bottom axis, in liters per 100 km; the height means nothing, it just spreads the dots out). Below, the thick blue bar is the interval: it goes from the average minus the margin to the average plus the margin, and the circle is the average. Further down, in gray, the intervals of the samples you drew before.

How many trips?

The more trips go into the average, the less it wobbles and the narrower the interval.

How confident?

The confidence level says how many times out of 100 this method gets it right. More confidence needs a wider interval. The usual choice is 95%.

Another sample and the true value

Draw other random trips of the same truck and compare. When you reveal it, the true average fuel use appears, which in real life you never know.

Try this, in order:

  1. With 10 trips, the average is 32.4 and the margin ±3.2: the true fuel use could be anywhere between 29.2 and 35.6. A bare “32.4” was hiding all that.
  2. Press “Draw another sample” five or six times: each sample gives another average and another interval. Watch them wobble in gray.
  3. Switch to 1,000 trips: the average comes out 31.9 and the margin drops to ±0.28. More data, less wobble.
  4. Press “Reveal the true value” (32.0) and count how many of your intervals catch it. Almost all of them; every now and then, one doesn't.
  5. Change the level to 99% and then to 90%: the interval widens and narrows. Confidence is paid for in width.

What you just saw

A number with its margin

An average computed from a few data points is an estimate: a number that gets close to the true value, but would change with other data. A confidence interval adds how wrong it might be:

  • Estimate ± margin. 32.4 ± 3.2 L/100 km means “the true fuel use is, fairly surely, between 29.2 and 35.6”.
  • The margin depends on three things: how much the data vary (trips very different from each other, more margin), how much data there is (more data, less margin, but slowly: to cut it in half you need four times as much) and the confidence you ask for (more confidence, more margin).
  • The interval can miss. A 95% interval is built with a method that's right 95 times out of 100. The other 5 times, the true value lands outside, and there's no way to know which ones those are.

There are two ways to compute the margin. With a formula, which works very well for averages and percentages (ideas 1 to 4). And by resampling the data itself, the bootstrap, which works for almost any number: a median, dollars per kilometer (ideas 5 and 6). At the end, how to compare two groups (idea 7).

Idea 1 of 7

With a formula: average ± 1.96 standard errors

In topic 11 we saw that the average of n data points “wobbles” from one sample to another, and that this wobble has a bell shape (the central limit theorem) with a width called the standard error: the standard deviation of the data divided by the square root of n. Here it's used the other way around: if the average almost always lands close to the true value, the true value is almost always close to the average.

How close is “almost always close”? In a bell curve, 95% of the time you land less than 1.96 standard errors from the center (topic 8). That gives the formula: average ± 1.96 × standard deviation / √n. This truck's trips vary by about 4.5 L/100 km from each other (their standard deviation, topic 7).

Its width becomes the interval below it. On the right (below on a phone), the margin by number of trips; the orange dot is your choice.

Idea 2 of 7

What “95%” really means

The 95% isn't a property of your interval, but of the method. Imagine 100 different teams each draw their own trips and compute their interval with the same formula. About 95 intervals will catch the true fuel use and about 5 won't. You are one of those teams, and you don't know whether you got one of the good ones.

How to read the chart. Each horizontal bar is the interval of a different sample (the most recent at the bottom). The dashed vertical line is the true fuel use, 32.0. The blue bars catch it; the thicker red ones leave it out. You see the last 100.

What it does mean

  • “If I repeated everything many times, 95% of the intervals I'd get would catch the true value.”
  • A narrow interval says the number is well measured; a wide one, that you need more data.

What it doesn't mean

  • It doesn't say that 95% of the trips fall inside: the interval is for the average, and single trips land outside much more often (idea 3).
  • Nor that the true value “has a 95% probability” of being in this particular interval: it either is or it isn't. That sentence belongs to another way of reasoning, the Bayesian one.
  • And it doesn't cover measurement errors or a badly chosen sample: if all the trips are from summer, the interval is about summer.

Idea 3 of 7

With little data, Student's t: a bit wider

The formula in idea 1 has a trap: it uses the standard deviation of the trips, and in real life that deviation is also computed from the sample. With 10 trips it can come out somewhat smaller or bigger than the real one. If it comes out small, the margin is too narrow and the interval misses more often than promised.

The fix was published in 1908 by a chemist at the Guinness brewery, William Gosset, under the pen name “Student”: replace the 1.96 with a somewhat bigger number that depends on n, taken from the t distribution. It's a bell curve with fatter tails, which looks more like the normal the more data there is.

How to read the chart. The dashed gray curve is the normal bell; the blue one, Student's t for the chosen number of trips. The red areas are the times the average lands more than 1.96 “estimated standard errors” from the true value: the misses from using 1.96. The vertical blue line marks the correct multiplier, the one that leaves out only 5%.

In practice, calculators and software always use the t for averages (with 1,000 data points it gives 1.96 anyway). The toy at the top was already using it: that's why with 10 trips the margin was ±3.2 and not ±2.8. And a warning: the t assumes the trips have a roughly bell-like shape; if a few trips are wildly off, not even the t fixes it.

Idea 4 of 7

Percentages: when the usual formula goes below 0

A new customer has received 40 deliveries and 1 arrived late: 2.5%. How much should you trust that? For a percentage, the usual formula (the Wald one) is: percentage ± 1.96 × √(p × (1 − p) / n), with p as a fraction. With 1 of 40 it gives −2.3% to 7.3%. A negative percentage of late deliveries makes no sense. And with 0 of 40 the margin comes out ±0: “this customer will surely never have a delay”. Not that either.

The problem is that, near 0% (or 100%), the bell of the percentage isn't symmetric: it's squashed against the wall (it's a binomial, topic 8). The Wilson interval takes that into account: it looks for all the true percentages for which what you observed would be normal. It never goes outside 0 to 100% and, when there are no delays at all, it still gives a margin.

How to read the chart. The orange dot is the observed percentage. The purple bar is the Wald interval and the blue one, the Wilson interval. The reddish strip on the left is negative percentages: impossible.

A handy shortcut, the rule of 3: if in n deliveries there hasn't been a single delay, the true percentage is almost surely below 3 / n. With 40 deliveries and no delays, below 7.5%; with 300, below 1%.

Idea 5 of 7

The bootstrap: resampling your own data

Formulas exist for averages and percentages. What about the median? Or a truck's dollars per kilometer, which are total dollars divided by total kilometers? There are formulas, but complicated ones. The bootstrap (Bradley Efron, 1979) skips them with an almost cheeky idea: the best picture we have of “all possible trips” is the trips we have.

So you draw, with replacement, as many trips as there are (here 12) from those same 12: some come out two or three times and others not at all. With that made-up sample you compute the number. You repeat that a thousand times and look at how much it wobbles: the central 95% of those thousand numbers is the interval (the percentile bootstrap).

How to read the chart. At the top, the 12 trips of a delivery truck, with their cost (diesel and tolls) and their km. The last one is a long trip. After each resample, each trip shows how many times it came out (×0 in gray: it didn't come out). At the bottom, the histogram of the chosen number across all the resamples so far; the black line is the number with the 12 real trips and the blue band, the central 95% interval (it appears from 200 resamples on).

The nice part is that the method doesn't change with the number: average, median, a ratio, a percentage, a model's precision… You resample and recompute. The price is doing the calculation thousands of times, which a computer does in an instant.

Idea 6 of 7

When the bootstrap fools you

The bootstrap assumes the data are a good miniature picture of all possible data, and that each data point is independent of the others. When that fails, the interval comes out too narrow and promises a confidence it doesn't have. Three typical cases:

The general rule: resample what is truly independent. If the trips come in trucks, resample trucks; if they are consecutive days of a series, resample chunks of several days (the “block” bootstrap). It's the same idea as splitting by truck before training a model (split before you look).

Idea 7 of 7

Comparing two depots: does the difference include 0?

The trucks at the South depot seem to burn more than those at the North. Is it a real difference or the wobble of the sample? You compute an interval for the difference of averages. If the whole interval is above 0, the South burns more (with the chosen confidence). If it includes 0, the data aren't enough to say: the difference could be zero, or even the other way around.

A detail that fools a lot of people: the margin of the difference isn't the sum of the two margins, but something smaller (the square root of the sum of their squares: the errors of two independent groups rarely both go the worst way at the same time). That's why two intervals that overlap can give a difference that does not include 0.

How to read the chart. At the top, the trips from each depot (North and South) with their average and their interval. At the bottom, the interval of the difference South − North, with the 0 line marked: green if it doesn't include 0 and gray if it does.

This way of looking (is 0 inside?) is the gateway to hypothesis tests and the famous p-values, which are topic 13 of this collection. An interval also shows something the p-value hides: how big the difference is. 1.4 L/100 km over 100,000 km a year is 1,400 liters, about $2,300 per truck at $1.65/L.

Putting it all together

The complete recipe

  1. Never a number on its own: give it with its interval and the number of data points it was computed from.
  2. For an average: average ± t × standard deviation / √n (with lots of data, t ≈ 1.96 for 95%).
  3. For a percentage, especially if it's small or there's little data: Wilson, not Wald.
  4. For any other number (median, ratio, a model's metric): the bootstrap, resampling thousands of times and keeping the central 95%.
  5. Resample what's independent: whole trucks, blocks of days. Be wary with very little data or with maxima and minima.
  6. To compare two groups, the interval of the difference: does it include 0? And what is it worth in dollars?
  7. To know how much data you need: desired margin → n ≈ (1.96 × deviation / margin)². Cutting the margin in half costs four times as much data.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Does the new tire save fuel?

A test with 30 trucks gives savings of 0.8 ± 0.6 L/100 km. Over 100,000 km a year that's between $330 and $2,300 per truck. With the interval in hand you decide whether it's worth the price, or whether you need to test with more trucks first.

On-time rate per customer

A customer with 0 delays in 12 deliveries doesn't have a 0% delay rate: Wilson says it could be as high as 24%. Rankings of customers with few shipments are sorted by the end of the interval, not by the bare percentage.

A model's score

“The breakdown model gets 81% right” on the test set. Is it 81 ± 2 or 81 ± 9? A bootstrap over the trucks in the test set tells you, and keeps you from celebrating one-point improvements that are pure noise.

How many trips to measure

To know the fuel use of a new model with a margin of ±0.5 L/100 km, with trips that vary by 4.5: n = (1.96 × 4.5 / 0.5)² = 311.2, so you need 312 trips. For ±0.25, about 1,245. You work it out before spending money on the test.

To be fair

Where it helps and where it fools you

Where it helps

  • It turns “32.4” into “between 29.2 and 35.6”: it prevents expensive decisions based on numbers that are pure chance.
  • It tells you how much data you need before you collect it.
  • The bootstrap gives an interval for almost any number, with no formulas, in a few lines of code.

Where it fools you

  • It only measures the chance in the sample: it doesn't cover biased data, badly calibrated sensors or trips that don't look like future ones.
  • With dependent data (trips by the same truck, consecutive days) it comes out too narrow if you don't account for it.
  • The “95%” is almost always misread: it belongs to the method, not to your particular interval.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Estimate
A number computed from a sample (the average of 10 trips) to get close to a true value nobody knows.
Standard error
How much the estimate would wobble from one sample to another. For an average, the standard deviation of the data divided by √n.
Confidence interval
Estimate ± margin: the range where, with the chosen confidence level, the true value lies.
Confidence level
How many times out of 100 the method gets it right (90%, 95%, 99%). Higher level, more width.
Student's t
The fat-tailed bell curve used instead of the normal when the standard deviation also comes from the sample. It gives a multiplier somewhat bigger than 1.96.
Wald and Wilson
Two formulas for the interval of a percentage. Wald is the simple one and fails near 0% and 100%; Wilson doesn't.
Bootstrap
Drawing from the sample, with replacement, another sample of the same size; repeating it thousands of times and seeing how much the number wobbles.
Percentile bootstrap
The interval that leaves out the lowest 2.5% and the highest 2.5% of the resampled numbers.
Block bootstrap
Resampling whole groups (trucks, weeks) instead of single data points, when the data within a group resemble each other.
Coverage
The fraction of times an interval method really catches the true value. It should equal the promised level.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need it to understand the idea:

10 trips: sum 324.0 average = 32.4 L/100 km deviation: s = 4.52 (the sample one, with n − 1) standard error: s / √n = 4.52 / √10 = 1.43 with 1.96: 32.4 ± 1.96 · 1.43 = 32.4 ± 2.80 with t (9 d.f.): 32.4 ± 2.262 · 1.43 = 32.4 ± 3.23 → [29.2, 35.6] how much data: n = (1.96 · 4.5 / 0.5)² = 311.2 → 312 for ±0.5 percentage: Wald p ± 1.96 · √(p(1 − p)/n) 1 of 40: 0.025 ± 0.048 → [−2.3%, 7.3%] Wilson (p + z²/2n ± z·√(p(1 − p)/n + z²/4n²)) / (1 + z²/n) 1 of 40 → [0.4%, 12.9%] bootstrap: repeat 1,000 times: resample n data points with replacement and compute the number; interval = percentiles 2.5 and 97.5 difference: (South average − North average) ± t · √(s²_S/n_S + s²_N/n_N)
