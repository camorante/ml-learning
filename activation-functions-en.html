Activation functions

A neuron adds up its inputs with weights and then passes the result through a function: the activation. It looks like a detail, but without it a network with a hundred layers can only draw straight lines. With it, a few neurons bend the line and follow curves like a truck's fuel use as a function of speed.

The setup

Each dot is a route segment driven by one truck of the fleet: its average speed (horizontal) and how much fuel it used (vertical, in liters per 100 km). In town, with stops and starts, it uses a lot; the minimum is around 75 km/h; on the highway it goes back up, because the air pushes back harder. The cloud of dots is shaped like a U.

A small neural network tries to follow that U. It takes the speed, passes it through a few hidden neurons (each one computes a weighted sum, like the neuron in topic 1, and then applies its activation) and returns a fuel use. Each combination of buttons is a different network, already trained: it has found the weights that best follow these 60 segments.

How to read the chart. The orange dots are the 60 segments. The blue line is the fuel use the network predicts for each speed. On the right (below, on a phone) is a diagram of the network: the speed comes in on the left, goes through the hidden neurons, and the fuel use comes out on the right. The little drawing inside each neuron is its activation; thicker lines are larger weights.

Which activation do the hidden neurons use?

None: each neuron passes its sum along unchanged. The other three bend it in different ways; you will see them up close in idea 1.

How many hidden layers?

Each layer has 3 neurons. With 3 layers the network has 34 numbers to tune instead of 10: it should be able to do more.

Try this, in order:

  1. With no activation and 1 layer, the network draws a line that goes down: it says the faster you go, the less you use. It is off by about 4 liters on average.
  2. Switch to 3 layers, still with no activation: more than three times as many numbers and exactly the same line, with the same error.
  3. Pick ReLU: the line bends at elbows and follows the U. The error drops from 4.0 to 1.2 liters.
  4. Try sigmoid and tanh: they also follow the U, with smooth curves instead of elbows.

What you just saw

The activation is what lets the network bend

Without an activation, each neuron only multiplies and adds. And chaining "multiply and add" gives "multiply and add" again: a straight line. It does not matter how many layers you stack; together they are equivalent to a single one. That is why the network with 34 numbers drew the same line as the one with 10.

All it takes is for each neuron to do something that is not a straight line after its sum (for example, "if it comes out negative, make it zero", which is the ReLU) and the layers no longer merge into one. Each neuron adds a bend, and with a few bends the network follows the curve.

  • The activation is a function applied after the weighted sum. The neuron in topic 1 computed a sum; now that sum goes through a curve before it comes out.
  • Without it, more layers are useless. The whole network is still a straight line (or a flat plane, with more inputs).
  • With it, the network can approximate curves: the U of fuel use, the probability of a breakdown versus mileage, whatever is needed.
  • Its slope matters for learning. A network learns by correcting its weights according to the slope of each piece; a flat activation stops passing corrections along.

The ideas that follow show the most common activations (1), why without them two layers are worth the same as one (2), how a few elbows follow the U (3), the slope of each activation (4), what happens with many layers (5) and neurons that switch off for good (6).

Idea 1 of 6

Four ways to bend a number (and two more)

The neuron in topic 1 computes a weighted sum z: for example, a risk score that a delivery will arrive late, based on traffic jams and distance. The activation decides what comes out of the neuron given that score. The best known ones:

  • Step: 1 if z is positive, 0 otherwise. A yes or a no; it is the one used by the perceptron in topic 1.
  • Sigmoid: an S that turns any number into something between 0 and 1. It reads as a probability.
  • Tanh: the same S, but between −1 and 1 and centered at 0.
  • ReLU: if z is negative, 0; if it is positive, z unchanged. The most used one today in hidden layers.
  • Leaky ReLU and GELU: variants of the ReLU. The first lets a tiny bit of the negative part through; the second is a ReLU with a rounded corner, the one used by language models.

Idea 2 of 6

Without an activation, two layers are worth the same as one

A toy network with two neurons in a row. The first takes the speed v and computes w₁ · v + b₁ (a weight and a bias). The second takes what comes out of the first and computes w₂ · (what it gets) + b₂. Between the two there may or may not be an activation.

Without an activation, you can open the parentheses: w₂ · (w₁ · v + b₁) + b₂ = (w₂ · w₁) · v + (w₂ · b₁ + b₂). That is a single neuron with weight w₂ · w₁ and bias w₂ · b₁ + b₂. Four numbers doing the work of two.

How to read the chart. Horizontally, the speed. The dashed purple line is what comes out of the first neuron; the blue one, what comes out of the whole network. If you pick ReLU, the part of the purple line below zero becomes zero before it reaches the second neuron, and the orange dot marks the speed where that starts.

Idea 3 of 6

A few elbows follow the U of fuel use

A ReLU neuron draws an elbow: it is zero up to some speed and from there it rises in a straight line. If the network's output adds up a straight line and a few elbows, each elbow changes the slope of the line from its speed onward. With two or three well-placed elbows, a line that could only go down goes down, flattens out and goes back up.

Here you choose how many elbows and where. The computer chooses the height of the line and how much each elbow tilts it so the line passes as close as possible to the 60 segments (the same thing training would do with those numbers).

Idea 4 of 6

The slope of the activation decides whether the neuron learns

A network learns by correcting its weights little by little. To know how much to correct, it asks each neuron: "if your sum went up a tiny bit, how much would your output change?". The answer is the slope (the derivative, from math topic 16) of the activation at that point. The correction that reaches the neuron is multiplied by that slope; you will see it in detail in topic 5, backpropagation.

If the slope is almost zero, so is the correction: the neuron is saturated and stops learning. That is why the shape of the curve matters, not just what it outputs.

How to read the chart. At the top, the activation (blue) and its tangent at the orange dot: the line with the same tilt. At the bottom, the slope of the curve above at each z. The areas shaded in red are where the slope drops below 0.05: there the neuron barely learns.

Idea 5 of 6

With many layers, the correction fades along the way

In a network with many layers, the correction travels from the output back to the first layer, and at each layer it is multiplied by the slope of the activation. With the sigmoid, that slope never goes above 0.25: after ten layers, what arrives is less than one millionth. The first layers get almost no correction and barely learn. This is the vanishing gradient.

With the ReLU, the slope is exactly 1 when the neuron is active, and the correction arrives whole. That is the main reason the ReLU replaced the sigmoid in hidden layers around 2010.

Idea 6 of 6

ReLU neurons that switch off for good

The ReLU has its own problem. If a neuron ends up with a bias so negative that its sum comes out negative on every segment, it always outputs zero and its slope is always zero. No correction reaches it, so it cannot get out of there: it is a dead neuron. It usually happens when the learning step is too large and one push sends it far away.

The Leaky ReLU avoids this by keeping a small slope (0.01) on the negative side: the switched-off neuron still gets a trickle of correction and can come back.

Activation

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Each neuron computes a weighted sum and then applies its activation.
  2. Without an activation, any number of layers is equivalent to a single one: the network only draws straight lines.
  3. With ReLU, each neuron adds an elbow; with a few elbows the network follows curves like the U of fuel use.
  4. In hidden layers, ReLU (or its variants, Leaky ReLU and GELU) is the default choice.
  5. At the output, the activation is chosen by what you want to get out: sigmoid for a probability between 0 and 1, none for a quantity such as liters.
  6. The slope of the activation decides how much correction gets through: the sigmoid and tanh saturate; the ReLU lets through 1 or 0.
  7. Watch out for dead neurons: smaller learning steps or Leaky ReLU.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Fuel use and emissions

Fuel use as a function of speed, load and road grade is not a straight line. The ReLU networks that estimate it help plan the diesel for each route and spot trucks that use more than expected.

Breakdown probability

A network that warns about breakdowns ends in a sigmoid: its output is a number between 0 and 1 that reads as "a 12% risk this week" and is compared with an alert threshold.

GPS time series

Networks that read speed second by second (the LSTMs of topic 9) use a sigmoid to decide what to remember and tanh for what they remember, precisely because of their ranges, 0 to 1 and −1 to 1.

Estimated time of arrival

Large arrival-time models, with traffic, route and driver, have dozens of layers. They work thanks to activations like ReLU or GELU, which do not switch off the correction in the first layers.

Connections

With the other topics

The weighted sum that goes through the activation is the one from the neuron of topic 1; there the activation was a step. A neuron with a sigmoid at the output is exactly a logistic regression, and its natural penalty is the cross-entropy of math topic 15. Two layers without an activation merging into one is the composition of linear transformations from topic 3: one matrix times another is another matrix. The slopes of idea 4 and the fading correction are the derivatives and the chain rule of topic 16, and those corrections are the steps of gradient descent.

Polynomial regression is another way to bend a line: instead of elbows, powers of the speed. In this collection, the multilayer network has its own topic (3), and backpropagation, which uses the slopes of each activation, is topic 5.

To be fair

What it gives and what to watch

What it gives

  • With a simple activation, layers add up instead of merging: the network can follow almost any curve.
  • The ReLU is very cheap to compute (compare with zero) and lets the whole correction through.
  • The sigmoid gives ready-to-use probabilities at the output.

What to watch

  • The sigmoid and tanh saturate: in deep networks they switch off the correction of the first layers.
  • ReLU neurons can die and stop learning.
  • More bends are not always better: a very flexible network can follow the noise in the data instead of the U.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Activation
The function a neuron applies to its weighted sum before passing the result on.
Nonlinear
Not a straight line. What is needed so the layers do not merge into one.
Hidden layer
A group of neurons between the input and the output of the network.
Step
Outputs 1 if the sum is positive and 0 otherwise. The perceptron's activation.
Sigmoid
An S that maps any number to a value between 0 and 1.
Tanh
An S between −1 and 1, centered at 0.
ReLU
Zero if the sum is negative; the sum unchanged if it is positive.
Leaky ReLU, GELU
Variants of the ReLU: with a little slope on the negative side, or with a rounded corner.
Saturation
A flat zone of an activation, where its slope is almost zero and the neuron barely learns.
Vanishing gradient
The correction becoming tiny as it is multiplied by small slopes layer after layer.
Dead neuron
A ReLU neuron that outputs zero for all the data and no longer gets any correction.
