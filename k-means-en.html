K-means

Group data without anyone telling you the groups: drop a few centers, let each point join the nearest one, move each center to the middle of its points, and repeat until nothing changes.

27

Before we start

Where should the warehouses go?

A company delivers to hundreds of customers and wants to open 3 warehouses. Where should it put them so the trucks drive as little as possible? A very natural method: put the 3 warehouses anywhere; assign each customer to the nearest warehouse; then move each warehouse to the center of its customers. Now some customers have a different warehouse closer to them: reassign them and move again. After a few rounds, nothing changes.

That is K-means. "K" is the number of groups you ask for, and "means" (averages) are the centers, which are always the average of their group. Nobody tells the algorithm which groups exist: it finds them on its own. That is why it is called unsupervised learning.

Idea 1 of 5

Two steps that repeat: assign and move

Eight customers and two warehouses. We start with the warehouses badly placed on purpose: both in the west, on top of customers A and B. Press "Next" and watch them fix themselves.

Each round makes the sum of squared distances from the customers to their warehouse (the inertia) go down or stay the same: assigning to the nearest warehouse never raises it, and the average is exactly the point that makes it smallest. That is why K-means always finishes.

Idea 2 of 5

How many groups? Look for the elbow

K-means doesn't know how many groups there are: you tell it. With more groups the inertia always goes down (with one group per point it would reach zero), so "the lowest inertia" is no way to choose. What you look for is the elbow: the K after which adding another group barely helps.

Idea 3 of 5

The result depends on where you start

K-means always reaches a place where nothing changes, but not always the best one. If two centers start in the same group, they can end up splitting it between them while two other groups share a single center. It gets stuck in a solution that is "good enough for it" but bad.

Two remedies are always used: start with centers spread apart (the k-means++ method, which picks each new center far from the previous ones) and run it several times, keeping the run with the lowest inertia.

Idea 4 of 5

It only sees round groups

Since each point goes to the nearest center, the border between two groups is always a straight line halfway between their centers. That works with round, similar clouds, but it fails with stretched or curved shapes, or with groups of very different sizes.

Idea 5 of 5

Groups don't come with names (and watch the units)

600 trips from a fleet, with their average speed and their kilometers. K-means returns "group 1, group 2, group 3": we are the ones who look at the centers and name them. And since it measures distances, it has the same problem as KNN: if you don't scale, the kilometers (hundreds) crush the speed (tens), and the groups get cut by distance alone.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Scale the features so they all weigh about the same.
  2. Choose K, the number of groups.
  3. Place K starting centers spread apart (k-means++).
  4. Assign each point to the nearest center.
  5. Move each center to the average of its points.
  6. Repeat 4 and 5 until nothing changes; do several starts and keep the one with the lowest inertia.
  7. Look at the centers and name them. Try several K values and choose with the elbow and common sense.

In the real world

What is it used for?

Placing warehouses and zones

Split customers or delivery points into delivery zones, or decide where to put depots and charging stations.

Driving and trip profiles

Group trips or drivers by speed, hard braking, stops and schedules to discover patterns without labeling them by hand.

Customer segments

Groups of customers with similar habits, so you can offer each one what they need.

Compressing images

Reduce millions of colors to 16: each pixel takes the color of the nearest center. It is K-means on colors.

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • Very simple and very fast, even with millions of points.
  • The centers are easy to interpret: "the typical trip of this group".
  • It needs no answers: it discovers structure in unlabeled data.
  • It works as a first step for other models (summarizing, compressing).

The not so good

  • You have to tell it K in advance.
  • It can get stuck depending on the start.
  • It assumes round groups of similar size.
  • Sensitive to units and to extreme values (one very odd point drags its center).

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Group (cluster)
A set of points that are more like each other than like the rest.
Center (centroid)
The average of the points in a group: its "typical point".
K
How many groups you ask for.
Inertia
The sum of the squared distances from each point to its center. The smaller it is, the tighter the groups.
Elbow
The K after which adding groups barely lowers the inertia.
k-means++
A way of choosing the starting centers far from each other.
Unsupervised
Learning without correct answers: the algorithm looks for structure on its own.
For the curious: the formulas behind it all

You don't need them to understand the idea:

inertia = sum, over every point, of (distance to its center)² repeat: assign: each point → the nearest center move: each center → average of its points until no point changes group
