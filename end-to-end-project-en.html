End-to-end project

We put the seven topics together in a real project: from raw fleet data to a list of trucks to inspect every Monday, and then keeping an eye on it while it's in use. You'll see which steps change the result, how a report can promise something that then doesn't happen, and what to do when, months later, something changes in the data.

The setup

A fleet of 300 trucks sends its data every week: kilometers driven, hours since the last maintenance, engine vibration, temperature, fault codes. Every Monday, a model works out the risk that each truck breaks down that week, and the ones above a threshold go to the maintenance shop. A breakdown on the road costs $4,000; an unnecessary inspection, $150.

The data arrives the way it arrives: with repeated messages, impossible temperatures (−40 °C or 255 °C when the sensor fails) and weeks with no vibration reading. We build the model with weeks 0 to 59 (it learns from the first 48 and takes the exam on the next 12) and write a report with what using it would cost. Then we use it for real in weeks 60 to 79 and look at what actually happened.

1 · Clean the data

topics 3 and 4

Yes: remove repeated messages and turn impossible temperatures into a "missing value". No: use the data exactly as it arrives.

2 · Flag the sensor gaps

topic 5

Yes: add a column "vibration was missing" (the sensor often fails before a breakdown). No: fill the gap with the average vibration and forget about it.

3 · History from the past only

topics 1 and 7

The model uses "the truck's breakdowns in the last 12 weeks". Yes: counting only weeks that are already over. No: also counting the week being predicted, which is already in the table but nobody knows on Monday.

4 · Don't rebalance the classes

topic 6

Yes: train on the data as it is, so the risks are realistic. No: give each breakdown the weight of many healthy trucks (and don't correct the risks afterwards).

5 · Alert threshold from costs

topic 6

Yes: alert if the risk goes above 150 / (150 + 4,000) ≈ 3.6%. No: alert if it goes above 50%, the default value in almost every library.

How to read the chart. Two bars of weekly cost for the 300 trucks (missed breakdowns plus unnecessary inspections): the top one is what the report promises, calculated on the exam weeks; the bottom one is what using that model actually cost over the next 20 weeks. The dashed line is the cost of never alerting (just paying for breakdowns). The shorter the bar, the better; if the two bars don't match, the report is lying.

Try this, in order:

  1. With everything on "Yes": the report and reality almost match, and the model saves more than half the cost.
  2. Set step 3 to "No": the report promises a fantastic model, and in reality it barely helps.
  3. Set it back to "Yes" and set step 5 to "No": the model hardly ever alerts and costs the same as not having one.
  4. Now also set step 4 to "No": rebalancing "fixes" the 50% threshold by accident. Set step 5 back to "Yes" and see what happens when you combine the two.
  5. Finally, try steps 1 and 2: they contribute less, but you can see the difference.

What you just saw

A data-prep mistake doesn't throw an error: it gives you a nice-looking report

None of these mistakes makes the program stop or prints a message in red. The model trains, the report comes out with good numbers, and the problem shows up weeks later, when the shop fills up or breakdowns slip through. That's why every step in this series exists: not so the model "works", but so what the report says is what will actually happen.

The steps don't weigh the same. A leakage (step 3) or a badly set threshold (step 5) can turn a good model into a useless one. Cleaning and gaps (steps 1 and 2) are worth a few hundred dollars a week. And some steps can only be judged together: rebalancing (step 4) looks like a good idea with the 50% threshold and is a disaster with the cost-based threshold.

Idea 1 of 3

The whole path

This is the order it pays to work in, with the topic in the series that explains each step:

  1. Split before you look (topic 1): set aside the last weeks as the exam, before touching anything.
  2. Look at the data (topic 2): ranges, distributions, relationships, odd things. Only with the learning part.
  3. Clean the records (topic 3): duplicates, GPS jumps, frozen sensors.
  4. Outliers (topic 4): tell errors apart from real extreme cases.
  5. Gaps (topic 5): ask why values are missing and flag them.
  6. Features (topic 7): scales, categories, cycles and history, all in one pipeline.
  7. Model and threshold (topic 6): measure with costs and choose the alert threshold.
  8. Final exam: just once, with the weeks set aside.
  9. Use it and watch it: the next two ideas.

Idea 2 of 3

Watching the model while it's in use

From week 60 on, the model does its job every Monday. In week 80, without telling anyone, a new telematics box is installed in 120 trucks, and its sensor measures vibration with a different filter: it reads 30% lower. The engines haven't changed; the numbers have. The model thinks those trucks are healthier than they are.

How do we find out? We can't wait to see the breakdowns: they arrive a week late and there are few of them. We also have to watch what arrives earlier: the data coming in and the alerts going out.

How to read the chart. The gray area is how vibration was spread out in the weeks the model learned from; the orange line, how it's spread out in the chosen week. If the orange moves away from the gray, something has changed in the data. Idea 3 of 3

What to do when the alarm goes off

The alarm goes off when week 80 closes. There are three common reactions:

  • Do nothing: keep the same model.
  • Retrain: retrain the model every 4 weeks with all the data that already has an outcome, including the new data.
  • Fix the data: find the source (the new box), work out how far off each truck is by comparing its weeks before and after, and correct its readings before they reach the model. It takes a few weeks of data to calculate.

How to read the chart. Savings each week versus never alerting (4-week average, so it's easier to read): 70% means the model avoids 70% of the cost of breakdowns. The gray line is "do nothing"; the colored one, the reaction you chose. The vertical line is week 80.

Putting it all together

The complete recipe for using it for real

  1. Save the whole pipeline: cleaning, features, model and threshold, with its version and the data it was trained on.
  2. Compute the features the same way in training and in daily use, with what's known each Monday.
  3. Watch the data coming in: how it's spread out, how much is missing, impossible values.
  4. Watch what goes out: number of alerts and average risk. A sudden change is an early clue.
  5. Measure the outcome when it arrives (breakdowns, cost) and compare it with the report.
  6. When an alarm goes off, look for the cause before retraining: a sensor, a software change, a new fleet, a season.
  7. Retrain with a plan: how often, with which weeks, and the same exam before replacing the model.

In the real world

What changes in a fleet without anyone saying so

Hardware and firmware

New telematics boxes, firmware updates, sensors from another manufacturer: same column names, different numbers.

Seasons

Winter pushes breakdowns up and engine temperature changes: what was unusual in summer is normal in January.

The fleet itself

New trucks, new routes, new customers: the model sees cases it had never seen.

The model itself

If the shop inspects the trucks it was alerted about, those breakdowns no longer happen: the new data has fewer breakdowns exactly where the model was right.

Connections

With the models of the ML series

The model in this project is tree boosting, the family of XGBoost: it doesn't need scaling and it handles gaps, but it doesn't escape leakage or a badly set threshold. Logistic regression would be a simpler alternative, and easier to explain to the shop. Bayes' theorem explains why, when the breakdown rate changes, you have to revisit the risks and the threshold. And the Kalman filter is another way to follow a signal that drifts over time.

To be fair

What it costs and what you gain

What you gain

  • A report that holds up.
  • Problems caught in a week or two, not when the shop complains.
  • Decisions (retrain, fix) based on the cause, not on panic.

What it costs

  • More work before training and after deploying than on the model itself.
  • Talking to the people who know the data: telematics, the shop, the business.
  • Alarms can be wrong too: sometimes the change is real and the model is still fine.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Production
Using the model for real, with new data, to make decisions.
Data drift
The incoming data is spread out differently from the training data.
Concept drift
The relationship between the data and what's being predicted changes: the same vibration no longer means the same risk.
Stability index (PSI)
A number that measures how much the spread of a column has changed: 0 means the same, above 0.25 is a major change.
Label delay
How long it takes to find out whether the prediction was right.
Retraining
Training the model again with more recent data.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need them to understand the idea:

cost of a week = missed breakdowns × $4,000 + unnecessary inspections × $150 savings = 1 − cost / cost of never alerting stability index (PSI): split the training data into 10 equal bins and, in each bin, with e = share in training and a = share this week: PSI = sum of (a − e) × ln(a / e)
