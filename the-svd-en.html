The SVD

Any matrix, however strange it looks, always does the same thing: rotate, stretch along two perpendicular directions, and rotate again. That recipe is called the SVD, and it also lets you split a table of data into layers, from the most important to the least, to compress it, clean it, find what's odd and fill in gaps.

The setup

On the plane there is a circle of arrows, all of length 1 and pointing in every direction. A matrix (a table of four numbers, as in topic 3) moves the whole plane: the first column says where the arrow "one step to the right" ends up, and the second, where "one step up" ends up.

Something curious happens: whatever numbers you pick, the circle always ends up as an ellipse (a stretched circle). The SVD explains why: every matrix can be done in three simple steps: first a rotation, then a stretch across and up-down, and finally another rotation. The step buttons do them one at a time.

How to read the chart. The dashed gray circle is the starting one. The little gray lines are the arrows of the circle, which move at each step. The dashed orange line is the destination: what the matrix does in one go. The two thick arrows are special: the blue one is the direction the matrix stretches the most and the purple one, the least. They start at a right angle and stay at a right angle in every step: they end up as the two axes of the ellipse.

What you just saw

Every matrix is rotate, stretch and rotate

That is the singular value decomposition, or SVD. It says that any matrix, of any size, can be done in three steps:

  • A first rotation, which places the direction that will be stretched the most (the blue arrow) on the horizontal axis.
  • A different stretch along each axis. Those factors are called singular values: the big one (σ₁, "sigma one") and the small one (σ₂). They are always positive or zero, and they are sorted from largest to smallest.
  • A second rotation, which moves the ellipse to its final place.

Sometimes one of the "rotations" also includes a reflection, as in a mirror (you'll see it with "reflect"). In a big table the same thing happens, just in more dimensions: instead of two stretches there are as many as the shorter side of the table. The five ideas that follow explain what those stretches mean and why they are so useful with real data: compressing, removing noise, finding what's odd and filling in gaps.

Idea 1 of 5

The singular values are the axes of the ellipse

A matrix doesn't stretch every arrow equally: some come out longer and others shorter. Which one comes out longest? And shortest? The answer is in the ellipse: the longest arrow possible goes from the center to the tip of the long axis, and its length is σ₁; the shortest goes from the center to the tip of the short axis, and its length is σ₂. All the others fall between those two values.

How to read the chart. On the left, the gray arrow of length 1 points in the direction you choose below; the orange one is what the matrix turns it into. The dashed blue and purple lines are the two axes of the ellipse. On the right, the curve says how long the orange arrow is for each direction of the gray one: its highest point is σ₁ and its lowest, σ₂.

0° (right)180° (left)

Two calculations come for free from the axes. The area factor from topic 3 (the determinant) is σ₁ × σ₂, without its sign: the ellipse is a circle of area 1 stretched σ₁ times one way and σ₂ times the other. And the ratio σ₁ / σ₂ says how much an error can grow when you undo the matrix: if one direction is barely stretched (very small σ₂), undoing it means stretching it enormously, and any measurement error with it. That's what happened with the nearly flattened matrix in topic 3, now with a number that measures it.

Idea 2 of 5

A table is a sum of simple layers

Now the matrix is a real table of data: the kilometers driven each day by each of 12 trucks over two weeks. Rows 1 to 5 are long-haul trucks, 6 to 8 regional, and 9 to 12 city delivery. That's 12 × 14 = 168 numbers.

The SVD splits that table into layers. Each layer is the simplest a table can be: a pattern of days (how the work is spread over the week) multiplied by an intensity for each truck (how much of that pattern it does). The layers come out in order: the first one is the closest to the table, the second corrects what's missing, and so on. Adding them all gives the exact table.

How to read the chart. Each row is a truck and each column a day (M, T, W, T, F, S, S of the first week and then of the second). The more blue the cell, the more kilometers. On the left, the data; in the middle, the sum of the first layers; on the right, what's missing to reach the data: orange if the data have more kilometers than the sum, purple if they have fewer.

The last layer added, from the inside. At the top, its pattern of days (bars going up add; going down, subtract). On the left, the intensity of each truck. Each cell of the layer is the number of its row times the number of its column: that's why a whole layer is stored with just 12 + 14 numbers.

Idea 3 of 5

Compressing: keep only the first layers

To store a layer you only need its pattern, its intensities and one scale number. In a table of 48 × 80 pixels (3,840 numbers), one layer is 48 + 80 + 1 = 129 numbers. If a few layers are already enough to recognize the table, you keep those and throw away the rest: that's compression. How much you can compress depends on how much structure the table has.

Idea 4 of 5

What the first layers don't explain: noise or something odd

Go back to the kilometers table. The first layers capture what repeats: the weekly routine and the differences between types of truck. What's left out are the small day-to-day variations (a traffic jam, a detour) and, now and then, something that doesn't fit anything. There are two such things hidden in this data. The layers know nothing about breakdowns, but once you subtract "the normal", the odd stuff stands out on its own.

How to read the chart. It's what's missing (data minus layers), like the right-hand panel of idea 2 but bigger. The rings mark the two cells that are furthest off. Choose how many layers count as "the normal".

14

It's the same idea as "removing noise": if the small layers are almost all noise, the sum of the first layers is closer to reality than the data themselves. And it's another way to look for outliers: instead of looking at each number on its own, you compare each number with what it "should" be according to the rest of the table. A Sunday with 330 km isn't odd for just any truck; it is for a delivery truck that never works on Sundays.

Idea 5 of 5

Filling gaps, PCA and least squares: the same tool

A table of fuel consumption (liters per 100 km) for 8 trucks on 6 types of route has gaps: not every truck has driven every route. If the table is almost a single layer (each truck burns a bit more or less than average, and each route is harder or easier), the known cells are enough to find that layer, and the layer gives a value for every gap. That's the idea behind recommender systems: customer × product, or truck × route.

How to read the table. Each number is a truck's consumption on one type of route. The ? are gaps. In step 2, the layer gives each truck a factor (1.10 = burns 10% more than average) and each route a base consumption; each gap is filled with factor × base, in orange. In step 3, under each filled value you see the real value, which the layer had not seen.

PCA is an SVD. Principal component analysis looks for the directions in which the data vary the most. If you subtract its mean from each column of the table and do the SVD, the patterns of the layers are exactly those directions, and the singular values say how much the data vary along each one. That's why almost every library computes PCA with an SVD.

Least squares with twin columns. In topic 4, two nearly identical columns (load and total weight) made the coefficients jump around. The SVD shows why: there is a direction with a tiny singular value, and undoing divides by it (idea 1). The most stable solution does the SVD and simply doesn't divide by singular values that are too small: it leaves that direction at 0 instead of making up a huge number.

And eigenvectors. If in topic 5 you saw the directions a matrix doesn't rotate, here is their cousin for any matrix: the directions that go in at a right angle and come out at a right angle. For a symmetric matrix (like a correlation table) the directions are the same, and the stretches are the eigenvalues without their sign.

Putting it all together

The complete recipe

  1. Every matrix is rotate, stretch along two perpendicular axes, and rotate. A circle always ends up as an ellipse.
  2. The stretches are the singular values: the axes of the ellipse, from largest to smallest. The big one says how much it stretches at most; the small one, how much at least.
  3. Their product is the area factor, and their ratio, how much errors grow when undoing. A singular value of 0 is a flattening.
  4. A data table is a sum of layers: pattern times intensity, sorted from the one that weighs most to the one that weighs least.
  5. Keeping the first layers compresses and removes noise. No other combination of the same number of layers is closer to the table.
  6. What the layers don't explain is noise or something odd: a good place to look for anomalies.
  7. The same tool fills gaps (recommender systems), computes PCA and solves least squares in a stable way.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Lighter history

A year of hourly activity for hundreds of trucks is a huge table full of routine. Storing its first layers takes a fraction of the space and keeps the shape of the week and of the season.

Days that don't fit

A truck stopped on a Thursday, a trip on a Sunday, a sensor that freezes: they stand out in what the routine layers don't explain, without having to write a rule for each case.

Expected consumption per route

With a few layers of the truck × route table you can estimate how much a truck will burn on a route it has never driven, and spot the one that burns more than it should.

More stable models

When two signals say almost the same thing (load and total weight, GPS speed and wheel speed), the SVD shows the poorly pinned-down direction and lets you not trust it when fitting.

To be fair

Where it helps and where it misleads

Where it helps

  • It works with any table, square or not, without assuming anything about the data.
  • It sorts the layers by importance, and with a few layers there is no better approximation.
  • The singular values tell you at a glance how much structure there is and how much you can compress.

Where it misleads

  • The layers are math, not explanations: sometimes they match something meaningful (the routine) and sometimes they mix things up.
  • Odd values also pull on the layers: with too many, the layers copy them and they stop standing out (idea 4 with 3 layers).
  • It depends on the units: a column in meters weighs a thousand times more than in kilometers. With gaps it can't be applied as is: you need to fit the layers to the known values only, as in idea 5.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

SVD
Singular value decomposition: writing a matrix as rotate, stretch and rotate; or, equivalently, as a sum of ordered layers.
Singular value (σ)
Each of the stretches: the length of each axis of the ellipse from the center to the tip. Always positive or zero, from largest to smallest.
Singular vectors
The special directions: the input ones (which go in at a right angle) and the output ones (the axes of the ellipse). In a table, the patterns and intensities of each layer.
Layer (rank-1 matrix)
A table in which every row is the same list multiplied by a different number: pattern times intensity.
Rank
How many singular values are not 0: how many layers you need to rebuild the exact table.
Rank-k approximation
The sum of the first k layers: the table closest to the original that can be made with k layers.
Condition number
σ₁ / σ₂ (the largest divided by the smallest): how much errors can grow when undoing.
Pseudoinverse
The best way to "undo" a matrix that can't be fully undone: it undoes what it can and sets to 0 what was flattened.
