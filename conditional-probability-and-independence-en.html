Conditional probability and independence

A probability changes when you already know something. If you know it rained, a hard-braking alert is more likely; if you know there was an alert, so is rain. Working it out is easy: keep only the cases that match what you know and count again. Getting it wrong is easy too, and this topic shows the most common traps.

The setup

A trucking company reviews 1,000 trips made by its trucks. For each trip it writes down two things: whether it rained during the trip, and whether the truck triggered a hard-braking alert (the telemetry device sends an alert when the truck brakes sharply).

It rained on 200 trips. There was an alert on 130. And on 50 trips both things happened. With those counts you can answer questions like: if I know it rained, what is the probability that there was an alert? That's a conditional probability: the probability of something given that you know something else.

The orange squares (at the top of each block) are the trips with a hard-braking alert. Whatever doesn't match what you know fades out, and what you count gets a dashed outline.

What the numbers measure

Trips you count: the ones that match what you know. With an alert and with rain: what share of those trips had an alert or rain. That share is the probability, already taking into account what you know.

Try this, in order:

  1. With “Nothing”: out of the 1,000 trips, 130 have an alert, 13%.
  2. Click “It rained”: only the 200 on the left remain, and 50 of them have an alert. Now it's 25%.
  3. Click “It didn't rain”: 80 out of 800, 10%. Knowing something can raise or lower a probability.
  4. Click “There was an alert” and look at the rain: 50 out of 130, 38.5%. It isn't the 25% from before, even though the same 50 trips are counted.

What you just saw

Knowing something means keeping one part and counting again

A probability can be seen as a share of the cases: 130 out of 1,000 trips with an alert, 13%. A conditional probability is the same thing, but counting only inside the group that matches what you know: out of the 200 rainy trips, 50 with an alert, 25%. It's written P(alert | rain) and read “probability of an alert if it rains” (the vertical bar means “given that”).

Three things worth remembering:

  • What you know changes the denominator (the number you divide by): you no longer divide by 1,000, but by the trips that match.
  • Order matters. P(alert | rain) = 25% and P(rain | alert) = 38.5% count the same 50 trips, but divide them by different groups (200 and 130). Mixing them up is one of the most expensive mistakes in statistics.
  • Sometimes knowing changes nothing. If rain had no influence on alerts, the 13% would still be 13% once you know it rained. That's independence.

The ideas below explain each piece, and at the end how this leads straight to Bayes' theorem, which is nothing more than turning a conditional probability around.

Idea 1 of 7

“And” versus “if”: the same cell, three divisions

The table below is the toy in numbers: each cell counts the trips that match its row and its column at the same time. A single cell gives three different probabilities depending on what you divide it by. Dividing by all trips answers “what share has both things?” (the probability of A and B). Dividing by its column or its row answers “what share, if I already know one of the two?”.

Idea 2 of 7

The tree: multiply along the branches and add at the end

Sometimes there's no table, just loose facts: “this month it rains on 20% of trips”, “when it rains, there's an alert on 25%”, “when it doesn't, on 10%”. A tree rebuilds the table. First you split by rain and then, inside each branch, by alert. Two rules are enough:

  • Product rule: the probability of a path is the product of its branches. Rain and an alert: 20% × 25% = 5%.
  • Total probability: the probability of an alert is the sum of all the paths that end in an alert. 5% + 8% = 13%.

How to read the chart. Read it from left to right. Each branch carries its probability, and its thickness is proportional to the share of trips that go through it. On the right, the end of each path with its calculation: the orange ones end in an alert. The four ends always add up to 100%.

Idea 3 of 7

Turning it around doesn't give the same thing

The trucks' diagnostic system sends an alert when it detects something odd in the engine. The manufacturer boasts: “if the truck has a breakdown, the alert goes off 90% of the time”. That's P(alert | breakdown). But the maintenance shop manager gets an alert and asks the opposite: if the alert goes off, what is the probability that there's a breakdown? That's P(breakdown | alert), and it can be very different.

Mixing up the two is called the prosecutor's fallacy: in a trial, “if he were innocent, it would be very unlikely for his DNA to match” does not mean “it is very unlikely that he's innocent”.

How to read the chart. Each square is one truck in one week. Solid orange: it has a breakdown and the alert went off. Hollow orange: it has a breakdown and the alert didn't go off. Purple: the alert went off, but the truck was fine (false alarm). Gray: healthy and no alert. The buttons let you keep only the breakdowns or only the alerts.

What you just did, counting the trucks with an alert and seeing how many have a breakdown, is exactly Bayes' theorem: from what the manufacturer says (90% and 5%) and how common breakdowns are (2%), you compute what matters to the shop (26.9%). Idea 4 of 7

Independent: knowing one thing doesn't change the other

Two things are independent if knowing that one happened doesn't change the probability of the other. With rain and alerts it would mean: alerts happen on the same percentage of trips whether it rains or not. Then something handy happens: the table “multiplies”. The share of trips with rain and an alert is the share with rain times the share with an alert.

How to read the chart. It's the table drawn with areas (a mosaic). The narrow column is the trips with rain (20%) and the wide one, the rest. Inside each column, the orange part is the share with an alert. The dashed line marks the alert rate across all trips together. If both columns have the orange at the same height, rain tells you nothing about the alert: they're independent.

Idea 5 of 7

Independent is not the same as mutually exclusive

Many people think two things are independent when they “have nothing to do with each other” or “can't happen together”. The second one has another name: they're mutually exclusive (if one happens, the other can't). And two mutually exclusive things are the exact opposite of independent: knowing that one happened tells you a lot about the other, which is now impossible.

Practice with these cards: in each one, A and B are two things that can happen. Choose what they are.

How to read the chart. The same mosaic as in the previous idea, for the last card you answered. The left column is “A happens”; the orange part, “B happens”. Independent: the orange at the same height in both columns. Mutually exclusive: no orange at all in A's column. Neither: different heights, but some orange in both.

Idea 6 of 7

Independent “inside”: conditional independence

Two alerts for the same driver, hard braking and speeding, seem to go together: on days with hard braking there's more speeding. But the company has two kinds of drivers: rookies, who trigger lots of alerts of both kinds, and veterans, who trigger few. If you look at each kind separately, the two alerts no longer have anything to do with each other. They're said to be independent given the type of driver.

How to read the chart. Each working day of a driver goes into one of two columns: days with some hard braking (left) and without it (right). The orange part is the share with some speeding. With all drivers together, the orange is higher on the left. Pick a single type of driver and see what happens.

This idea is the basis of a very common classification model, Naive Bayes: to decide whether a truck has a breakdown from several signals at once, it assumes the signals are independent given whether there's a breakdown or not. That way it just multiplies what each signal says on its own. It's almost never entirely true, but it works surprisingly well.

Idea 7 of 7

Simpson's paradox: wins every group and loses overall

Two depots, North and South, make 1,000 deliveries each. Some are in the city (traffic jams, narrow streets: harder to arrive on time) and others on the highway. North arrives on time more often than South in the city (75% vs. 70%) and also on the highway (95% vs. 90%). And yet, counting all deliveries together, South comes out ahead: 86% vs. 79%. There's no arithmetic mistake.

How to read the chart. One panel per depot. Each bar is a route type: blue city and purple highway. The height is the % of on-time deliveries (the axis starts at 50% so the differences show) and the width, how many deliveries of that type the depot makes. The dashed line is the depot's total: an average of the two heights in which the wider bar counts more.

Which one to believe? Here, the comparison by route type: the route type influences both which depot makes the delivery and whether it arrives on time, so lumping everything together compares different things. The general rule isn't “always split”: it's to think about which variable explains the other two before deciding which table to look at. An ML model trained on the totals would inherit the same wrong conclusion.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. A probability is a share of the cases, and a conditional one, a share of a group: keep the cases that match what you know and count again.
  2. Tell “and” from “if”. P(A and B) divides by everything; P(A | B), only by the cases with B.
  3. Don't turn it around carelessly. P(alert | breakdown) and P(breakdown | alert) can be 90% and 27%. To go from one to the other you need to know how common each thing is: that's Bayes.
  4. Tree: multiply along the branches (product rule) and add up the paths that end the same way (total probability).
  5. Independent: knowing one doesn't change the other, and the table multiplies. Mutually exclusive is something else: they can't happen together.
  6. Independence can depend on the group. Two signals related across the whole fleet can be independent within each type of driver.
  7. Before comparing totals, look at the groups: a different mix can flip the conclusion (Simpson).

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Maintenance alerts

What matters to the shop isn't how many breakdowns the system detects, but how many alerts turn out to be breakdowns: P(breakdown | alert). With rare breakdowns, even with a good sensor, most alerts are false, and every inspection costs money (about $150).

Risk by conditions

P(incident | rain), P(incident | night), P(delay | roadwork on the route): comparing each conditional with the overall probability tells you which conditions deserve a warning to the driver or a different insurance price.

Comparing depots, routes or drivers

Before rewarding or singling out someone for their percentage of on-time deliveries or of alerts, you have to compare within similar groups (same route type, same shift). Otherwise, Simpson shows up.

Combining signals

Vibration, temperature and fault codes: a Naive Bayes-style classifier combines them assuming they're independent given the truck's condition. Knowing when that assumption fails (two sensors that measure the same thing) keeps you from counting the same clue twice.

To be fair

Where it helps and where it misleads

Where it helps

  • Counting in a table turns almost any probability question into a simple division.
  • The tree lets you rebuild the table from loose facts and answer “what if the mix changes?” (more rain, more rookies).
  • Independence, when it holds, simplifies things enormously: you just multiply.

Where it misleads

  • With few cases in the group, the conditional jumps around: 3 out of 10 is not a reliable 30%.
  • Turning a conditional around without accounting for how common each thing is leads to badly wrong conclusions.
  • Two things going together doesn't mean one causes the other, and a hidden third variable can reverse the conclusion (Simpson).

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Conditional probability
The probability of something given that something else happened: P(A | B), “A if B”.
Joint probability
The probability that both things happen at once: P(A and B).
Contingency table
A table that counts the cases for each combination of two variables (rain yes/no × alert yes/no).
Product rule
P(A and B) = P(B) × P(A | B): multiplying along the branches of the tree.
Total probability
Adding up the paths that end in the same place to get its overall probability.
Independent
Knowing one doesn't change the other: P(A | B) = P(A). The table multiplies.
Mutually exclusive
They can't happen at the same time: P(A and B) = 0.
Conditional independence
Independent within each group (given the type of driver), even if they aren't when groups are mixed.
Simpson's paradox
A comparison that comes out the opposite way in the total than in each group, because of a different mix of groups.
Bayes' theorem
The rule for turning a conditional around: from P(alert | breakdown) to P(breakdown | alert).
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need them to understand the idea:

conditional P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B) P(alert | rain) = 50 / 200 = 25% P(rain | alert) = 50 / 130 = 38.5% product rule P(A and B) = P(B) · P(A | B) = P(A) · P(B | A) 0.20 · 0.25 = 0.13 · 0.385 = 0.05 total probability P(A) = P(B) · P(A | B) + P(not B) · P(A | not B) 0.20 · 0.25 + 0.80 · 0.10 = 0.13 Bayes P(B | A) = P(B) · P(A | B) / P(A) P(breakdown | alert) = 0.02 · 0.90 / (0.02 · 0.90 + 0.98 · 0.05) = 0.018 / 0.067 = 26.9% independence P(A | B) = P(A) ⇔ P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B) mutually exclusive P(A and B) = 0 (if P(A) and P(B) > 0, they're not independent) Simpson total = city share · % city + highway share · % highway North: 0.8 · 75% + 0.2 · 95% = 79% South: 0.2 · 70% + 0.8 · 90% = 86%
