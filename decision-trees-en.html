Decision trees

A model that decides the way a person would: by asking yes-or-no questions, one after another, until it reaches an answer.

Before we start

Something you already know how to do

Have you ever played "Guess Who?" You don't ask "Is it Anna?", "Is it Luis?", "Is it Martha?"… You ask "Do they wear glasses?" With a single question you rule out half of them. Then "Do they have blond hair?", and so on until only one is left.

A mechanic does the same thing: "Does the engine start?" → no → "Do the lights come on?" → no → "It's the battery." A decision tree is exactly that: a chain of yes-or-no questions that ends in an answer.

The difference is that the mechanic learned those questions over years of experience. A decision tree learns them on its own by looking at past cases: it searches for the questions that best separate the answers.

Idea 2 of 5

Which question goes first? The one that leaves the "purest" groups

A group is pure if all its deliveries have the same answer. There's a simple trick to measure how mixed up a group is: pick two deliveries at random. What's the chance that one is "late" and the other is "on time"? If the group is pure, zero. If it's half and half, the highest possible.

The tree tries every possible question and keeps the one that leaves the least mix-up overall. Move the distance cut and find the best one yourself:

Idea 3 of 5

Asking too much is memorizing

If you let the tree ask as many questions as it wants, it ends up with a leaf for every odd case: it gets 100% of the data it learned from right and fails on new data. It's the same overfitting as in polynomial regression, in a different costume.

Data to learn from New data (the real exam)

The cure is called pruning: limiting how many questions in a row the tree may ask, or requiring each leaf to have a minimum number of cases. A smaller tree makes a few more mistakes on what it has already seen, but gets more right on what's new.

Idea 4 of 5

It can only cut in straight lines

Each question looks at just one thing at a time ("distance ≤ 20?"), so the cuts are always horizontal or vertical. If the real boundary is diagonal, the tree imitates it with a staircase: it needs many questions for something a straight line would do with one.

Idea 5 of 5

A tree is jumpy

Change the data a little (another sample of the same problem) and the tree may change completely: a different first question, different cuts. That instability is its big weakness.

The fix, which we'll see in the next topic, is brilliant in its simplicity: instead of one tree, train hundreds, each on a different sample, and let them vote. Their individual jitters cancel out. That's a Random Forest.

Putting it all together

The complete recipe

  1. Start with all the cases in a single group.
  2. Try every possible question ("distance ≤ 10?", "≤ 11?", "rush hour?"…) and pick the one that leaves the two purest groups.
  3. Repeat in each group separately.
  4. Stop when a group is pure, is very small, or the maximum depth is reached.
  5. Each leaf answers with the most common answer among its cases (and its share works as a probability).
  6. Choose the depth by looking at accuracy on new data, not on the learning data.

In the real world

What is it used for?

Rules you can explain

Banks, insurers and hospitals use them when every decision has to be justified: the path through the tree is the explanation.

Diagnosis and maintenance

Which combination of fault codes, mileage and temperature predicts a breakdown? A small tree sums it up in rules a maintenance shop can follow.

Exploring data

A quick tree shows which features matter and at which thresholds behavior changes, before you build a more complex model.

The foundation of the champions

Random Forest and Gradient Boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM), the models that win most often on tabular data, are teams of trees.

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • You can read and explain it: every prediction is a path of questions.
  • It doesn't care about the scale of the data: no need to standardize.
  • It mixes numbers and categories ("rush hour: yes/no") with no trouble.
  • It picks up combinations ("far away and at rush hour") without being told.

The not so good

  • It overfits easily if it isn't pruned.
  • It's unstable: a small change in the data changes the tree.
  • It only cuts in straight lines: diagonal boundaries are hard for it.
  • On its own, it's rarely the most accurate model. As a team (forests), it is.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Node
Each question in the tree.
Root
The first question.
Leaf
The end of a path: the answer.
Depth
The maximum number of questions in a row from the root to a leaf.
Purity
How alike the answers within a group are.
Gini
The most widely used measure of mix-up: the probability that two random cases from the group have different answers.
Pruning
Trimming the tree so it doesn't memorize.
Overfitting
Learning the data by heart instead of the general rule.
For the curious: the formulas behind it all

You don't need them to understand the idea:

mix-up of a group (Gini) = 1 − (share "late")² − (share "on time")² quality of a question = average mix-up of the two groups it leaves, weighted by the size of each group the tree picks the question with the lowest value
