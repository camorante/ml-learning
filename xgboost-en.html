XGBoost

Boosting, made ready for production. The idea is the same (each tree fixes what the team before it got wrong), but with a brake against getting too complicated. It knows when to stop, copes with missing data and can explain every one of its predictions. It is the model that wins most often on data in tables.

Before we start

A team of apprentices with a demanding coach

In the Random Forest and Boosting topic we saw the idea: the first tree makes a rough prediction, the second one looks only at what the first got wrong, the third at what the team of two got wrong, and so on. Each one adds a small correction.

XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) adds a demanding coach. Each new tree pays a fine for getting complicated (every extra branch and every big correction costs something), it keeps only the branches that are worth it, and training stops as soon as the team stops improving on data it has not seen. It also comes ready for real life: empty values, millions of rows and the question "why did the model say that?".

Idea 1 of 5

A fine for getting complicated

Here there is a single tree, with up to 5 levels of questions. Each split divides the trips into two groups and improves the prediction a bit. The pruning control (gamma) demands a minimum improvement to keep a split: the ones that fall short disappear. The caution control (lambda) shrinks the value of each leaf, especially leaves with few trips, which are the least reliable.

Idea 2 of 5

Missing data: let the model decide

In telemetry something is always missing: a sensor that disconnects, a reading that never arrives. Many models do not accept gaps, and the usual fix is to drop those rows or fill them in. XGBoost takes them as they are: at each split it tries sending the empty values to the left and to the right, and keeps whichever helps more.

In this data, the temperature sensor fails mostly on old trucks, which burn more fuel. The fact that the value is missing is already information. If you drop those rows for training, the model never learns that "no value" usually means "old truck", and when one of them shows up it predicts normal fuel use.

Idea 3 of 5

Why did it say that? Explaining each prediction

A model with hundreds of trees cannot be read. But you can split each prediction fairly among the features: how much the predicted fuel use went up or down because of the load, the grade, the speed… That split is called SHAP. It starts at the fleet's average fuel use and each feature adds or subtracts, until it lands exactly on the prediction. Idea 4 of 5

Common sense: "more load, more fuel"

With little data and some noise, an unconstrained model can learn absurd things: that over some range, carrying more makes the truck burn less. You can give XGBoost a direction constraint: predicted fuel use never goes down when the load goes up. Its predictions will be more reliable and easier to defend in front of a customer.

Idea 5 of 5

Its weak spot: it won't venture beyond what it saw

Trees answer with steps, and past the last data point the step just stays flat. If the model only saw trips below 80 km/h, at 110 km/h it will say the same as at 80, even though real fuel use keeps rising. To predict outside the known range you need models with a shape (a regression) or, better, data that covers that range.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Set aside validation data that the model does not see while learning.
  2. Start from the average and add small trees that fix what is wrong, in short steps.
  3. Charge for complexity: each split must improve things enough, and each leaf shrinks a little.
  4. Stop when validation stops improving (early stopping).
  5. Leave empty values empty and add common-sense constraints if you know them.
  6. Explain the predictions with SHAP before showing them to anyone.

In the real world

What is it used for?

Data in tables

Fuel use, delays, risk of a breakdown or of a customer leaving: if the data is rows and columns, it is usually the first thing to try.

Explainable insights

With SHAP, each prediction comes with its reasons ("it burns more because of the grade and the idling"), ready for a customer report.

Competitions

Together with its cousins LightGBM and CatBoost, it has won more competitions on tabular data than any other method.

Ranking and prioritizing

Which trucks to inspect first, which routes to optimize: it can also learn to rank, not just to predict values.

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • Usually the most accurate on data in tables.
  • Fast even with millions of rows, and there is no need to scale the features.
  • Tolerates empty values and accepts common-sense constraints.
  • Every prediction can be explained with SHAP.

The not so good

  • Lots of knobs to tune (although the defaults already work well).
  • Overfits if you do not use early stopping.
  • Does not extrapolate beyond the range of the data.
  • For images, sound or long text, neural networks are better.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Round (or tree)
Each correction added to the team.
Learning rate (eta)
How much of its correction each tree applies. With small steps, learning is slower but finer.
Early stopping
Stop adding trees when the error on new data stops going down.
Gamma
The minimum improvement a split must give to be kept.
Lambda
How much the leaf values are shrunk, especially for leaves with little data.
SHAP
A fair split of a prediction among the features, adding up exactly to the prediction.
Monotonic constraint
Forcing the prediction to only go up (or only go down) when a feature goes up.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need them to understand the idea:

value of a leaf = − (sum of errors in the leaf) / (trips in the leaf + lambda) × learning rate improvement of a split = quality(left) + quality(right) − quality(no split) quality of a group = (sum of errors)² / (trips + lambda) the split is kept if its improvement exceeds gamma
