Random forest and boosting

A single decision tree is jumpy and makes plenty of mistakes. Many trees together are among the most accurate models there are. There are two ways to build the team: have them all vote at once, or have each one fix the one before it.

Before we start

The wisdom of the crowd

In 1906, at an English livestock fair, about 800 people bet on how much an ox weighed. Almost nobody got it right: some guessed far too high, others too low. But the statistician Francis Galton took the average of all the guesses, and it was less than half a kilo off the real weight.

Why? Because each person's errors went in different directions, and when you average them, they cancel out. That is the whole idea behind random forest.

Idea 2 of 5

Where do the different samples come from? Drawing with replacement

We only have one dataset. To give each tree a "different" one, we use a trick: put the cases in a bag, draw one, write it down and put it back, and repeat as many times as there are cases. Some come out two or three times; others, never.

This is called the bootstrap. Try it with 10 cases:

About one third of the cases are always left out (37%). And that's a gift: those cases the tree never saw can be used to test it for free, without setting data aside. They're called out-of-bag cases.

Idea 3 of 5

Voting only helps if they make different mistakes

Picture a jury where each member is right 65% of the time. If each one thinks for themselves, the majority is right far more often than any one of them. But if they all copy the same person, voting does nothing: the jury is only as good as one member.

That's why random forest adds a second trick: at each question, each tree may only choose among a few features picked at random. That way they don't all start by asking about the same "star" feature, and their mistakes look less alike. Different data + different questions = different trees.

Idea 4 of 5

Boosting: each tree fixes the one before it

The other way to build a team is the opposite: instead of many independent trees, tiny trees, one after another. The first one makes a rough prediction. The second one doesn't look at the original data but at the first one's errors, and tries to predict them. The third, the errors that are left. And so on.

It's like an artist who makes a sketch and then, pass after pass, only touches up what's wrong. Press "Add a tree":

Idea 5 of 5

Boosting can memorize too

If you keep adding corrections, the model ends up chasing the noise in each point: the error on the training data drops to almost zero, but on new data it starts to go up. You have to stop in time. In practice, you set aside a slice of the data and stop when its error stops going down (early stopping).

Putting it all together

Two recipes for a team of trees

Random forest (all at once)

  1. Draw a sample with replacement from the data.
  2. Learn a big tree from it; at each question, it may only choose among a few random features.
  3. Repeat hundreds of times. Each tree is independent: they can all be trained at the same time.
  4. Predict by voting (or averaging, if it's a number).
  5. Evaluate with the out-of-bag cases. More trees never make it worse: they only take more time.

Boosting (one after another)

  1. Start with a simple prediction: the average.
  2. Compute the errors the model makes so far.
  3. Learn a small tree that predicts those errors.
  4. Add it to the model, but only a little bit (the learning rate).
  5. Repeat, and stop in time, when the error on held-out data stops going down.

Random forest calms down big, jumpy trees (it removes variance). Boosting sharpens small, clumsy trees (it removes bias). That's why a forest pretty much works "as is", and boosting usually wins if it's tuned carefully.

In the real world

What is it used for?

Arrival times

Estimating how long a delivery or a trip will take from the route, the time, the day, the weather and the driver's history. Boosting is the industry standard.

Predictive maintenance

Combining hundreds of vehicle signals (fault codes, temperatures, kilometers, usage) to anticipate breakdowns, better than with a handful of rules.

Fraud and risk

Banks and insurers score every transaction in milliseconds with XGBoost or LightGBM.

The king of tables

With data in rows and columns (not images or text), teams of trees still win most competitions and projects.

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • Very accurate on tabular data, with hardly any data preparation.
  • They inherit the good parts of trees: they mix numbers and categories and don't care about scale.
  • Random forest works well with almost no tuning and tests itself (out-of-bag).
  • They tell you which features weigh most in their decisions.

The not so good

  • You can no longer read them like a single tree: they're hundreds of trees.
  • Boosting has lots of knobs and overfits if you don't stop it in time.
  • They can't extrapolate: outside the range of the data, they repeat the last value they saw.
  • Slower and heavier than a single tree or a regression.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Ensemble
A model made of many models combined together.
Bootstrap
Drawing a sample the same size as the data, with replacement.
Bagging
Training one model on each bootstrap sample and averaging them. Random forest is bagging of trees plus the random-features trick.
Out-of-bag (OOB)
The cases a sample didn't include; they're used to test that tree.
Boosting
Chaining small models, each one fixing the errors the previous ones left behind.
Residual
The error left at a point: the real value minus the predicted one.
Learning rate
What fraction of each correction gets added. Small = more trees, but a better result.
Early stopping
Stopping adding trees when the error on held-out data stops going down.
XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost
Very fast libraries for boosting with trees.
For the curious: the formulas behind it

You don't need them to get the idea:

Random forest: prediction = average of the predictions of the B trees Boosting: model₀ = average of the data error_i = real_i − model(x_i) new tree = the one that best predicts those errors new model = model + rate × new tree
