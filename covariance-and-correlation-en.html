Covariance and correlation

Do trucks that carry more load burn more fuel? Do drivers with more years of experience brake hard less often? Two numbers tell you whether two columns of data move together, in which direction and how strongly. What they never tell you is why.

The setup

A trucking company has a table with one row per truck and one month of data: the average load, the fuel consumption, the hours the engine runs while the truck is standing still (idling), the money spent on diesel, the driver's years of experience, the harsh braking events…

Each pair of columns can be drawn as a scatter plot: each point is a truck, with one column across and the other one up. The question is always the same: when one column is above normal, is the other one usually above normal too (they move together), usually below (they move in opposite directions), or does it not care?

How to read the chart. Each point is one of the 120 trucks. With the cross on, the two gray lines are the mean of each column and they split the chart into four zones. In the blue zones (top right and bottom left) the two columns "agree": both above their mean or both below. In the purple ones, one is above and the other below. The rectangles go from the cross to each truck (they are explained in idea 1).

Which two columns?

Four pairs from the same fleet. Each one moves in a different way.

What to draw on top?

Start with the cross of the means: it is the key piece. The rectangles are the covariance calculation turned into a drawing.

The two numbers

The covariance has the right sign (plus or minus) but its units are weird. The correlation r always goes from −1 to 1: 1 is a straight line going up, −1 one going down, and 0, no line at all.

Try this, in order:

  1. With "Load and consumption" and the cross: almost 8 out of 10 trucks fall in the blue zones. The cloud rises to the right and r = 0.74.
  2. Switch to "Experience and braking": now the purple zones win and r is negative, −0.65. More experience, less harsh braking.
  3. Try "Day of the month and consumption": half and half, r = 0.02. No relationship.
  4. Turn on "The rectangles" and compare "Load and consumption" with "Idling and fuel spend": the covariance of the second is more than a hundred times larger, but the clearer relationship is the first one. Covariance depends on the units; r doesn't (idea 2).

What you just saw

Two numbers for "they move together"

To know whether two columns move together you don't need to look truck by truck. It's enough to check which side of its mean each truck falls on in each column:

  • The covariance adds up, with a sign, how much the two columns "agree" on each truck. If it comes out positive, they move together; negative, in opposite directions; close to zero, there is no clear direction. Its problem: it comes in weird units (tonnes times liters, hours times dollars) and its size doesn't say whether the relationship is strong.
  • The correlation r is the covariance without units. It always goes from −1 to 1 and can be compared across pairs: 0.74 between load and consumption is a clearer relationship than 0.52 between idling and fuel spend, even though the covariance says the opposite.

A rough guide: below 0.1 (positive or negative) there is nothing; around 0.3, something weak; around 0.5, moderate; from 0.7 up, strong. But the number alone is not enough: always look at the cloud, because r only sees straight lines (idea 3), a single truck can change everything (idea 4) and, above all, it doesn't say what causes what (idea 5).

Idea 1 of 6

Rectangles with a sign: this is how covariance is computed

We take just 8 trucks, with their average load (tonnes) and their consumption (liters per 100 km). For each truck we look at how far it is from the mean in load and in consumption (these are the deviations from topic 7) and we multiply the two. If it is off to the same side in both (more load and more consumption, or less and less), the product is positive. If it is off to opposite sides, negative.

That product is exactly the area of a rectangle that goes from the cross of the means to the truck. All the areas are added up with their sign and divided by n − 1 = 7 (the same n − 1 as in topic 7). The result is the covariance.

How to read the chart. The gray cross marks the means (at the start, 15 tonnes and 32 L/100 km). Each rectangle goes from the cross to a truck: blue if its product is positive (it counts in favor of "they move together") and purple if it is negative (it counts against). Drag any truck with the mouse or your finger and watch its rectangle, the cross and the covariance change.

Idea 2 of 6

Removing the units: the correlation r

The covariance of the 8 trucks is 18.6, in "tonnes times liters per 100 km", which is nothing you can picture. Worse: if the load is recorded in kilograms instead of tonnes, all the rectangles stretch 1,000 times and the covariance becomes 18,571. Same trucks, same relationship, a different number.

The fix is to measure both columns with the same yardstick: turn each value into a z, that is, into "how many standard deviations it is from its mean" (topic 7). The covariance of the z's has no units and always falls between −1 and 1: it is the Pearson correlation, r. It is the same as dividing the covariance by the two standard deviations.

How to read the chart. These are the 8 trucks from idea 1. When you change the units, the numbers on the axes change, but the points don't move: the relationship is the same. In "In z", both axes measure standard deviations and the cross sits at 0. The blue line is the line that best fits the points (the one from linear regression).

In which units?

The line

The best-fitting line has a slope that comes from r. Turn it on and switch to "In z".

Another way to see r, which comes from topic 2: put the 8 load deviations in one arrow of 8 numbers and the 8 consumption deviations in another. r is the cosine of the angle between the two arrows. If they point the same way, r = 1; if they point in opposite directions, −1; if they form a right angle, 0. That's why r never leaves the range −1 to 1.

Idea 3 of 6

r only sees straight lines

A truck's consumption depends a lot on its average speed: at traffic-jam pace it burns a huge amount (starting, braking, engine running while stopped); around 50 km/h it burns the least; and faster than that it burns more again, because pushing the air costs fuel. The relationship is very strong, but it is shaped like a U, not a line. And r only measures how much the points look like a straight line.

How to read the chart. Each point is a trip. With the sliders you choose which trips go into the calculation: the ones inside show in orange over the shaded band; the ones outside, in gray. The blue line is the best line for the trips inside.

So r ≈ 0 does not mean "no relationship"; it means "no straight-line relationship". And the other way around, a high r doesn't guarantee that a line is a good idea either: there is a famous example, Anscombe's quartet, with four very different clouds of points (a line with noise, a curve, a perfect line with one stray point and a vertical column with one loose point) that have exactly the same r, 0.82. The only defense is to draw the cloud.

One consequence: if two things are independent (knowing one doesn't change what you expect of the other, topic 9), their covariance is 0. But not the other way around: here the covariance is almost 0 and consumption depends heavily on speed.

Idea 4 of 6

A single truck can create or destroy a correlation

The covariance adds up rectangles, and the rectangle of a truck very far from the cross is huge: it can weigh more than all the others combined. A single bad record (a flow sensor that goes haywire, a misplaced decimal point) is enough to make a correlation appear that doesn't exist, or make one that does exist vanish.

A sturdier version is the Spearman correlation: each value is replaced by its rank in the column (1st, 2nd… 21st) and r is computed with the ranks. An absurd value can only be "the last one"; it can't be "ten times the last one".

How to read the chart. The 20 orange points are normal trucks. The red point with a ring is an outlier record: drag it wherever you like. The blue line is the best line with the outlier; the dashed gray one, without it. With "Show the ranks", each axis shows each truck's rank in its column: what Spearman uses.

What to do: look at the cloud before believing an r; check whether the odd point is an error (outliers topic); also compute Spearman as a second opinion, and if the two don't look alike, be suspicious. What you should not do is delete a point just because it spoils the correlation you were expecting.

Idea 5 of 6

Correlation is not causation

In the fleet, older trucks burn more fuel: r = 0.60, and the line says each year of age adds 0.62 L/100 km. Replace the old ones to save diesel? First, a question: what else is different about the old trucks? In this company, old trucks are sent to city delivery (89% of those over 10 years old) and new ones to the highway (only 7% of those under 5 years old go to the city). And city driving burns much more.

How to read the chart. Each point is a truck: its age across and its consumption up. In "Whole fleet", the blue line is the best line for all of them. When split, the city trucks show in orange and the highway ones in purple, each group with its own line.

The route type is a confounding variable: it pushes both columns at once (it decides which trucks go to the city and how much they burn), and it creates a correlation between them without one causing the other. There are more ways for a correlation to fool you:

Selection bias

If you only study the trucks still in the fleet after 12 years, the ones that broke down a lot have already been sold. Among those that remain, age seems not to increase breakdowns. The missing data counts too.

The cause runs backwards

The trucks that visit the maintenance shop most have more breakdowns. It's not that the shop causes them: the breakdowns cause the visits. The correlation is the same in both directions; the arrow is added by whoever reads it.

Chance from looking a lot

With few trucks and many pairs of columns, some pair comes out with a striking r by pure chance (idea 6). If you search through 36 pairs, the best "relationship" may mean nothing.

How do you prove a cause? The most reliable way is an experiment: change one thing on purpose in a group of randomly chosen trucks and compare it with another, similar group where it isn't changed (an A/B test). If you can't, compare within similar groups (like when splitting by route) or use a model that accounts for the known confounding variables, knowing that the unknown ones are still there.

Idea 6 of 6

Many columns at once: the correlation matrix

With 9 columns there are 36 pairs. Instead of 36 clouds, each r goes into a table: the correlation matrix. The diagonal is always 1 (each column with itself) and the table is symmetric (the r of A with B is the r of B with A). Two of the columns are pure chance: the letter of the license plate and the day of the month the driver was born.

How to read the table. Each cell is the r of its row's column with the column of its number. Blue: they move together; purple: in opposite directions; the more intense, the stronger. Tap a cell to see its scatter plot below. (On a phone, the almost-white cells, below 0.15, have no number.)

Three things you can read in the matrix:

  • Nearly duplicated columns. Diesel liters and diesel dollars have r = 1.00: they are the same information (the liters times the price). If you put both in a linear regression, the model can't tell which one to credit with the effect and its weights become unstable. This is called multicollinearity, and the usual fix is to keep just one.
  • Groups that move together. The km, the liters and the dollars form one block; age and idling, another. Principal component analysis (PCA) starts from this matrix (or its cousin, the covariance matrix) and looks for its eigenvectors: the directions that summarize each block in a single new column.
  • Chance with little data. With 500 trucks, the chance columns don't go above 0.07 with anything. With only 15, the license plate letter comes out with r = 0.48 with the truck's age. Pure coincidence: with little data, r moves around a lot, and among 36 pairs there is always some surprise.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Draw the scatter plot with the cross of the means before computing anything.
  2. The covariance is the mean of the products of the deviations (dividing by n − 1). Its sign says whether they move together or in opposite directions; its size depends on the units.
  3. The correlation r is the covariance of the z's: no units, between −1 and 1. r² says what share of the variation goes in a straight line with the other column.
  4. r only sees lines: a U or a curve can give r ≈ 0 with a very strong relationship.
  5. Watch out for outliers: a single one can create or destroy r. Also compute Spearman (r of the ranks) and compare.
  6. With little data and many pairs, be suspicious of the most striking r: it may be chance.
  7. Before talking about causes, look for confounding variables, missing data and whether the arrow could run backwards. To prove a cause, run an experiment.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Price per tonne

The line for load and consumption (0.485 L/100 km per tonne) says what each extra tonne costs: over 100,000 km a year, 485 liters, about $800 at $1.65/L. It is useful for pricing, as long as the relationship is measured within similar routes.

Choosing columns for a model

Before training, the correlation matrix warns you about duplicated columns (liters and dollars, gross weight and load): drop one or summarize them with PCA so the model stays stable.

Signals before a breakdown

What rises along with engine breakdowns: temperature, idling hours, cold starts? Correlations point to where to look; the maintenance shop and an experiment (changing the oil earlier in half the fleet) tell you whether it's a cause.

Dashboards that mislead

"Drivers who took the training use 8% less fuel"… but the ones who went to the training were the highway drivers. Before crediting the savings (and budgeting $12,000 for more courses), compare within each route type.

Connections

With the models of the ML series and the other topics

The correlation is the covariance of the z's from topic 7 and the cosine between arrows from topic 2. The independence of topic 9 implies covariance 0, but not the other way around. In the ML series, the slope of linear regression with a single column is r times the ratio of the standard deviations, and its r² is this topic's r squared; PCA looks for the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix; and the Kalman filter carries its own covariance matrix to know how the position and speed errors move together. In "Preparing the data", exploratory analysis starts with scatter plots and matrices like these, outliers are the ones that create correlations, and scaling doesn't change r (but it does change the covariance).

To be fair

Where it helps and where it misleads

Where it helps

  • A quick, comparable number to find which columns move together among hundreds.
  • The correlation matrix sums up a whole table at a glance and warns you about duplicated columns.
  • It is the basis of linear regression, PCA and many other techniques.

Where it misleads

  • It only sees straight-line relationships: a U or a curve goes unnoticed.
  • A single extreme data point can create it or destroy it.
  • It says nothing about causes: a confounding variable, a selection bias or chance give equally pretty correlations.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Scatter plot
A drawing of two columns: each row (each truck) is a point.
Deviation
How far a value is from the mean of its column, with a sign.
Covariance
The mean of the products of the deviations of two columns (dividing by n − 1). Positive if they move together, negative if in opposite directions.
Correlation (Pearson's r)
The covariance without units, between −1 and 1. It measures how much the cloud looks like a straight line.
r²
r squared: the share of one column's variation that goes in a straight line with the other.
Spearman
The r of the ranks (1st, 2nd…) instead of the values. It holds up better against outliers and sees any relationship that always goes up or always goes down.
Confounding variable
A third column that pushes both at once and creates a correlation between them.
Correlation matrix
A table with the r of each pair of columns. The covariance matrix is the same, but with the covariances.
Multicollinearity
When several columns in a model say almost the same thing and the model doesn't know which one to give the credit to.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need them to understand the idea:

deviations: dx = x − mean(x) dy = y − mean(y) covariance: cov(x, y) = sum(dx · dy) / (n − 1) 8 trucks: 130 / 7 = 18.6 (tonnes · L/100 km) correlation: r = cov(x, y) / (sd(x) · sd(y)) = 18.6 / (6.19 · 3.25) = 0.92 always between −1 and 1 = sum(zx · zy) / (n − 1) with z = deviation / std. dev. = cosine of the angle between the arrows dx and dy line: slope = r · sd(y) / sd(x) = 0.92 · 3.25 / 6.19 = 0.485 L/100 km per tonne r² = 0.85: 85% of the variation in consumption goes in line with the load Spearman: r of the ranks; 8 trucks: 0.91
