Duplicates and logging errors

Telemetry data doesn't arrive the way a textbook would draw it. It arrives repeated, out of order, with clocks that run fast, positions that jump kilometers away and sensors that freeze. None of these glitches is rare: they show up every day in any fleet. Before computing anything, you have to undo them.

The setup

A truck works from 6:00 to 16:00. Its onboard device sends one message every 30 seconds with the time, the GPS position, the speed, the odometer (the mileage counter) and the fuel level. Over the day that is 1,200 messages, but 1,260 reach the server.

We want something very simple: how many kilometers it drove, adding up the distance from each position to the next. The odometer, which is reliable, says it drove 544 km. With the messages as they arrive, the sum comes out more than double.

What you just saw

Errors in the log, not in the truck

None of these problems is real behavior of the truck: they are glitches in how the data is logged and transmitted. That's why they're called logging errors, and that's why they're fixed with rules about the messages themselves, before any analysis or model. The next five ideas look at them one by one, plus two that the toy doesn't show: the clock that runs fast and the frozen sensor.

This step comes before exploring (topic 2): with duplicated, out-of-order messages, any histogram or average comes out skewed.

Idea 1 of 5

A duplicate isn't always a repeated row

Every message that arrives gets an identification number (id) on the server. A resend arrives later and gets another number, so if you look for identical rows you won't find it. What repeats is the content: same device time, same position, same speed, same odometer.

How to read the table. These are 8 consecutive messages, just as they reached the server. "Device time" is when it happened; "arrival", when the server received it. The rows in red are the ones the chosen rule marks as duplicates.

How do you recognize a duplicate?

  • Same id: two rows with the same message number.
  • Identical row: every column equal, including the arrival time.
  • Same content: equal in what the truck measures (device time, position, speed, odometer), even if the id and arrival change.

Idea 2 of 5

Arrival order is not the order of events

Between 12:10 and 12:40 the truck drove through an area with no coverage. The device stored the messages and, once it got signal back, sent them mixed in with the new ones. On the server, a message from 12:15 arrives after one from 12:45. If you draw the route in arrival order, the truck seems to jump 30 km backward and forward over and over.

How to read the chart. On the left, each dot is a message between 11:50 and 13:10: further right means it reached the server later; higher means it happened later. If everything arrived in order, the dots would form a single rising staircase. On the right, the route for that hour and a half, drawn by joining the messages in the chosen order.

Idea 3 of 5

A clock that runs fast

The onboard device stamps the time with its own clock, which on this truck runs 40 seconds fast every hour. By 11:00 it is already 3 minutes and 20 seconds ahead. That doesn't sound like much, until the data is matched against another source: the gas station pump logs the fill-up at 11:01, and according to the device the truck didn't stop until 11:03. The system concludes "fill-up without the truck present" and fires a false fraud alert.

How do you discover the clock is fast? By comparing the device time with the time the message reached the server (which has a reliable clock). The network always delays a little, it never makes things early. So the messages that arrived fastest show how far the clock has drifted.

How to read the chart. At the top, each dot is a message: its height is "arrival time minus device time" in seconds. With a perfect clock they would all sit just above 0 (the network delay). The blue line follows the bottom edge, the fastest messages: it drops 40 seconds per hour. At the bottom, the fuel stop according to the device and the time on the pump's receipt.

Idea 4 of 5

Impossible positions: the GPS that jumps

Reflections off buildings, tunnels or a bad receiver start make the GPS give, for an instant, a position several kilometers away. You don't need a map to find them: you compute how fast the truck would have had to go to reach each point from the previous one in 30 seconds. If getting there and getting back both take more than a certain speed, that point is fake.

How to read the chart. Each bar counts how many pairs of consecutive positions need that speed (the scale stretches to the right so the huge values fit). Almost all are below 100 km/h, the truck's real speed. The ones on the right, at hundreds or thousands of km/h, are the jumps. The red line is your threshold: points that exceed it both coming in and going out are marked as fake.

Idea 5 of 5

Frozen sensors

Sometimes a sensor stops updating and the device keeps sending the last value it read. It doesn't give an error: it gives a perfectly normal number, always the same one. Here, the fuel level stays at the same value for two hours while the truck covers more than 160 km. A fuel-consumption analysis would conclude that it burned nothing on that stretch.

How to read the chart. The line is the tank level (in %) during the day. It drops slowly as fuel is used and jumps up when refueling at 11:00. The red bands are the stretches where the value didn't change at all for more minutes than you choose below.

With the truck stopped, it's normal for the fuel not to change. If you don't require movement, every stop gets marked as a frozen sensor.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Keep both times: the device time (when it happened) and the arrival time (when it was received). Without both you can fix almost nothing.
  2. Remove resends by content, not by id.
  3. Sort by device time before computing distances, trips or anything else that depends on order.
  4. Watch the clock: the bottom edge of "arrival minus device" tells you how fast or slow it runs.
  5. Flag impossible positions using the speed needed to get there and back.
  6. Look for values that don't change while the truck is moving.
  7. Compare with a reliable source (here, the odometer) to know whether the cleanup works.
  8. Don't delete: flag. Keep the original data and add columns that say what was corrected and why.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Kilometers and billing

If you charge or pay by the kilometer, duplicates and jumps mean money. Always reconcile against the odometer.

Real-time alerts

Speeding, unauthorized stops or fill-ups: with duplicates or drifting clocks, false alerts fire.

New device or firmware

Each device model has its own quirks: error codes, frequencies, how it resends. When you switch devices you have to check everything again.

Data for models

A fuel-consumption model trained on frozen-sensor stretches learns that you can drive 160 km without using any fuel.

Connections

With the models of the ML series

The Kalman filter does continuously what we do here with rules: it decides how much to trust each position based on what it expects, and discards the ones that don't fit. Markov chains depend entirely on the order of the messages: with out-of-order messages they would learn transitions that don't exist.

To be fair

What it costs and what you gain

What you gain

  • Numbers that match reality (kilometers, hours, fuel use).
  • Fewer false alerts.
  • Models that learn from the truck, not from the device's glitches.

What it costs

  • You need to know how each device works: there is no universal rule.
  • The thresholds (maximum speed, minutes frozen) are chosen with judgment and reviewed.
  • Some data gets flagged as doubtful and can't be recovered.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Device time
The time the onboard device stamps on the message: when it happened.
Arrival time
The time at which the server receives the message.
Resend
A second copy of a message that the device sends again because it got no confirmation.
Clock drift
When a clock slowly runs fast or slow.
GPS jump
A fake position, away from the real one for an instant.
Frozen sensor
A sensor that keeps repeating the last value it read.
Speed needed
Distance between two positions divided by the time between them.
