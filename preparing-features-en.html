Preparing features

A model knows nothing about kilometers, truck types or the time on the clock: all it sees is columns of numbers. How you turn each piece of data into numbers decides what it can learn. Here you'll see five conversions that change the result, and the rule that ties them together: everything is learned from the training data only.

The setup

We want to estimate how much diesel a trip will burn (in liters per 100 km) before it leaves. The simplest idea: find the 5 most similar past trips and average what they burned. This method is called "nearest neighbors".

We measure "similar" with two facts about each trip: the distance, in kilometers (short trips burn more per 100 km, because of starts and a cold engine), and the load, in kilograms (more weight, more fuel). The dots below are 150 past trips by identical trucks; the model has to estimate one of them as if it were new.

How to read the chart. Each dot is a trip: horizontally, its distance (the scale is stretched for short trips so you can see them); vertically, its load. The color says how much it burned: the more orange, the more fuel. The star is the trip to estimate, and the lines connect it to the 5 trips the model considers most similar.

How do we compare kilometers with kilograms?

  • As is: the difference between two trips is computed with the raw numbers. A 100 km difference counts the same as a 100 kg difference.
  • Each on its own scale: first, each column is expressed as "how far it is from normal for that column". That way 1 extra tonne and 50 extra km count about the same.

What you just saw

Units decide what matters

Kilograms go from 0 to 24,000 and kilometers from 5 to 600. If you subtract them as they are, any load difference is huge compared with any distance difference, and the model ends up looking for trips with the same load without looking at the distance. Nobody decided that: the units did. If the load were in tonnes, exactly the opposite would happen.

Scaling means putting each column on a comparable measure before training. It matters for models that measure distances or add up columns with equal weight (nearest neighbors, support vector machines, k-means, models with a penalty). Trees and forests and boosting don't need it: they only ask "is it bigger than some value?" about one column at a time.

Idea 1 of 5

Three ways to scale, and one crazy value

There are three common recipes for putting a column on its own scale:

  • Standardize: subtract the average and divide by the typical spread. Normal values end up near 0; values far from normal, at 2, 3 or more.
  • Min-max: the smallest value becomes 0 and the largest, 1.
  • Robust: subtract the median and divide by the width of the middle half of the data. An extreme value barely affects it.

Idea 2 of 5

Categories are not numbers

The fleet has four truck types: refrigerated truck, van, rigid truck and semi-trailer. A model needs numbers, and the temptation is to number them 1, 2, 3 and 4 (in alphabetical order of their Spanish names, say). But then the model believes there is an order and distances: that a van sits "between" a refrigerated truck and a rigid truck, and that a semi-trailer is "four times" something. The alternative is to give each type its own column, which is 1 if the truck is of that type and 0 if not (in the jargon, one-hot).

How to read the chart. The orange dots are the real average fuel use of each truck type (with the same load). The blue bars are what a simple linear model estimates, trained on the load and the truck type written in one of the two ways.

Idea 3 of 5

When there are a thousand categories: the customer's average, carefully

The customer matters too: some are downtown, others up in the mountains. But there are almost 1,300 customers, and half of them have 3 trips or fewer. One column per customer means 1,300 almost-empty columns. A very common trick is to replace each customer with the average fuel use of their trips. It has two traps:

  • With few trips, the average is luck: a customer with a single trip, made by an empty van, seems to "burn 8 liters". The fix is to pull the average toward the overall average when there are few trips, as if we had a few extra "normal" trips.
  • The average includes the answer: if you give a trip its customer's average computed with that same trip, you're leaking part of what it has to guess. The fix is to compute each trip's average without that trip (using chunks of the data).

How to read the chart. Each row is a customer, with their number of trips. The hollow circle is the plain average of their trips; the filled dot, the adjusted average; the vertical line, the average fuel use of the whole fleet.

Idea 4 of 5

Time of day is a circle

In the late afternoon there's more traffic and more fuel is burned; in the early morning hours, less. If you give the model the hour as a number from 0 to 24, it thinks 11 PM (23:00) and 1 AM (01:00) are very far apart (22 apart), when they are two hours apart. And a simple model can only draw a straight line: "the later, the more fuel" or "the less fuel". The fix is to place the hour on a circle, like on a clock, and give the model the two coordinates of the point on the clock.

How to read the chart. Horizontally, the departure time. The orange dots are the extra (or reduced) fuel use of trips leaving at that hour, once everything else is taken out (truck type, load, distance); the more trips, the bigger the dot. The blue line is what the model learns.

Idea 5 of 5

The truck's past, without peeking at the future

Each truck has its own way of burning fuel: some are better tuned, others wear out over time. A very useful feature is "how much has this truck burned on its trips over the last 30 days". It's a time window: an average that keeps moving along with the days.

The trap: it's very easy to compute the window with trips from before and after, because the table has everything. In the report the model will look better than it is, and on the day it's really used it won't have that data from the future.

How to read the chart. Each violet dot is a trip by the same truck (horizontally, the day). The blue line is the feature we'd give the model on each day. The vertical line is "today": to its right, the exam trips. The shaded band is the days used by the average for the last trip before today.

Putting it all together

The full recipe, in a single pipeline

All these conversions learn something from the data: the average and spread for scaling, the list of truck types, each customer's average. Just like in topic 1, that learning is done with the training data only and then applied as is to the exam and to new trips. The safe way not to get it wrong is to put the conversions and the model into a single "pipeline" that is trained in one go.

  1. Look at what kind of data each column is: quantity, category, time or date, text.
  2. Quantities: scale them if the model measures distances; with extreme values, the robust recipe.
  3. Few categories: one column per category, never a made-up number.
  4. Many categories: the average of each one, adjusted toward the overall average and computed without the row itself.
  5. Hours, days of the week, months: on a circle.
  6. History: windows that only look back.
  7. Everything inside the pipeline, fitted with the training data only.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Mixed units

Speed in km/h, RPM in the thousands, temperature in degrees, odometer in the hundreds of thousands: without scaling, the odometer rules.

Codes with many values

Driver, customer, route, engine fault code: hundreds or thousands of values, many with only a few cases.

Cycles

Hour, day of the week, month of the year, heading (359° and 1° are next to each other).

Signal aggregates

From thousands of pings a day to "hard brakes per 100 km over the last week" or "idling hours this month".

Connections

With the models of the ML series

Nearest neighbors and k-means measure distances: without scaling, the units rule. Gradient descent moves much faster with scaled columns. Logistic regression with a penalty punishes every column equally, so it needs them on the same scale. XGBoost doesn't need scaling, but it does need categories and windows done right.

To be fair

What it costs and what you gain

What you gain

  • Models that use all the information, not just the column with the biggest numbers.
  • Categories and cycles the model can learn.
  • History features, which are often the most useful of all.

What it costs

  • Every conversion is one more piece that has to be saved with the model and applied the same way in production.
  • Per-category averages and windows are easy to get wrong, with leaks that don't show up in the report.
  • More columns isn't always better: each one has to be checked.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Feature (or variable)
Each column you give the model.
Scale
Put the columns on comparable measures.
Standardize
Subtract the average and divide by the typical spread.
One-hot
One column of zeros and ones for each category.
Target encoding
Replacing each category with the average of what you want to predict in that category.
Time window
A summary (average, sum, maximum) of the last few days.
Leakage
When the model sees, while learning, information it won't have when it's really used.
Pipeline
The conversions and the model chained together, fitted together with the training data only.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need it to understand the idea:

standardize: (value − average) / typical spread min-max: (value − minimum) / (maximum − minimum) robust: (value − median) / (quartile 3 − quartile 1) adjusted average of a customer with n trips, adding m "normal" trips: (n × customer average + m × overall average) / (n + m) hour on a clock: sine(2π × hour / 24) and cosine(2π × hour / 24)
