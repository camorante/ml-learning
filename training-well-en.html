Training well

We already know what a network computes, how it gets graded and how to tell which way to move each weight. What’s left is the practical part: how many examples to look at before each step, how long to make it and how to give it momentum. With the same network and the same data, some choices learn in minutes and others never learn at all.

The setup

It has the same shape as the network from topic 3, but with tanh in the hidden layer instead of sigmoid, which trains better (you saw this in topic 2). It recognizes the engine’s normal zone: it looks at the engine speed (in revolutions per minute, rpm) and the coolant temperature (in °C) of a reading and says whether it is normal or an anomaly. It has 2 inputs, 8 hidden neurons and 1 output: 33 numbers to adjust.

The maintenance shop has checked 600 readings to train it and keeps another 400 aside for validation: the network doesn’t see them while training, and they are used to check whether it really learned. Near the edge of the zone, not even the shop is sure, so no network can get them all right.

Nothing is trained in your browser here: we trained the network for real 72 times, once for each combination of the three controls, always from the same starting weights and for 60 epochs (60 passes through the 600 readings). You choose which one to see.

How does it take its steps?

SGD takes each step downhill, nothing more. With momentum, it remembers the previous steps, like a rolling ball. Adam also adjusts the step of each weight separately. Ideas 4 and 5 explain them.

How many readings does it look at before each step?

The batch. With 1, it takes 600 steps per epoch, each one guided by a single reading. With 32, 19 steps. With all 600, a single, very well-informed step per epoch.

0.001 (baby steps)3 (big strides)

Try this, in order:

  1. Start with Adam, batches of 32 and rate 0.1: in 10 epochs it goes below the target, and it ends up getting 91.2% of the validation readings right.
  2. Lower the rate to 0.001: the curve barely moves. Raise it to 3: the curve jumps and the zone comes out warped.
  3. With Adam and rate 0.1, change the batch to 1 and to all 600: with 1 the curve shakes; with all 600 it takes 27 epochs, because it only takes one step per epoch.
  4. Switch to SGD with rate 0.1 and batches of 32: it falls short. Raise the rate to 1: now it works. Each optimizer has its own good rate.
  5. With momentum, batch 1 and rate 1: the loss blows up and training stops.

What you just saw

Same data, same network, very different results

Training means repeating a cycle many times: look at a few examples, see how wrong the network is, work out which way to move each weight and move them a little. Gradient descent already told the idea with a ball rolling down a foggy mountain. In a real network there are three decisions that change everything:

  • The batch size: how many examples it looks at before each step. Few, cheap and shaky steps; all of them, expensive and steady steps.
  • The learning rate: how long the step is. Too short, it never gets there; too long, it bounces or blows up.
  • The optimizer: the rule for taking the step. With momentum, the network moves faster along valleys; with Adam, each weight has its own step size.

And before all of that, the inputs on the same scale: without it, none of the 72 combinations learns.

The ideas that follow: the training loop step by step (1), the batch size (2), the learning rate (3), momentum (4), Adam (5), how to read loss curves (6) and why the inputs have to be scaled (7).

Idea 1 of 7

The loop: one batch, one grade, one push, one step

Before starting, the network has random weights and says roughly “fifty-fifty” to everything. Then it repeats:

  1. Take a batch: the next 32 readings on the list.
  2. Forward: the network gives its answer for each one.
  3. Loss: it is compared with what the shop said, and out comes a number (topic 4).
  4. Backward: it works out how much and in which direction to move each of the 33 weights. That is the backpropagation of topic 5.
  5. Update: each weight moves a tiny bit in that direction.

When the 600 readings run out, one epoch has passed. They are shuffled again, so the batches don’t always come out the same or in the same order, and it starts over.

Idea 2 of 7

The batch: a poll instead of a census

To know exactly which way is down, the network would have to look at every example before each step. With millions of readings, each step would take forever. Instead, it looks at a random batch and works out the direction from it: it’s like a poll, which is more or less accurate depending on how many people you ask (math topic 11). With 4 times as many examples, the error of the estimate is cut in half.

To see it in a picture, we use the smallest possible network: one neuron with two numbers, a weight w and a bias b, which estimates the probability of a breakdown within 30 days from the kilometers since the last inspection. These are the 2,000 trucks from topic 4.

Idea 3 of 7

The learning rate: the most important knob

The learning rate says how much each weight moves at each step, in proportion to the slope. If it is very small, the network learns, but so slowly that it never gets there. If it is very large, it overshoots the bottom of the valley and lands on the opposite slope, higher up: the loss bounces and, if it is huge, blows up.

A very common trick: don’t keep it fixed. Start with a large rate to move fast and keep lowering it (decay; here, shaped like half a cosine) to fine-tune at the end. Often a warmup is added: the first steps go from zero up to the chosen rate, so the network doesn’t shoot off while its weights are still random.

Idea 4 of 7

Momentum: a rolling ball that doesn’t stop at every bump

The loss valley is often narrow and elongated: very steep in one direction and almost flat in the other. Plain descent bounces from wall to wall and makes very little progress toward the bottom, the zigzag you already saw in gradient descent.

Momentum fixes that like a heavy ball: each step adds the current slope to part of the previous step. Whatever changes sign every time, the bouncing between walls, cancels out; whatever always points the same way, toward the bottom, builds up and speeds up. The number β says how much is remembered: 0 means remembering nothing; 0.9, keeping 90% of the velocity.

How to read the chart. A toy valley with two weights, a and b: the curves are lines of equal loss, and the bottom is in the center (green). In the direction of b it is 100 times steeper than in the direction of a. The gray path is descent without momentum; the orange one, with the momentum you choose. Both use the same rate, 0.019, almost the largest at which plain descent doesn’t blow up.

Idea 5 of 7

Adam: a step tailored to each weight

In the narrow valley, plain descent has a problem: the steepest direction sets the rate. If the rate is too large for that direction, it blows up; so it has to be small, and in the flat direction it moves at a snail’s pace. The narrower the valley, the worse.

Adam keeps two memories for each weight: an average of its recent slopes (the momentum of idea 4) and an average of how large they have been (squared). It divides the first by the square root of the second: that way, each weight moves about what the rate says, whether its slope is huge or tiny. At the start it corrects for the fact that both memories begin at zero. It is the default optimizer almost everywhere.

Idea 7 of 7

Scaling the inputs: the step you can’t skip

The rpm of the readings go from about 800 to 2,050, with a standard deviation of 261; the temperature, from 76 to 102 °C, with a deviation of only 5.2. If they are fed to the network as they are, a weight attached to the rpm moves the sum 50 times more than one attached to the temperature, and on top of that 1,400 rpm times any starting weight gives huge sums that saturate the neurons (tanh goes flat at ±1 and stops learning).

The fix is to standardize: subtract the mean and divide by the deviation of each input, using the training numbers (topic 7 of Preparing the data). In topic 1 the perceptron needed 128 corrections with the raw data and 4 with the standardized data.

Putting it all together

The recipe for training a network

  1. Set aside some validation data the network won’t see while training.
  2. Scale the inputs with the training mean and deviation.
  3. Start with Adam, batches of 32 to 256 and a rate of 0.001; also try rates 3 and 10 times larger and smaller.
  4. Shuffle every epoch and repeat the loop: batch, forward, loss, backward, update.
  5. Watch the curves: if they don’t go down, raise the rate; if they jump or blow up, lower it; if the validation curve goes up while the other goes down, stop (topic 7).
  6. Fine-tune at the end by lowering the rate little by little.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Millions of readings

A fleet of 500 trucks with one reading per second generates more than a billion rows a month. No network looks at all of them before each step: it trains with batches, often of 256 or 512, that fit in the machine’s memory.

Sensors on different scales

rpm in the thousands, temperature in the tens, oil pressure in bar, speed in km/h, idle time in seconds. Without scaling, the sensor with the biggest numbers rules the training and the others barely count.

Bad data that makes the loss blow up

A sensor that sends 65,535 °C because of a communication glitch can produce a giant push and ruin the network in a single step. That is why the data gets cleaned, careful rates are used and the curve is watched every epoch.

Retraining every week

A fuel-consumption or breakdown model is retrained with the new data. Fixing the seeds, saving the curves and comparing with the previous version on the same validation data avoids shipping a model that trained badly out of bad luck.

To be fair

What it gives you and what to watch out for

What it gives you

  • With batches, you can train on as much data as there is: each step costs the same.
  • Momentum and Adam reach the bottom of narrow valleys in far fewer steps.
  • Adam works well with a wide range of rates: there is less to tune.
  • Loss curves warn you about almost every problem in time.

What to watch out for

  • There is no rate that is good for everything: it changes with the optimizer, the batch and the data.
  • With small batches and a large rate, the loss shakes and can blow up.
  • If the inputs are not scaled, not even Adam can fix it.
  • Well tuned, plain SGD can match Adam: choosing an optimizer is no substitute for trying rates.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Batch (mini-batch)
The examples the network looks at before taking a step.
Step (iteration)
One update of all the weights.
Epoch
One full pass through all the training data.
Shuffle
Mixing up the data at the start of each epoch so the batches change.
Learning rate
How long each step is, in proportion to the slope.
SGD
Stochastic gradient descent: a step downhill using the slope of one batch.
Momentum
Adding part of the previous step to the current one, like a rolling ball.
Adam
Momentum plus a step size of its own for each weight, based on how large its slopes have been.
Decay and warmup
Lowering the rate over time, and raising it little by little at the start.
Validation
Data set aside to check whether the network really learns or just memorizes.
Standardize
Subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation of each input.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don’t need it to understand the idea. θ is a weight, g its slope on the batch and η the rate:

SGD: θ ← θ − η·g with momentum: v ← β·v + g θ ← θ − η·v Adam: m ← 0.9·m + 0.1·g (average of the slopes) s ← 0.999·s + 0.001·g² (average of their squares) m̂ = m/(1 − 0.9ᵗ) ŝ = s/(1 − 0.999ᵗ) (t = step number) θ ← θ − η·m̂/(√ŝ + 0.00000001) Adam's first step: m̂ = g and √ŝ = |g| → the weight moves η, whether g is large or small batch noise: error of the slope ≈ (error with 1 example)/√(batch size) standardize: x' = (x − mean)/deviation rpm: (x − 1,418)/261 °C: (x − 89.3)/5.21 the default toy: Adam, η = 0.1, batches of 32 600/32 → 19 steps per epoch (the last one with 24) 60 epochs = 1,140 steps validation loss: 0.672 at the start → 0.212 accuracy 91.2%
