Matrices as transformations

A matrix looks like a table of numbers, but it is a way to move the whole plane at once: rotate it, stretch it, mirror it, tilt it or flatten it. With four numbers and one simple idea you can understand what it does, when it can be undone, and why a layer of a neural network is almost the same thing.

The setup

On the grid there is a truck drawn, plus the positions of five trucks from the fleet (the dots). We are going to move all of it at once with a matrix: a table of four numbers, in two columns of two.

The trick to reading it: watch two arrows, → one step to the right and ↑ one step up. The first column of the matrix says where the → arrow ends up, and the second column says where the ↑ arrow ends up. Everything else (the grid, the truck, the dots) moves by following those two arrows.

Try this, in order:

  1. With “identity” (→ at (1, 0) and ↑ at (0, 1)) nothing moves. Raise the first number, “steps to the right” of the → arrow, up to 2: the truck stretches sideways and its area doubles.
  2. Press “rotate”: the truck turns about 37° without changing shape. The area does not change (× 1).
  3. Press “mirror”: the truck faces the other way, as in a mirror. The area factor comes out negative: the minus sign warns you that the figure has been flipped over.
  4. Press “flatten”: both arrows point in the same direction and the whole plane collapses onto a line. Area × 0.

What you just saw

A matrix is a recipe for moving the plane

You moved a whole plane by changing just four numbers. It works because this kind of movement (mathematicians call them linear transformations) follows two rules: straight lines stay straight and parallel to each other, and the center (0, 0) does not move. With those rules, as soon as you know where the two arrows → and ↑ end up, you know where any point ends up.

  • The matrix is written with the two arrows as columns: first where → ends up, then where ↑ ends up.
  • The area factor (mathematicians call it the determinant) says how much every surface grows or shrinks. If it is negative, the figure has also been flipped over. If it is 0, everything has been flattened.

Vectors (the data arrows from topic 1) were the data; matrices are what you do to the data. The five ideas below explain how it is computed, what is preserved, what happens when you chain several, when it can be undone and what it has to do with neural networks.

Idea 1 of 5

Matrix times point: so many steps of one column and so many of the other

Where does any point end up, for example (3, 2)? That point means “3 steps → and 2 steps ↑”. After the transformation, each → step has turned into the first column and each ↑ step into the second. So the point ends up at 3 times the first column plus 2 times the second. That is all “multiplying a matrix by a vector” does.

Here the matrix is fixed: the → arrow ends up at (2, 1) and the ↑ arrow at (−1, 1).

How to read the chart. The gray square is the starting point (drag it or use the sliders); the dashed gray line shows its → and ↑ steps. The orange dot is where it ends up: you get there by taking the same steps, but now each → step is the blue arrow (first column) and each ↑ step is the purple one (second column).

The point’s ↑ steps

The same math with fleet data. One hour of truck driving in the city burns 14 liters of diesel and $0 in tolls; one hour on the highway, 25 liters and $9 in tolls. Those are the two columns of a matrix: (14, 0) and (25, 9). A truck that does 3 hours of city and 2 of highway uses 3 × (14, 0) + 2 × (25, 9) = (42, 0) + (50, 18) = 92 liters and $18. The matrix turns “hours per road type” into “liters and dollars”.

You can also do it by rows: the liters are 14 × 3 + 25 × 2 = 92, which is the dot product of the first row with the hours (we saw it in topic 2). They are two ways of doing the same calculation.

Idea 2 of 5

What each transformation preserves

A depot (at the center), a customer and a gas station form a triangle, with positions in kilometers east and north. Each transformation respects some things and changes others. Knowing which is what lets you use them without fear:

  • A rotation preserves distances, angles and areas: it only changes which way you are facing.
  • A scaling equal in every direction (changing units) changes distances and areas, but not angles.
  • Stretching just one direction changes the angles too. A tilt (shear) distorts angles and distances but preserves area.
  • A mirror preserves everything except the orientation: what went counterclockwise now goes clockwise.

How to read the chart. The triangle with a dashed outline is the original; the orange one is the transformed one. The gray band is the road from the depot to the customer. The arc marks the angle at the depot. The corners of the chart tell you what the axes mean now.

Idea 3 of 5

Chaining transformations: order matters

If you do one transformation and then another, the result is another transformation, with its own matrix. Computing that combined matrix is called multiplying the two matrices. But, unlike multiplying numbers, here the order changes the result: rotating and then stretching sideways is not the same as stretching sideways and then rotating.

Idea 4 of 5

Undoing a transformation, and when it is impossible

Many transformations can be undone: if you know where a point ended up, you can work out where it came from. The transformation that undoes it is called the inverse. But if the matrix flattens the plane onto a line (area factor 0), two different points can end up in the same place, and then there is no way to know which one it came from. The information is lost.

And there is a warning before you reach 0: when the area factor is very small, undoing works, but it magnifies any error. A tiny measurement error in the final point becomes a huge error when you work out where it came from.

How to read the chart. The blue and purple arrows are the two columns. The shaded area is what the square with side 1 turns into. The orange dots are the positions of five trucks after transforming. In “undo” mode, the gray rings are the true positions and the blue crosses are the ones recovered by undoing when each final point was measured with an error of just 0.08.

What do you want to see?

The same thing happens with data. Imagine a table of trips with two columns: the distance in kilometers and the same distance in miles. They look like two pieces of data, but they are one: the second is the first multiplied by 0.621. It is like a matrix with two columns pointing in the same direction. A model that tries to split the importance between the two cannot decide (any split gives the same result), and if the columns are almost equal, the weights it computes change wildly with any noise. That is why it pays to look for redundant columns before training, in the exploratory analysis.

Idea 5 of 5

A neural network layer: matrix, shift and fold

Each dot is a truck: horizontally, how far the engine temperature is from normal; vertically, how far the oil temperature is. Normally the two rise or fall together (the purple dots, in a diagonal band). If one drifts far from the other, on either side, something is wrong (a sensor, the thermostat, a leak): the orange dots.

No straight line separates the two groups, because the odd ones are on both sides of the band. And matrices alone cannot fix it: rotating, stretching or tilting the cloud never turns a straight line into something else. A layer of a neural network does three things: it multiplies by a matrix, adds a shift and then folds the plane (it sets everything negative to 0; this is called ReLU). That fold is what a matrix cannot do.

How to read the chart. Go through the four steps with the buttons. The axes change at each step: in steps 2 and 3, each axis is one of the layer’s two outputs. The green line is the best straight boundary in each chart; dots with a red ring are errors. The gray area in step 2 is the one the fold sends to the corner.

A neural network stacks many layers like this, with much bigger matrices, and learns the numbers of each matrix and each shift on its own. Without the fold, stacking layers would be useless: several matrices in a row are a single matrix (idea 3). Neural networks will get their own collection.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. A matrix moves the whole plane at once, without bending straight lines and without moving the center.
  2. Its columns say where → and ↑ end up. That is enough to know what it does.
  3. Matrix times point = x steps of the first column + y steps of the second. By rows, they are dot products.
  4. Each type preserves different things: rotation, distances and angles; tilt, area; a mirror flips things over.
  5. Chaining is multiplying matrices, and order matters.
  6. The area factor (determinant) says whether it can be undone: 0 means lost information; almost 0, magnified errors.
  7. Matrix + shift + fold is a neural network layer.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Road axes

GPS gives positions east and north. A rotation turns them into “along the road” and “toward the shoulder”: that is how you measure whether the truck drifts out of its lane or onto the shoulder.

A badly mounted accelerometer

The telemetry box gets bolted wherever it fits, rotated relative to the truck. A rotation matrix converts its readings to the vehicle’s axes: forward, sideways and up. Without it, hard braking looks like a sharp turn.

Units and combinations

Going from km to miles or from liters to gallons is a scaling. Going from hours per road type to liters and dollars is a cost matrix, as in idea 1.

Compressing data

Dozens of signals per truck are summarized into two or three numbers by multiplying by a matrix that projects onto the most important directions: that is what principal component analysis does.

Connections

With the models of the ML series and the other topics

Principal component analysis is a matrix that rotates the data and then keeps the first directions (a projection, which deliberately flattens what does not matter). Linear regression solves a system of equations with matrices, and suffers with redundant columns (idea 4). Support vector machines separate with a straight line, and their trick for separating what a line cannot is similar to the fold in idea 5: taking the data to another space. All of this rests on the vectors and distances of topic 1 and the dot product of topic 2.

To be fair

What helps and where it misleads

What helps

  • Four numbers (or a few hundred, in more dimensions) describe a movement of all the data at once.
  • Computers multiply matrices incredibly fast: it is the foundation of all modern machine learning.
  • Looking at the columns, the area factor and what is preserved is enough to understand almost any matrix.

Where it misleads

  • The order of the steps matters, and chaining them the wrong way around is a very common mistake.
  • An almost-flattened matrix seems to work, but it magnifies errors when you undo it.
  • It only makes “straight” movements: to separate twisted things you need something more, like the fold in neural networks.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Matrix
A table of numbers. Here, the recipe for a movement: each column says where one of the starting arrows ends up.
Linear transformation
A movement of the plane that keeps straight lines straight and parallel and does not move the center.
Matrix times vector
Where a point ends up: x times the first column plus y times the second.
Matrix product
The matrix for doing one transformation and then another. Order matters.
Determinant
The area factor: how much surfaces grow. Negative if the figure is flipped over; 0 if everything is flattened.
Inverse
The matrix that undoes another one. It only exists if the determinant is not 0.
Shear (tilt)
Slides each row of the grid a little more than the one before, like a deck of cards pushed sideways.
ReLU
The fold of neural networks: it leaves positive values as they are and sets negative ones to 0.
