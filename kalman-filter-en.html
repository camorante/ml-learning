Kalman filter

Predict where the truck will be, look at what the GPS says, and keep a blend of the two, weighted by how much it trusts each one. Repeated every second, it turns a noisy GPS into a clean track, and it also knows how far off it might be.

Before we start

The navigator and the lighthouse

A ship without GPS knows its speed and its heading. With that it works out where it should be an hour later, but with every hour that passes it is less sure: currents and wind push it off course. Now and then it sights a lighthouse and takes a position fix, which isn't exact either.

A sensible navigator throws neither away. They settle on a point in between, closer to whichever is more reliable, and their uncertainty after combining them is smaller than either one on its own. The Kalman filter is that calculation, done the best possible way and repeated every second.

Idea 2 of 5

Which one does it listen to more?

The only thing you need to tune is how much the filter trusts its own prediction. If the filter thinks the truck can change speed abruptly, it trusts its prediction very little and chases every GPS reading, noise included. If it thinks the truck barely changes, it trusts the prediction a lot, and in curves it overshoots because it reacts too late.

Move the slider on this curve and watch the average error. The best setting is in the middle. It is worked out from how trucks really move and how good the GPS is.

Idea 3 of 5

When the GPS drops out

In a tunnel or between tall buildings, no readings come in. The filter keeps working: it moves forward at the last speed it knew, just like the navigator with no lighthouse. The nice part is that it knows it is flying blind, and its zone of doubt (the circles) grows every second. On the way out, the first GPS reading carries a lot of weight and corrects it all at once.

Idea 4 of 5

Estimating what nobody measures

In this example the GPS only gives positions, but the filter also keeps track of the speed, because it needs it to predict. If we computed the speed by subtracting two consecutive positions, the GPS noise would make it jump by tens of km/h. The filter infers it from many readings at once and the result is smooth, following the truck as it stops at a traffic light and pulls away again.

Idea 5 of 5

Its weak spot: it believes everything it's told

The filter assumes GPS errors are small and randomly spread. But sometimes the signal bounces off a building and the GPS reports a point 70 meters away. The filter obediently pulls the estimate toward that impossible point. The usual fix is a sanity check: if a reading is too far from what the filter expects, it is ignored.

There is another limit: the classic filter only works if everything is "in a straight line" (linear). With sharp turns or sensors like a compass, people use extended versions (EKF, UKF), with the same idea of predicting and correcting.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Describe the state: what you want to track (position, speed) and how it changes from one second to the next.
  2. Say how unsure you are about that model (the truck can speed up) and about each sensor (the GPS is off by a few meters).
  3. Predict: move the state according to the model and increase the doubt.
  4. Correct with each reading: blend prediction and measurement, weighted by their doubts, and the doubt goes down.
  5. If there is no reading, just predict. If the reading is impossible, ignore it.
  6. Repeat every time new data arrives. If you don't need it live, a backward pass (smoothing) refines it even more.

In the real world

What is it used for?

Cleaning up GPS tracks

Smooth routes for the map, more accurate distances traveled, and fewer "jumps" from a parked vehicle.

Sensor fusion

Combining GPS, the CAN bus odometer and the accelerometer: each one fills the gaps of the others, for example in tunnels and parking garages.

Navigation

It was born to take Apollo to the Moon, and it is still in every plane, drone and phone that works out its own position.

Values that change over time

Tracking, in real time, things measured with noise: fuel level, engine temperature, average fuel consumption.

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • Very fast: a handful of calculations per reading, ideal for real time.
  • It gives the estimate and also how far off it could be.
  • It copes with gaps in the measurements and combines different sensors.
  • It is the best possible answer when its assumptions hold.

The not so good

  • You have to tell it how unsure it is about each thing, and if you choose badly, it shows.
  • Absurd readings (bounces) fool it unless they are filtered out.
  • The classic model is linear; turns and nonlinear sensors need an EKF or UKF.
  • Live, it only uses the past; for the best route you have to smooth afterwards.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

State
What you want to track, even if not all of it is measured: position and speed.
Prediction
Where it should be now according to the model and the previous state.
Measurement (or observation)
What the sensor says, with its error.
Innovation
The surprise: the difference between what the GPS says and what was expected.
Kalman gain
How much of the surprise it believes: 0 = ignores the GPS, 1 = believes it completely.
Covariance
The measure of doubt: the size of the blob where it could be.
Smoothing
Going back over the route, also using the later readings.
For the curious: the formulas behind it all

In the simplest case, with just one thing to track:

predict: position = previous position + speed × time doubt = previous doubt + model doubt correct: gain = doubt / (doubt + GPS doubt) position = position + gain × (GPS − position) doubt = (1 − gain) × doubt
