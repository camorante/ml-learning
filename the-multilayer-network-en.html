The multilayer network

A single neuron can only draw a straight line. Put several of them side by side (a layer) and each one draws its own line, while another neuron behind them combines what they all say. That middle layer does something surprising: it folds the picture until what could not be split by a straight line ends up on either side of one.

The setup

These are the same 30 trips from the end of topic 1. For each trip we know the load (in tonnes) and the fuel use (in liters per 100 km). A more heavily loaded truck burns more, so normal trips form a sloping band. There are 12 trips that don’t add up: 6 burn too much (a breakdown, low tire pressure, a leak) and 6 burn too little (the load or the refueling was logged wrong).

In topic 1, a single neuron couldn’t handle them: its best line leaves 6 mistakes, because it only flags one side. Here there is a small network: neuron A flags the trip if it is above its line, neuron B flags it if it is below its own line, and an output neuron combines the two votes and gives the final answer.

The output neuron: how does it combine the votes?

One vote is enough: it raises an alert if A or B votes for one. Both votes needed: it raises an alert only if A and B vote for one at the same time. The output neuron is another neuron like the one in topic 1, but its inputs are the votes of A and B.

Try this, in order:

  1. At the start, line B is so low that it never votes: the network is the single neuron from topic 1, with its blue line. The 6 odd trips at the bottom get away.
  2. Raise line B to about 21 L: now neuron B watches the lower side and, with “One vote is enough”, the network flags both sides of the band. Mistakes drop to 0: two neurons draw what one alone could not.
  3. Keep raising line B above 24 L: it moves into the normal band and the extra alerts begin. The lines have to sit in the gap between the normal trips and the odd ones.
  4. Switch to “Both votes needed”: the network would only raise an alert if a trip were above A and below B at the same time, which is impossible here. The output rule matters as much as the lines.

What you just saw

Several neurons in a layer, and one more that combines them

That is what a multilayer network is: neurons organized in layers. The neurons in the hidden layer look at the trip’s data, each with its own weights and bias, just like the neuron in topic 1. The output neuron doesn’t look at the data: it looks at what the hidden neurons say. It is called “hidden” because nobody tells each of those neurons what to flag; only the final answer is checked.

  • Each hidden neuron draws its own line. On one side it votes “yes” and on the other “no”, as in topic 1.
  • The output neuron combines the votes. Depending on its weights, it can ask for one vote to be enough (an “or”), for all of them to be needed (an “and”) or for stranger combinations.
  • With that, the boundary stops being a single line. Two lines and an “or” surround the band; with more neurons you can surround closed or curved zones.
  • Seen from the hidden layer, the problem becomes easy. If instead of load and fuel use you look at what A and B vote, the odd trips and the normal ones are split by a straight line. The hidden layer has folded the picture.

The ideas that follow take it one piece at a time: the calculation inside, with a table of weights (1), how votes are combined to solve the famous XOR (2), the folded picture (3), what more neurons give you (4) and what more layers give you (5), and what the theorem saying a network can learn “anything” really promises (6).

Idea 2 of 6

Combining votes: XOR, solved at last

A truck has two motion detectors: the GPS and the wheel sensor. They should agree. If the GPS says it is moving and the wheel doesn’t (or the other way around), something is wrong: a broken sensor, or a truck riding on another vehicle, on a ferry. The alarm should go off when one says yes and the other says no. That is the “exclusive OR” (XOR), the problem no straight line can solve, which in 1969 made people doubt neural networks for years.

With two hidden neurons and one output neuron, it can be solved. Neuron A votes if at least one detects motion; neuron B, if both do. The output raises the alarm with the rule “A yes and B no”.

How to read the chart. Across, the GPS (0 = stopped, 1 = moving); up, the wheel sensor. The four dots are the four possible cases: orange if the alarm should go off (they disagree) and purple if not. The blue line belongs to neuron A (it votes above it) and the green one to neuron B. The orange zone is where the network raises the alarm; a red ring marks every mistake.

Output neuron rule

“A yes and B no” is a neuron that adds A’s vote, subtracts B’s and asks for the total to be above half a vote: weights 1 and −1.

Idea 3 of 6

The hidden layer folds the picture

There is another way to see the same thing, and it is the most important idea of this topic. For the output neuron, a trip is not “load and fuel use”: it is what A and B vote, two percentages. If you plot the trips using those two numbers as coordinates, you get a new picture, the hidden space. And in that picture, the odd trips and the normal ones end up on either side of a straight line: the output neuron only has to draw it, just like the neuron in topic 1.

The hidden layer has taken the plane, with the band in the middle, and folded it like a sheet of paper: the two sides of the band, which were far apart, end up together in a corner of their own, away from the normal trips.

Idea 4 of 6

More neurons, more sides: surrounding a closed zone

Another fleet case: the maintenance shop wants an alert when an engine runs outside its normal zone. Two numbers are checked: the engine speed (rpm) and the coolant temperature. Normal is a rounded zone in the middle, and the odd readings are all around it, in every direction: an overheated engine, a thermostat stuck open that won’t let it warm up, an engine revved very high or lugging at low rpm uphill.

To surround a zone you need several sides, and each hidden neuron adds one. With 1 neuron, the network only cuts the plane in two; with 3, it already forms a triangle; with more, a polygon that looks more and more like the round zone. The networks in this chart were trained beforehand on 120 readings that the shop reviewed (you will see how they are trained in topics 5 and 6).

How to read the chart. Each dot is a reading: engine speed across, temperature up. Purple: normal. Orange: anomaly. The unshaded zone is what the network treats as normal; the orange one, where it raises an alert. The gray dashes are the lines of each hidden neuron. The dashed green ellipse is the true normal zone, which the network doesn’t know: it only sees the dots.

Idea 5 of 6

More layers: folding what is already folded

Another way to grow is to put layers after layers: the second hidden layer looks at what the first one says, the third at what the second says, and so on. If each layer folds the picture, the next one folds what is already folded. It is like folding a strip of paper: one fold gives 2 pieces, two folds give 4, three give 8… each fold doubles the pieces.

The chart shows the cleanest known example: a network in which each layer has only 2 neurons (ReLU ones, from topic 2) and folds a strip of numbers between 0 and 1 in half. It is not a fleet case, but it explains why the networks used in practice usually have several layers rather than a single very wide one.

How to read the chart. Across, the number that goes into the network (from 0 to 1); up, the number that comes out. The blue line is the network’s output: it is made of straight pieces, alternating in two shades so you can count them. Below, the network: each column of circles is a layer of 2 neurons.

Idea 6 of 6

“A network can learn anything”: what it really says

There is a famous result, the universal approximation theorem (Cybenko, 1989; Hornik, 1991). It says that a network with a single hidden layer can get as close as you like to any reasonable curve, as long as it has enough neurons. It sounds like “networks can do anything”, but it says a lot less:

  • It says that a network with those weights exists. It doesn’t say training will find it.
  • It doesn’t say how many neurons are needed. It can be a huge number.
  • It only talks about the data you already know, inside their range. It says nothing about how well it will do on new cases.

The chart shows it with the speed profile of a 100 km route: highway, a village, a city, some roadworks and a mountain pass. Each hidden neuron is a smooth step at one point of the route, and the output adds up steps going up and down.

How to read the chart. Across, the kilometer of the route; up, the average speed. The thick gray line is the speed logged by the GPS every 500 m. The blue one is the network. The little ticks at the bottom mark where each neuron’s step is. Here the steps are spread out evenly and the computer only chooses how much each one goes up or down.

Putting it all together

The complete recipe

  1. Hidden layer: several neurons look at the same data. Each one has its own weights and bias; together they form a table (a matrix).
  2. Each hidden neuron draws its line and votes, with a percentage, on which side the case is.
  3. The output neuron combines the votes: with its weights it decides whether one is enough, all are needed, or something in between.
  4. Seen from the hidden layer, the output’s job is to draw a single line: the hidden layer has folded the picture to make that possible.
  5. More neurons give the boundary more sides; more layers fold what is already folded and multiply the pieces with few numbers.
  6. Count the adjustable numbers: each neuron has one weight per input and one bias.
  7. Don’t trust the “it can do anything”: a network existing doesn’t guarantee you will find it or that it will get new cases right.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telematics

Fuel use that doesn’t add up

Normal is a band of fuel use that depends on load, grade and speed. A network with one hidden layer watches both sides at once: the truck that burns too much and the one that, on paper, burns too little.

Engine outside its zone

Engine speed, temperature, oil pressure: healthy is a closed zone and the odd readings are all around it. The networks that detect engine anomalies surround that zone with many neurons at once.

Sensors that disagree

GPS versus wheel, odometer versus route kilometers, tank level versus refuelings: raising an alert when two sources disagree is an XOR, and it needs at least one hidden layer.

Estimated time of arrival

With dozens of numbers per trip (distance, time of day, traffic, road type, driver), multilayer networks with a few hidden layers are a common choice for estimating when the truck will arrive.

Connections

With the other topics

Each neuron in the network is the one from topic 1, and its weighted sum is the dot product from math topic 2. A whole layer is a matrix that transforms the plane, like the ones in topic 3; what lets it fold the plane instead of only stretching and rotating it is the activation from topic 2.

In the ML series, the output neuron with a sigmoid is a logistic regression working on the hidden space. Support vector machines do something similar with a different trick: they take the data to a new space, chosen in advance, where a straight line is enough. PCA also changes the space, but it only rotates and stretches it, without folding it. And decision trees surround zones with horizontal and vertical cuts, while each hidden neuron cuts in whatever direction it likes.

In this collection, topic 4 covers the output and the loss (how to measure what the network gets wrong), and topics 5 and 6 cover how all these weights are trained at once: backpropagation and the tricks for training well.

To be fair

What it gives you and what to watch out for

What it gives you

  • It separates what a straight line can’t: bands, closed zones, XOR.
  • It learns on its own which lines to draw: you don’t have to invent features like “distance to the band” by hand.
  • The whole calculation is matrix multiplications, very fast on a computer.

What to watch out for

  • The more neurons, the more numbers to tune, and the more data you need so it doesn’t learn the noise.
  • Training it is harder than training one neuron: it can get stuck in a bad solution.
  • It is hard to explain why it decides what it decides: a neuron had one weight per input; a network has whole tables.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Multilayer network (MLP)
Neurons organized in layers, each layer looking at what the previous one says.
Hidden layer
A layer between the input and the output. Nobody tells it what to compute.
Output neuron
The one that gives the final answer, based on what the hidden ones say.
Weight matrix
The table with a layer’s weights: one row per neuron, one column per input.
Forward pass
Computing the network’s answer layer by layer, from the input to the output.
Hidden space
The picture of the cases according to what the hidden neurons say, instead of according to the original data.
Width
How many neurons a layer has.
Depth
How many layers the network has.
Parameters
All the adjustable numbers: the weights and biases of every neuron.
XOR (“exclusive OR”)
“One yes and the other no”. The classic example of what a single neuron cannot separate.
Universal approximation
The theorem that says that, with enough neurons, there is a network as close as you like to any reasonable curve.
