Bayes

A rule for changing your mind in an orderly way: what you already knew, corrected by the new clue according to how well that clue tells the possibilities apart. Underneath Gaussian processes, Markov models and Kalman filters, it is always this same idea.

Before we start

What you already knew, plus the new clue

A breakdown alert goes off on a truck. Is it broken? Almost everyone answers "probably yes: the system is right 90% of the time." But a piece is missing: how rare breakdowns are. If only 2 out of every 100 trucks have one, the false alerts from the 98 healthy ones far outnumber the real ones.

Bayes puts the reasoning in three steps: what you believed before (breakdowns are rare), how well the clue tells the cases apart (an alert is 18 times more likely on a broken truck than on a healthy one) and what you should believe now. The toy above does it with 1,000 trucks counted one by one.

Idea 1 of 5

Each clue moves the belief

What you believe after one clue becomes what you believe before the next one. A second, independent alert (another sensor) pushes much further, because we are already starting from 27%. An inspection with no alert pushes it down. Try combinations: the order doesn't matter, only how many clues of each kind arrive.

Idea 2 of 5

Learning a proportion from little data

What percentage of a new driver's trips arrive late? After a single late trip, the direct count says "100%," which is absurd. Bayes starts from what we know about the fleet (around 20%, with plenty of room) and each trip adjusts it. The curve shows which values are believable: with few trips it is wide, and it gets narrower with every trip.

Idea 3 of 5

A classifier that multiplies clues: Naive Bayes

Was this trip aggressive? We start from the fact that 1 in 10 is. Each signal from the accelerometer and the GPS multiplies the odds in favor by a factor: how many times more frequent that signal is on aggressive trips than on normal ones. That's how Naive Bayes works: one factor per clue, and they all get multiplied. Turn signals on and off.

Idea 4 of 5

"Naive": watch out for counting the same clue twice

The "naive" part comes from treating each clue as if it were independent of the others. If two sensors measure the same thing, for example hard braking according to the accelerometer and according to the GPS speed drop, the math multiplies twice by what is really a single clue. It still gets the side right, but it becomes overconfident.

Idea 5 of 5

When what you believed before was wrong

The starting point matters. A driver is really late on half of their trips, but we start from the fleet average, 20%. If we cling hard to that belief (as if we had already seen 100 trips), it will take many trips to convince us. If we start with little conviction, the data takes over right away. Move the strength of the initial belief.

Putting it all together

The complete recipe

  1. Start from what you already know: how common each possibility is before looking at the case.
  2. Measure how well each clue tells the cases apart: how many times more likely it is in one case than in the other.
  3. Multiply: the earlier belief by the strength of the clue, and turn it back into a percentage.
  4. Chain: what you believe now is the starting point for the next clue.
  5. To estimate a proportion, add what you observed to a reasonable initial belief, not to zero.
  6. Be wary of too much confidence if your clues repeat each other.

In the real world

What is it used for?

Designing alerts

Knowing, before launching an alert, how many will be false given how rare the breakdown is, and setting the threshold accordingly.

Little data per group

Rates per driver, per route or per maintenance shop with few cases: pull them toward the fleet average until there is enough data.

Classifying text

Spam filters and sorting maintenance shop notes: Naive Bayes is extremely fast and works surprisingly well with words.

Measuring uncertainty

All Bayesian methods (Gaussian processes, Kalman) give the answer together with how much you can trust it.

To be fair

The good and the not so good

The good

  • A single rule, always the same, for combining prior knowledge and data.
  • Works with little data and gives its uncertainty.
  • Naive Bayes trains in an instant and needs very few examples.
  • The math can be explained with "out of every 1,000 cases…".

The not so good

  • You have to choose the initial belief, and a bad choice weighs a lot when there is little data.
  • Naive Bayes gives probabilities that are too extreme if the clues repeat each other.
  • Beyond the simple cases, the exact math is impossible and you need approximations (simulation).
  • For classifying with lots of data, other models (logistic regression, trees) are usually more accurate.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Prior probability (prior)
What you believed before seeing the clue: for example, that 2% of trucks have a breakdown.
Likelihood
How likely the clue is in each case: an alert on 90% of the broken trucks and on 5% of the healthy ones.
Posterior probability
What you should believe after seeing the clue.
Likelihood ratio
How many times more likely the clue is in one case than in the other (90 / 5 = 18). It's the "strength" of the clue.
Base rate
How common something is in general. Forgetting it is the most common mistake.
Naive Bayes
A classifier that multiplies the strength of each clue as if they were independent.
Credible interval
A range where we believe the value lies with, for example, 90% probability.
For the curious: the formulas behind it all

You don't need them to understand the idea:

P(breakdown | alert) = P(alert | breakdown) × P(breakdown) / P(alert) in "betting odds": after = before × strength of the clue strength = P(clue | broken) / P(clue | healthy) proportion with an initial belief of a "successes" and b "failures": estimate = (successes seen + a) / (trips seen + a + b)
