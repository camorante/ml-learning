Convolutional networks

A harsh brake has the same shape 2 seconds into the trip as it does at 5. A convolutional network (CNN) takes advantage of that: instead of looking at each instant separately, it slides a small magnifying glass, a filter, across the whole signal, and the same magnifying glass recognizes the shape wherever it is. With 10 times fewer numbers than a regular network, it gets more right.

The setup

Every truck carries an accelerometer that measures, 10 times per second, how it speeds up going forward, in meters per second every second (m/s²). Below zero, the truck is braking. The fleet calls it harsh braking when the truck brakes at more than 3 m/s² on average for half a second, and it wants to detect it in windows of 6.4 seconds: 64 readings.

The detector is a filter: 5 numbers, one for each reading in half a second. It is placed over 5 consecutive readings, multiplies each reading by its number and adds them up. Then it slides one reading over and repeats, until it has covered the window: that gives 60 sums. If the largest one goes over a threshold, the alert goes off.

We did not set the 5 starting numbers by hand: a real network learned them, trained on 2,000 reviewed windows. Here you can move it, change it and test it on 200 windows it did not see during training.

Try this, in order:

  1. With the learned filter and the "harsh braking" window, drag the filter: while the truck drives smoothly, the sum stays around zero; over the braking it climbs to 8.81, at 3.4 s, above the threshold of 6.03. Alert.
  2. Switch to "pothole": one reading reaches −3.61 m/s², but the largest sum is only 2.38. The jolt goes down and right back up, and the filter, by adding five readings, cancels it out.
  3. Try "gentle braking": it lasts longer, but the truck does not brake as hard. The largest sum, 4.59, does not reach the threshold.
  4. Pick the "single instant" filter, which only looks at one reading: accuracy drops from 97.5% to 83.5%, because it mistakes potholes for braking. The "average" filter, five equal weights, scores the same as the learned one: the network discovered on its own that you have to average half a second.
  5. Pick "reversed", with positive weights: now the filter looks for speed-ups, not braking, and gets 58.5% right, almost like always saying "harsh".

What you just saw

A small magnifying glass that sweeps the whole signal

A filter is a handful of weights, very few. At each position it does the same thing as a neuron from topic 1: multiply and add, the dot product from math. The sum is large when the stretch of signal looks like the shape drawn by the weights. As it slides, it leaves a trail of sums, the feature map, which tells you where that shape appears.

That gives two advantages at once:

  • The same weights everywhere. A braking at 2 s and another at 5 s are detected with the same 5 numbers. There is no need to learn the shape 60 times.
  • Very few numbers. The filter has 5 weights, whatever window it looks at, even one an hour long.

A convolutional network stacks several layers of filters and ends with a regular layer that decides. The ideas that follow: what goes wrong with a regular network (1), how the filter moves and what size comes out (2), several filters at once (3), pooling (4), stacked layers (5) and the same thing in an image (6).

Idea 1 of 6

The problem: a regular network learns each instant separately

A regular layer, like the ones in topic 3, has a weight for each reading in each neuron. The weight for reading 12 only learns from what happens at reading 12. If in the training data the braking always fell at the start, the weights at the end never saw one.

That really happened with the fleet's old device: it only recorded when it noticed a braking, so the braking always started in the first 2 seconds of the window. With those 2,000 windows we trained two networks: a regular network (dense) with 2,113 numbers and a convolutional network with 201. Then we tested them with the new device, which records nonstop.

Idea 2 of 6

How the filter moves and how many sums come out

Three choices set how many sums the filter leaves. Its size: how many readings it covers. The stride: how many readings it moves each time; with stride 2 it skips every other one and half as many sums come out. And the padding: adding zeros at the edges so the filter can also be centered on the first and last readings, and as many sums come out as there are readings.

How to read the chart. At the top, 16 readings in a row (the gray boxes with a dashed border are padding zeros). At the bottom, the sums that come out. The orange lines join the chosen sum with the readings it used. Below the chart, the size calculation.

Idea 3 of 6

Several filters: each one looks for a shape

A convolutional layer does not have one filter but several, and each one leaves its own map. Each map is called a channel. The first layer of our convolutional network has 4 filters of 5 weights. Nobody told them what to look for: they learned it during training. Each one also has a bias, and below zero it switches off (the ReLU from topic 2).

How to read the chart. At the top, the window. Below, one row per filter: on the left, its 5 weights as bars (up, positive; down, negative); on the right, its map, lined up with the window. The more intense the orange, the more strongly the filter responds at that moment; no color, switched off.

Idea 4 of 6

Pooling: keeping the strongest value of each piece

A layer of filters is usually followed by pooling: the map is split into pieces and only the highest value of each piece is kept (max-pooling). With pieces of 2, the map shrinks to half. And if the braking comes a little earlier or a little later, the maximum of each piece changes less than the individual sums: the network becomes more tolerant of small shifts, and the bigger the piece, the more tolerant. Our network ends with the extreme case, the maximum of the whole map: "does the shape appear anywhere?".

How to read the chart. At the top, the map of the learned filter on a harsh brake: in gray with the braking in place and in orange with the braking shifted. At the bottom, the same after pooling: each wide bar is the maximum of its piece. The more alike the two versions, the more tolerant the network is of the shift.

Idea 5 of 6

Stacked layers: each one sees a longer stretch

A 5-reading filter only sees half a second. But if another layer of filters is put on top of its map (already shrunk by pooling), each sum in the second layer combines 5 sums from the first, which together cover more readings of the signal. That stretch of signal a neuron sees is called its receptive field. The first layers see short details, like the edge of a braking; the upper ones, long shapes, like the whole braking. At the end, a regular layer, the head from topic 4, turns what it found into a probability.

Idea 6 of 6

The same in an image: a 3 × 3 filter

In an image, the filter is a square of weights that sweeps the picture from left to right and from top to bottom. This is the idea these networks were born with, to read handwritten digits, and the one onboard cameras use today. Here, tiny images of 16 × 16 pixels, like a piece of the image from a camera looking at the road, and a 3 × 3 filter: 9 weights.

Putting it all together

How a convolutional network works

  1. The signal comes in: 64 accelerometer readings (or the pixels of an image).
  2. First layer: 4 filters of 5 weights slide across the signal and leave 4 maps, one per shape.
  3. ReLU and pooling: the negatives are switched off and the maximum of each pair of sums is kept.
  4. Second layer: 8 filters sweep those 4 maps at once and combine short shapes into long ones.
  5. Maximum of each map: "does this shape appear anywhere?", no matter where.
  6. The head: one neuron turns those 8 numbers into the probability of harsh braking (topic 4). The weights are learned as always, with backpropagation (topic 5) and Adam (topic 6).

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Maneuvers in the accelerometer

Harsh brakes, sharp turns, hard acceleration or impacts: each maneuver has a shape in the accelerometer and gyroscope signals. A 1D convolutional network finds them at any moment of the trip, with one filter per shape.

Vibrations that warn of a breakdown

A worn bearing or a failing engine leaves a pattern that repeats in the vibration. The filters detect it whichever second of the recording it shows up in, weeks before the truck stops.

Onboard cameras

Cameras facing the road or the cab use 2D convolutional networks to see whether the truck is crossing the lane line, whether there is a pedestrian or whether the driver is looking at their phone. The first layers find edges; the upper ones, objects.

City maps on a grid

If you count harsh brakes or delays in each cell of a grid over the city, you get an image. A 2D filter finds dangerous intersections or congestion zones by their shape, whatever neighborhood they are in.

Connections

With the other topics

Each filter sum is a dot product (math topic 2), and sliding a filter is the same as multiplying by a huge matrix almost full of zeros, with the same 5 numbers repeated in every row: the matrices as transformations of math topic 3.

In this collection, the head that decides is a regular layer from topic 3 with the output and loss from topic 4. The filters are learned with the backpropagation of topic 5: since each weight is used at every position, the pushes from all of them add up. And they are trained with batches and Adam, as in topic 6. The other big way of handling sequences, reading them step by step with memory, is topic 9, recurrent networks.

To be fair

What it gives you and what to watch out for

What it gives you

  • It recognizes a shape wherever it is: what it learns in one place works everywhere.
  • Far fewer numbers: 201 versus 2,113 in this topic's network, and the gap grows with longer signals.
  • It needs less data to learn, because each window teaches the same filters many times.
  • It works the same for signals (1D), images (2D) and video.

What to watch out for

  • It only sees short stretches: for distant relationships you need many layers.
  • It assumes the shape matters equally everywhere; if position matters (for example, the start of the trip), you have to give it to the network.
  • The learned filters are not always easy to interpret.
  • Training can get stuck: with another starting point, the toy's one-filter network stayed at 53% accuracy.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Filter (kernel)
A few weights placed over a stretch of the signal; they multiply and add.
Convolution
Sliding the filter across the whole signal, doing that sum at each position.
Feature map
The sums a filter leaves: they tell you where its shape appears.
Channel
Each of the maps of a layer, one per filter.
Stride
How many readings the filter moves each time.
Padding
Zeros added at the edges so the filter fits there too.
Pooling
Keeping the maximum (or the mean) of each piece of the map.
Receptive field
The stretch of the original signal that influences a neuron.
Shared weights
Using the same filter weights at every position.
Dense network
The regular network from topics 3 to 6, with a weight for each input in each neuron.
