End-to-end project with networks

We bring the whole collection together in a real problem: at the end of each month, deciding which trucks go to the maintenance shop before they break down. We'll do it step by step, the way it's done at work: the question and its price, splitting the data, preparing the inputs, a simple baseline, the network, the exam and what happens afterward. And with a surprise at the end that's worth telling: here the network doesn't win.

The setup

A fleet of 3,000 trucks has been sending three engine readings every day for two years: the vibration, the coolant temperature and the oil pressure. For each truck we also know its model, its depot, its age, its kilometers, its load and how many breakdowns it had the year before. The data is simulated, but it has the traps of real data: potholes, heat waves, sensors that fail and breakdowns that come without warning.

At the end of each month we have to decide, truck by truck: do we send it for an inspection? An inspection costs $400 (the mechanic and one day off the road). A breakdown on the road costs $4,000 (tow truck, urgent repair and the lost delivery). If the truck was going to break down and we inspect it, the breakdown is avoided.

We have trained three models that give, for each truck, its risk of a breakdown in the next 30 days. The chart uses them on 600 trucks that none of them has seen, in the last 4 months: 2,400 decisions, 199 of which ended in a breakdown.

The gray dashed lines are never alerting and alerting everyone; the vertical orange one, the 10% threshold. The dot is the chosen threshold. On the right, what happened with those 2,400 decisions: breakdowns avoided, breakdowns on the road, unnecessary inspections and healthy trucks that nobody touched.

Try this, in order:

  1. With the network and 10%: 449 alerts in 4 months, 111 breakdowns avoided, and the cost drops from $331.7 to $221.5 per truck and month. A third less.
  2. Lower the threshold to 2%: almost everyone gets an alert and the unnecessary inspections eat up the savings. Raise it to 40%: almost nobody goes to the shop and the breakdowns on the road come back.
  3. Go back to 10% and switch models. The three curves almost touch: boosting costs $214.7, logistic regression $219.5.
  4. Notice that the curve is flat near the minimum: a threshold of 8% or 12% costs almost the same. What you can't do is leave the 50% that libraries use by default.

What you just saw

A model is one piece; the project is everything else

The end of a project isn't "the network is right X% of the time". It's a decision with money behind it: which trucks get an alert, how much it costs and how much it saves. Getting there is a path, and at every step you can ruin everything without a single red error showing up.

The ideas follow that path: the question and the price of each mistake (1), splitting the data before looking at it (2), preparing the network's three inputs (3), comparing with a simple baseline (4), training and reading the curves (5), trusting the exam only as much as it deserves (6), looking at the mistakes (7), and calibrating and monitoring the model once it works (8).

Idea 1 of 8

The question and the price of each mistake

Before touching any data, you have to write the question out in full: "at the end of the month, what is the probability that this truck breaks down in the next 30 days?". And then, what it costs to be wrong in each direction. Here the two mistakes don't weigh the same: an unnecessary inspection costs $400; a breakdown that slips through, $4,000.

Those two numbers give the decision rule, without looking at any model: if the risk is p, alerting costs $400 and not alerting costs, on average, p × $4,000. Alerting pays off when p × 4,000 is more than 400, that is, when p is above 10%. It's the same reasoning as in imbalanced classes.

How to read the chart. Across, a truck's risk of breaking down. The blue line is what alerting costs (the inspection, no matter what); the orange one, what not alerting costs on average. Where they cross is the threshold; to its right (green area), alerting is cheaper.

Idea 2 of 8

Split before you look: by truck and by time

The first thing you do with the data is split it, as in split before you look. But not just any way. The model will be used on months that haven't happened yet and on trucks that will join the fleet. So you split by truck (1,800 to learn, 600 to choose, 600 for the exam) and by time (the first 16 months to learn, the next 4 to choose, the last 4 for the exam).

Splitting the snapshots at random looks the same and isn't: it mixes future months with past ones and puts the same truck on both sides. If, on top of that, someone has computed a column with the whole table, leakage is guaranteed. Here an analyst in a hurry computed "truck breakdowns per year" with the two full years, including the months they wanted to predict.

Idea 3 of 8

Three inputs, one network

Each end-of-month snapshot has three kinds of data, and the network has one branch for each:

  • The 28 days of sensors, a sequence. A recurrent network (a GRU, from topic 9) reads them day by day, and at the end gives 16 numbers that summarize what it has seen. First, each sensor is scaled, and a copy is added with its value minus what is normal for that truck in the first 21 days: how much it has moved.
  • The model and the depot, categories. Each one becomes 3 learned numbers, an embedding from topic 10.
  • The table: age, kilometers, load, kilometers in the month and breakdowns in the previous 12 months (past only), scaled as in preparing features.

The three outputs are joined in a row of 27 numbers and go through a layer of 16 neurons (topic 3) down to a single output: the risk, with the sigmoid from topic 4.

How to read the chart. The 28 days of each sensor of the chosen truck. The gray dashed line is its mean over the first 21 days: what is normal for that truck. The shaded area is the last week, the closest to the decision. Below, what that truck's table says.

Idea 4 of 8

Simple first; the network has to beat it

Before the network, you train a baseline: a simple model with some hand-made summaries of each sensor (mean, spread, maximum, minimum, slope and the last week minus the three before), the table and the categories. Here, two: logistic regression and tree boosting (the family of XGBoost). The network is only worth it if it beats them.

And here's the honest part: it doesn't beat them. It ranks the trucks a little better, but in dollars boosting comes out cheaper, and all the differences fit inside the noise.

How to read the chart. One bar per model, on the exam. In dollars per truck and month, less is better. Idea 5 of 8

Training and knowing when to stop

The network is trained as in topic 6: batches of 256 snapshots and Adam. Since only 8.7% of the training snapshots end in a breakdown, each breakdown weighs 10.5 times more when computing the loss (the class weights from imbalanced classes). And it stops in time, with the early stopping from topic 7: when validation has gone 12 epochs without improving, training stops and the weights from the best epoch are restored.

How to read the chart. Each epoch is one pass over the 28,800 training snapshots. The loss measures how surprised the network is by what really happened: the lower, the better. The green line marks the best epoch on validation.

Idea 6 of 8

The exam, just once, and with its margin of error

The exam is looked at once, at the end, with the model and the threshold already decided. If you look at it more times to tweak things, it stops being an exam. And even looking at it once, the figure has noise: it's only 600 trucks and 199 breakdowns. With another 600 trucks you'd get something different.

To know how much, you use the bootstrap: draw 600 trucks from the exam trucks, with repetition (each one with its 4 months), recompute the figure, and repeat 2,000 times.

How to read the chart. Each bar is a group of bootstrap replicates according to the difference between the network and boosting; to the right of zero, the network wins. The blue band contains the central 95% of the replicates: the confidence interval.

Idea 7 of 8

Looking at the mistakes, one by one

A single overall number doesn't tell you what's failing. You have to open up the mistakes and group them. Since the data is simulated, here we know something that in real life you find out from the shop: whether each breakdown gave a warning in the sensors beforehand, and what type it was.

How to read the chart. Each bar is a group of breakdowns from the exam: how many got an alert in time with the 10% threshold. The first two separate the ones that were already giving a signal in the 28 days from the ones that weren't; the next three, by type.

Idea 8 of 8

Making the risk mean what it says, and monitoring it afterward

The 10% threshold only works if a 10% risk really is 10%: if the model is calibrated. Class weights break that on purpose: the network learns as if there were 10.5 times more breakdowns. You have to undo it before using the threshold, with a simple calculation.

And once the model is in the shop, another job begins: monitoring it. A new telematics box, a firmware change or a harsh winter change the data without warning. You monitor the data coming in (for example, with the reconstruction error of an autoencoder trained on normal data: if it goes up, something is arriving that it hadn't seen), the alerts going out and, when they come, the real breakdowns. And you retrain with a plan, passing the same exam before switching models, as in the data-prep project.

Putting it all together

The recipe for a project with networks

  1. Write down the question and the price of each mistake: that gives the threshold (400 / 4,000 = 10%) before training anything.
  2. Split before you look, by truck and by time, and compute each column only with what was known on that day.
  3. Prepare the inputs with the training means: scaling, embeddings for the categories and one branch for the sequence.
  4. Train a simple baseline with hand-made summaries. It's the yardstick.
  5. Design the network small, with one branch per kind of data.
  6. Train with Adam, class weights and early stopping, with several seeds.
  7. Read the curves: where it stops, whether it overfits, whether all the seeds tell the same story.
  8. Take the exam just once: cost in dollars with the chosen threshold, confusion matrix and a bootstrap interval.
  9. Open up the mistakes and group them: what can be fixed and what never gives a warning.
  10. Calibrate: undo the class weights and check that the risk tells the truth.
  11. Monitor and retrain: data coming in, alerts going out, breakdowns arriving, and a plan for switching models.

In the real world

Fleet projects with this same recipe

Predictive maintenance

The one on this page: engine sensors, shop history and truck data to decide on inspections. With high-frequency signals (vibration at hundreds of readings per second) hand-made summaries fall short and the network usually does win.

Delivery delays

A sequence of GPS positions along the route, the route and the customer as embeddings and the time of day as a table. The expensive mistake here is not warning the customer; the cheap one, warning too often.

Abnormal fuel consumption

Predict the consumption of each trip (L/100 km) and alert when the real one drifts far from it: a dirty filter, a leak or a refueling that doesn't add up. The monitoring from idea 8, turned into the product.

Risky driving

Accelerometer windows to detect harsh braking and sharp turns (topic 8), and the driver and the road type as extra inputs. Splitting by driver is, here, what splitting by truck is on this page.

To be fair

When a network pays off, and when it doesn't

When it pays off

  • Long or high-frequency signals, where hand-made summaries lose information.
  • Many sources at once (sequences, images, text, categories) that have to be combined.
  • Categories with thousands of values, where embeddings save columns and share what they learn.
  • Lots of data: tens of thousands of breakdowns, not about 2,500.

When it doesn't

  • When the information is in the table, as here: the history and the age matter more than the sensors, and boosting makes just as good use of them.
  • When there's little data: with 199 breakdowns in the exam, an improvement of a few dollars can't be told apart from noise.
  • When each alert has to be explained to the shop: logistic regression can be read; the network can't.
  • When cost matters: the network takes minutes to train and needs more care (seeds, scaling, calibration).

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Alert threshold
The risk from which a truck is sent to the shop. It comes from the costs: inspection / breakdown.
Leakage
Information the model wouldn't have on the day of the decision that sneaked in during training or measuring.
Validation
The data used to choose: when to stop, which threshold, which model.
Exam
The data that is looked at only once, at the end, to know how the model will do.
Baseline
A simple model the network is compared with.
Ablation
Removing one part of the model to see how much it contributed.
Confusion matrix
The table of hits and mistakes: breakdowns avoided, on the road, unnecessary inspections and healthy trucks left alone.
PR-AUC
A number between the breakdown rate and 1 that measures how well the model puts the trucks that break down first.
Bootstrap
Repeating the measurement with draws from the same data to see how much it could vary.
Calibration
That a 10% risk really means 1 in 10 break down.
Drift
Changes in the data over time that the model hasn't seen.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need them to understand the idea. p is the risk the model gives:

alert: $400 always don't alert: p × $4,000 on average alert if p × 4,000 > 400 → p > 0.10 cost per truck and month = (alerts × 400 + breakdowns on the road × 4,000) / snapshots class weights: w = healthy / breakdowns = 26,297 / 2,503 = 10.51 correction: risk = 1 / (1 + e^−(z − ln w)) (z: network output) the data: 3,000 trucks × 24 months = 72,000 snapshots, 8.6% with a breakdown split: learn 1,800 trucks × months 0–15 = 28,800 choose 600 trucks × months 16–19 = 2,400 exam 600 trucks × months 20–23 = 2,400 (199 breakdowns) the network: GRU of 16 + embeddings 3 + 3 + table 5 = 27 → 16 → 1 exam, 10% threshold: network $221.5 PR-AUC 0.376 (111 avoided, 338 unnecessary, 88 on the road) boosting $214.7 PR-AUC 0.365 logistic $219.5 PR-AUC 0.371 never $331.7 the trap: promises PR-AUC 0.498 and $181.2; on the exam, 0.378 and $221.7
