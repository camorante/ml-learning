Sampling and the central limit theorem

You almost never look at all the data: you look at a part and compute a mean. That mean changes every time the part changes. Here you'll see how much it moves, why it moves less the more data you have, and why, wherever they come from, means end up drawing a bell.

The setup

A trucking company wants to know how long its trucks' stops at customer docks last on average. Each minute stopped costs about $0.70 (the driver's wage and a truck that isn't working), and the company wants to bill long waits to whoever causes them.

In a year there are 12,000 stops. Checking one by hand (looking at the delivery note, separating the wait from the unloading) costs $4: checking them all would be $48,000. So only a part is checked, chosen at random: a sample. The 12,000 are the population.

In the toy we cheat: we know all 12,000 stops, so we know the true answer (28.6 minutes) and can see how close each sample gets. In real life you only have the sample.

What you just saw

One sample gives one mean; another sample, another

The mean of a sample is not the mean of the population: it is an estimate that is a bit off, up or down, and each sample is off differently. But it isn't off in just any way:

  • It's right on average. The means of many samples spread around the true mean, without leaning more to one side than the other (if the sample is chosen at random).
  • It wobbles less the bigger the sample, but slowly: with 4 times more stops, it wobbles half as much. That “how much it wobbles” has a name: the standard error (ideas 1 and 2).
  • With large samples, the means draw a bell even if the individual data are skewed. This is the central limit theorem (idea 4), and it's the reason the bell shows up everywhere.

These three facts let you say how much to trust a mean without knowing the true answer. But they only work if the sample is chosen well: if the data depend on each other or if the sample leaves out part of the population, they fail (ideas 5 and 6).

Idea 1 of 6

The population, the sample and the mean that wobbles

The population is everything you want to know something about: all the stops of the year, all the trucks in the fleet, all the trips that will be made next month. A number that summarizes it (its mean, for example) is called a parameter, and it's almost never known.

The sample is the part you actually look at: the stops you check, the trucks you audit, the week of data that fits on your laptop. What you compute with it (its mean) is called a statistic, and it changes from one sample to another. Idea 2 of 6

The standard error and the square-root law

How far does the mean of a sample usually land from the true mean? There's a simple calculation: take the standard deviation of the population (how far the individual stops land from their mean, topic 7), which here is 30.3 minutes, and divide it by the square root of n. The result is the standard error of the mean.

With 16 stops: 30.3 / 4 = 7.6 minutes. With 64: 30.3 / 8 = 3.8. The square root has a consequence that costs money: to cut the error in half you need 4 times more data, and to cut it by 10, 100 times more.

How to read the chart. The blue curve is the standard error for each sample size. The orange dot is the size you pick with the slider; the purple one, that of a sample 4 times larger. Notice how fast the curve drops at first and how little it drops afterwards.

In practice you don't know the population's standard deviation, so you use the sample's (with its n − 1, topic 7). And if the sample is a large part of the population (checking 6,000 of the 12,000), the error is somewhat smaller than the calculation says: with the whole population, the error would be 0. With 16 out of 12,000, the difference is unnoticeable.

Idea 3 of 6

The law of large numbers

Imagine you check stops one by one and, after each one, recompute the mean of all you've checked so far. At first the mean lurches around: a single 3-hour stop sends it flying. But each new stop weighs less than the one before (it's one in 10, then one in 100…), and the mean settles down close to the true mean. That's the law of large numbers.

How to read the chart. Horizontally, how many stops you've checked so far (the scale is stretched at the start: 1 to 10 takes as much room as 100 to 1,000). The orange line is the mean of those checked so far. The blue line is the true mean and the blue funnel marks two standard errors on each side: the mean almost always stays inside.

The law says the mean gets there; the standard error says how fast. And one thing the law does not say: there is no “making up for it”. If the first stops came out long, the next ones don't come out shorter to balance things; the long ones simply end up diluted among many others.

Idea 4 of 6

The central limit theorem: from any shape to a bell

Stops are not bell-shaped: most are short (half last under 19 minutes) and a few are extremely long. It's a skewed shape, with a long tail to the right (topic 8). A day's hard-braking alerts are a count, 0, 1, 2…, that follows a Poisson. The day of the month is flat. And even so, the means of samples from any of them end up bell-shaped (a normal), centered on the true mean and as wide as the standard error.

Why? A mean adds up many small, independent things: some pull up and others pull down, and it's rare for all of them to pull the same way. The quirks of each data point cancel out, leaving a shape that no longer depends on the starting one.

How much data do you need? It depends on how skewed it is. With the day of the month, which is already symmetric, 5 are enough. With the stops, with their long tail, 30 still leave the means somewhat skewed and you need 100 or more for a good bell. The “30 is enough” rule is just that, a rule of thumb, and it fails precisely with long-tailed data.

When it fails or takes a while

The most dangerous thing about the third case: more data doesn't fix it. With a million badly recorded stops, the mean comes out spot on… at the wrong value. The standard error measures how much the mean wobbles by chance, not whether the sample is skewed. The same happens with “only the trucks that report properly”, “only the customers who answer the survey” or “only the drivers who accepted the pilot”.

Idea 6 of 6

How to choose the sample

There are three route types with very different stops: on the highway (fast logistics centers) they last 14 minutes on average, in the city 30 and at the port 68. With 60 stops to check, you can choose them in several ways:

  • Purely at random (simple random sampling): every stop has the same probability. By bad luck, you may get few from the port, or too many.
  • By route type (stratified): the 60 are split ahead of time among the three types and chosen at random within each. There's no more bad luck in the split. You can split according to each group's size, or put more where stops vary more (the port).
  • By truck (cluster sampling): 3 trucks are chosen and 20 stops of each are examined. It's convenient (three drivers, three sets of delivery notes), but the stops of one truck are very similar to each other.

How to read the chart. At the top, the fleet's 60 trucks grouped by route type; the number under each group says how many of the sample's 60 stops fall there, and the orange trucks are the ones that contribute some. At the bottom, the estimates of the mean in 2,000 samples made that way; the gray outline is that of “purely at random”, for comparison. “Another sample” changes the sample at the top.

With the stratified sample, the mean is not the plain mean of the 60: each group weighs according to its size in the population (half the stops are highway, a third city, a sixth port). If you give more stops to the port because it varies more, you have to reweight them; otherwise the mean would come out inflated.

All this is the basis for the next step: with the sample mean and its standard error you build a confidence interval: two values between which the true mean reasonably lies. It's explained in topic 12.

Putting it all together

The full recipe

  1. Define the population: all the stops of the year, those of this customer, those of next month? The answer is only valid for that population.
  2. Choose the sample at random, for real: no “the ones I had at hand” or “the trucks that report properly”.
  3. Compute the mean and its standard error: the sample's standard deviation divided by the square root of n.
  4. Remember the square-root law: half the error costs 4 times more data. Decide how much error you can afford before paying.
  5. Trust the bell with care: with symmetric data it arrives early; with long tails, it takes a while (or never arrives).
  6. Watch out for dependence: consecutive data from the same truck, the same day or the same driver are worth less than independent data.
  7. Stratify if there are distinct groups and weight each group by its real size.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Audits that cost money

Checking 16 stops costs $64 and leaves a standard error of 7.6 minutes, which in the annual cost of the stops (about $240,000) is ±$63,600. Bringing it down to ±$16,000 takes 16 times more stops: 256, about $1,000.

Dashboards with daily means

The mean fuel use of a day with 12 trips wobbles much more than that of a month with 400. Before panicking over a bad day, look at its standard error: with few trips, jumps of 10% are pure chance.

Pilots with few trucks

Testing a new device on 3 trucks and measuring 600 trips is not 600 independent data points: the trips of one truck look alike. The effective size is closer to 3 than to 600.

Data that gets lost

Covered docks cut the GPS signal and long stops get recorded worse. A mean over millions of records can be biased: more data doesn't correct a sample that leaves out a part.

Connections

With the models of the ML series and the other topics

The standard error uses the standard deviation and the n − 1 of topic 7; the starting shapes (the long tail of the stops, the Poisson of the alerts) are those of topic 8; the condition that the data be independent is that of topic 9, and when they aren't, the covariance of topic 10 says how much the error grows. The next step, confidence intervals, is topic 12.

In the ML series, the bagging of ensembles averages many models trained on different samples so that they “wobble” less, just like a mean; the Kalman filter averages noisy GPS measurements and its uncertainty drops with each one; and in Gaussian processes, several measurements at the same point reduce the noise like 1/√n. In “Preparing the data”, splitting before looking is a sample set aside (and it must be split by truck, not by trip, so there are no dependent data on both sides), and with imbalanced classes you stratify by class so the rare class isn't left out.

To be fair

What helps and where it misleads

What helps

  • With a small, well-chosen sample you learn a lot about a huge population, and also how much to trust it.
  • The standard error tells you in advance how much data you need for a given error, and how much it will cost.
  • The bell of the central limit theorem works for almost any mean, wherever it comes from.

Where it misleads

  • With very long tails, the bell takes a long time to arrive or never does.
  • Dependent data (same truck, same day) make the real error much larger than the calculated one.
  • A biased sample isn't fixed with more data: the standard error doesn't see it.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Population
Everything you want to know something about: the 12,000 stops of the year.
Sample
The part you actually look at. Its size is called n.
Parameter
A number of the population (its mean, 28.6 minutes). It's almost never known.
Statistic
A number computed from the sample (its mean). It changes from one sample to another.
Sampling distribution
The shape of the means of many samples: what piles up at the bottom of the toy.
Standard error
How much the mean of a sample wobbles: the standard deviation divided by the square root of n.
Law of large numbers
With lots of data, the sample mean gets close to the population mean.
Central limit theorem
With lots of independent data, the mean is bell-shaped, whatever the shape of the data.
Stratify
Split the sample among known groups (route types) and choose at random within each.
Cluster
A group that's chosen whole or nearly whole (a truck with its stops). Convenient, but its data look alike.
Selection bias
When the way the sample is chosen leaves out part of the population. More data doesn't fix it.
