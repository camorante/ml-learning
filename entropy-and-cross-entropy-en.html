Entropy and cross-entropy

An alert that almost never fires says a lot when it does; an "all good" that arrives every night says almost nothing. Entropy turns that intuition into a number: how many bits it takes, on average, to tell what happens. Its cousin, cross-entropy, is the loss that almost every classifier is trained with.

The setup

Every night, the telematics box of each truck sends a report with one of four answers: no incidents, delay, breakdown or accident. In the fleet, out of every 1,000 reports, about 900 say "no incidents", 60 "delay", 35 "breakdown" and 5 "accident".

How much information does a report carry? It depends on how surprising it is. A "no incidents" is what you expected; an "accident" is not. The surprise of an answer is measured in bits, which you can read as "how many yes-or-no questions it would take to guess it". Entropy is the average surprise over all reports: how much uncertainty there really is in what is about to arrive.

Three numbers for the same report

  • Entropy: the average surprise of a report, in bits.
  • Fitted code: the bits per report spent by the best practical code, which gives short codes to frequent answers (idea 3).
  • Fixed code: 2 bits per report, because 2 bits (00, 01, 10, 11) can write 4 answers.

Try this, in order:

  1. At 90% (the real fleet), the entropy is only 0.59 bits per report: the same thing happens almost every time and almost nothing is surprising.
  2. Go down to 25%: the four answers are equally likely and the entropy reaches its maximum, 2 bits. That is as unpredictable as four answers can be.
  3. Go up to 99%: the entropy drops to 0.09 bits. But look at the accident: it is now so rare that its surprise is almost 11 bits.

What you just saw

Rare things surprise more; entropy is the average surprise

Information theory (Claude Shannon, 1948) measures the information in an answer by how little you expected it:

  • Surprise. An answer that happens 1 time in 2 is worth 1 bit; 1 in 4, 2 bits; 1 in 8, 3 bits. Every time something is half as likely, it is worth one more bit. An accident, 1 in 200, is worth 7.6 bits; a "no incidents", 0.15.
  • Entropy. It is the average surprise, weighting each answer by how often it arrives. An accident is very surprising but rarely arrives; a "no incidents" arrives a lot but surprises nobody. In the real fleet, the average comes out to 0.59 bits.
  • From 0 to the maximum. If the same thing always arrived, the entropy would be 0: there would be no need to send the report. It is highest when everything is equally likely: with 4 answers, 2 bits.

The ideas below explain why we use bits and yes-or-no questions (1), the entropy of a single question (2), why entropy is the limit of how much you can compress (3), what happens when you use the wrong probabilities: cross-entropy and the KL divergence (4 and 5), and how all of this becomes the loss function of classifiers (6 to 8) and the criterion of decision trees (9).

Idea 1 of 9

Surprise is measured in yes-or-no questions

One night, one of 16 trucks sends a sensor alert, and it could have been any of the 16. How many yes-or-no questions does it take to find out which one? With a good strategy, each question rules out half: "is it among the first 8?", "among the first 4 of those?"… 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1: 4 questions, because 16 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2.

That is why an event that happens "1 time in 16" has a surprise of 4 bits. In general, the surprise of something with probability p is log₂(1/p): the number of times you have to split in half to reach that probability. It also works when it doesn't come out exact: a breakdown, at 3.5%, is worth 4.84 bits.

Idea 2 of 9

A single question: the fair coin and the loaded coin

The simplest question has two answers: "will this delivery be late?". If it is late half the time, it is like tossing a fair coin: the answer is worth exactly 1 bit, and there is no way to guess it better than at random.

If only 10% are late, it is a loaded coin. "Late" is very surprising (3.3 bits), but it almost never arrives; "on time" arrives almost always and surprises little (0.15 bits). The average is 0.47 bits: less than half the uncertainty of the fair coin. The more loaded the coin, the lower the entropy, down to 0 if the answer is always the same.

How to read the chart. Horizontally, the percentage of deliveries that are late. The blue curve is the entropy of the question: the average surprise. The orange dot is your percentage. The two bars on the right are the surprise of each answer: "late" in orange and "on time" in purple, with what each one contributes to the average.

Idea 3 of 9

Compressing: short codes for frequent things

Besides the nightly report, the telematics box sends its status every 30 seconds: driving, stuck in traffic, unloading, refueling, at the shop or alert. With six statuses, a fixed code needs 3 bits per message (000, 001, …). But "driving" arrives more than half the time: if you give it a 1-bit code and leave the long ones for the rare statuses, the average goes down.

The best code of this kind is built with Huffman's method (1952): merge the two least frequent statuses as if they were one, repeat until only one is left, and the path of merges gives the code of each status. Shannon proved that no code can spend fewer bits on average than the entropy: it is the limit of how much you can compress. Huffman always stays less than 1 bit away from that limit, and almost always much closer.

How to read the chart. One row per status. The blue bar is how frequent it is. The orange squares are its Huffman code, bit by bit (frequent ones, short); the gray ones, the 3-bit fixed code. On the right, the surprise of each status: the Huffman code has roughly the same length, rounded.

Idea 4 of 9

Cross-entropy: paying with the wrong code

In winter the reports change: there are more delays, more breakdowns and more accidents (78% no incidents, 12% delays, 8% breakdowns, 2% accidents). The winter entropy is 1.05 bits per report. But if you keep using the codes, or the model, built with the whole year's frequencies, each answer is paid with its surprise according to the model, not according to reality: accidents, which the model believes are extremely rare, are expensive, and now they arrive four times as often.

The average of what you pay is the cross-entropy between the data (p) and the model (q): 1.15 bits per report. If the model gets the frequencies right, it equals the entropy; if not, it is always higher. That extra cost is what you lose by believing something that isn't true.

How to read the chart. For each answer, the orange bar is how frequent it is in winter (the data, p) and the blue one, the probability the model gives it (q). On the right, what it costs every time it arrives: its surprise according to the model. At the bottom, two bars: the winter entropy (the minimum possible) and the cross-entropy (what you pay with this model). The red piece is the extra cost.

Idea 5 of 9

The KL divergence: a distance that depends on who is looking

The extra cost of idea 4 has a name: the Kullback-Leibler divergence (KL). It is cross-entropy minus entropy, it is never negative and it is 0 only when the model matches the data. It is used as a "distance" between two distributions, but it is not symmetric: measuring winter with the year's frequencies (0.094 bits) is not the same as measuring the year with winter's (0.074 bits).

Where it shows most is with rare things. If the model says an accident is almost impossible and then accidents happen, each one costs a huge number of bits: in that direction the KL shoots up. The other way around, a model that fears an accident that almost never comes pays little. It is the first hint of why cross-entropy punishes being confident and wrong so hard (idea 6).

Idea 6 of 9

The loss of classifiers: confident and wrong is very expensive

A model gives each truck a probability of breaking down next week. The week goes by and you look at what happened. How much should the model be punished? Cross-entropy answers: whatever the surprise of what happened costs, according to its own probabilities. If it gave 90% to what happened, little; if it gave it 1%, a lot. That average over all trucks is the logarithmic loss (log loss) of logistic regression, and the one minimized by almost every neural network that classifies.

Libraries compute it with the natural logarithm instead of base 2: the unit is called a nat (1 bit = 0.69 nats), but the idea is the same. And minimizing it is the same as the maximum likelihood of topic 14: finding the model that gave the most probability to what really happened.

How to read the chart. Horizontally, the probability the model had given to what happened (100% = it got it right with total confidence). The red curve is the penalty of the log loss; the purple one, that of the squared error (the distance to 100%, squared), which never goes above 1. The dot is your choice.

Idea 7 of 9

It rewards telling the truth: honest probabilities

A truck truly has a 10% probability of breaking down this week. The model can say whatever it wants: 10%, 1% ("nothing will happen") or 50%. Since nobody knows what will happen, what counts is the average penalty: 10% of the time it is charged the surprise of "breakdown" and 90% of the time, that of "no breakdown".

That average penalty is lowest exactly when the model states the true probability, and then it equals the entropy. Any other answer also pays the KL divergence from idea 5. That is why a model trained with cross-entropy tends to give calibrated percentages: if it says 10%, about 10 out of 100 break down. Neither exaggerating nor downplaying pays off. (If you rebalance the data, this breaks: the topic on imbalanced classes explains it.)

Idea 8 of 9

Several classes: from scores to percentages with softmax

When a truck breaks down, another model tries to guess what kind of breakdown: engine, brakes, tires or electrical. Neural networks don't output percentages directly: they output a score per class, which can be any number. The softmax function turns them into percentages that add up to 100%: it raises the number e to each score and splits proportionally. Adding 1 to a score multiplies its weight by 2.7.

The loss is the same as in idea 6: the surprise of the true class, −log(probability it was given). And during training, each score is pushed in a very simple way: the true class's score goes up in proportion to what it was missing to reach 100%, and the others go down in proportion to the probability they took. Why it comes out like that is explained in the topic on derivatives and the chain rule.

Idea 9 of 9

Trees: the question that clarifies the most

Out of 1,000 trucks, 251 broke down last year. Before knowing anything else, "did it break down?" has an entropy of 0.81 bits. A decision tree looks for the question that, when it splits the trucks into two groups, leaves each group as "tidy" as possible: with the lowest entropy. How much the entropy drops on average is called the information gain, and the tree keeps the question that gains the most.

That gain, measured over a whole feature, is the mutual information: how much one thing becomes clearer when you know the other. It is 0 only if the two are independent, and unlike the correlation of topic 10, it detects relationships that aren't a straight line.

Putting it all together

The complete recipe

  1. Surprise of an answer: log₂(1/p) bits. Rare things are worth a lot; sure things, nothing.
  2. Entropy: the average surprise. 0 if the same thing always happens; highest if everything is equally likely.
  3. Compression: no code goes below the entropy; Huffman stays less than one bit away.
  4. Cross-entropy: what you pay describing the data with a model's probabilities. Always greater than or equal to the entropy.
  5. KL divergence: the difference between the two. It is not symmetric and it shoots up if the model gives almost 0 to something that happens.
  6. Classification loss: the average cross-entropy between what happened and what the model said. Minimizing it is maximum likelihood, and it rewards honest probabilities.
  7. Several classes: softmax turns scores into percentages; training pushes each score by "percentage given − what it should have been".
  8. Trees and mutual information: pick the question that lowers the entropy the most, and measure relationships of any shape.

In the real world

Where it shows up in fleet telemetry

Sending less data

2,000 trucks sending their status every 30 seconds make 172.8 million messages a month: 64.8 MB with a fixed code and 40.8 MB with Huffman. Over satellite, at about $5 per MB, that is about $120 a month of difference on the statuses alone.

Surprising days

Adding up the surprise of everything a truck sends in a day gives a rarity score: a day with an accident and three harsh-braking alerts adds up to dozens of bits. It is a simple way to rank what to look at first.

Breakdown models

They are trained and compared with the log loss. A model that gives 1% to trucks that then break down is exposed, even if it is right almost every time; and since its percentages are honest, they can be turned into dollars of shop costs.

Alerting when the fleet changes

The KL divergence between this month's reports and the year's tells how much what arrives has changed. If it goes up (winter, a new route), it is time to review the models before they fail.

Connections

With the models of the ML series and the other topics

Logistic regression is trained by minimizing cross-entropy, and that is why its percentages are probabilities. Decision trees choose their questions by information gain (or with Gini impurity, a close cousin), and random forests inherit that criterion. XGBoost classifies by minimizing the same log loss, step by step. In Bayes, the surprise of a data point is minus the logarithm of its likelihood, and mutual information is used to compare two k-means clusterings.

In this collection, cross-entropy is the log-likelihood of topic 14 with its sign flipped; mutual information generalizes the independence of topic 9 and sees what the correlation of topic 10 misses; the normal distribution of topic 8 is the one with the most entropy for a given spread, and the softmax push comes from the derivatives of topic 16. With rare classes, the log loss and rebalancing meet in the topic on imbalanced classes.

To be fair

What helps and where it misleads

What helps

  • A single idea for three things: how much uncertainty there is, how much you can compress and how wrong a model is.
  • As a loss, it rewards honest percentages and punishes false confidence.
  • Mutual information finds relationships of any shape, not just straight lines.

Where it misleads

  • Giving 0% to something that happens costs infinity: libraries clip the probabilities, and a single wrong label can dominate the average.
  • KL is not a true distance: it depends on the direction.
  • With little data, entropy is underestimated and mutual information is overestimated; with continuous features, it depends on how they are binned.

Mini glossary

The jargon, in plain English

Bit
The information in a yes-or-no question whose two answers are equally likely.
Surprise (information)
log₂(1/p): what an answer that had probability p is worth. The rarer, the more bits.
Entropy
The average surprise: how much uncertainty there is before you know the answer.
Huffman code
A code that gives short words to frequent things and long ones to rare things; the best possible, symbol by symbol.
Cross-entropy
The average surprise when the data come from one distribution and are measured with the probabilities of another (a model).
KL divergence
Cross-entropy minus entropy: what you pay on top for using the wrong model.
Logarithmic loss (log loss)
The average cross-entropy between what happened and what a classifier said.
Nat
The same unit as the bit, but with the natural logarithm: 1 bit = 0.69 nats.
Softmax
The computation that turns scores into percentages that add up to 100%.
Calibration
That a 10% means it happens 10 times out of 100.
Information gain
How much the entropy drops when you split the data with a question.
Mutual information
How much the entropy of one thing drops when you know another. 0 if they are independent.
For the curious: the math behind it

You don't need them to understand the idea:

surprise of an answer with probability p: log₂(1/p) bits entropy: H = Σ p · log₂(1/p) fleet: 0.9 · 0.152 + 0.06 · 4.059 + 0.035 · 4.837 + 0.005 · 7.644 = 0.588 bits per report cross-entropy: H(p, q) = Σ p · log₂(1/q) winter with the year's code: 1.145 bits KL divergence: H(p, q) − H(p) = 1.145 − 1.051 = 0.094 bits log loss (nats): average of −ln(probability given to what happened) giving 1% to what happens: −ln 0.01 = 4.6 (squared error: (1 − 0.01)² = 0.98) softmax: percentage of class k = e^(score k) / Σ e^(score) training push on score k: percentage given − (1 if it's the true one, 0 if not) information gain: H(before) − Σ (share of the trucks) · H(group) engine outside 80–100 °C: 0.813 − 0.202 · 0.738 − 0.798 · 0.512 = 0.255 bits
